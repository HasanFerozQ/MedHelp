Section:  General Surgery

Topic: Surgical Principles


Front: Most reliable clinical indicator of general nutritional status in a preoperative patient?
Back: A recent history of unintentional weight loss greater than 10% of baseline body weight.

Front: Best objective serum lab marker for long-term nutritional status due to its half-life of 20 days?
Back: Serum albumin level.

Front: Best objective serum lab marker for acute nutritional status changes due to its short 2-day half-life?
Back: Prealbumin level.

Front: Minimum duration of smoking cessation required to significantly reduce postoperative pulmonary complications?
Back: At least 4 weeks.

Front: Absolute minimum timeline for cessation of smoking to improve ciliary clearing function before surgery?
Back: 8 weeks.

Front: Single most important predictor of postoperative cardiac risk based on the Goldman Index?
Back: Presence of an uncompensated third heart sound or jugular venous distention.

Front: Length of time surgery must be delayed following an acute myocardial infarction to minimize re-infarction risk?
Back: 6 months.

Front: Ejection fraction threshold below which non-cardiac elective surgery carries a prohibitive risk of mortality?
Back: Less than 35%.

Front: Standard classification system used by anesthesiologists to stratify a patient’s baseline physical status?
Back: American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status Classification System.

Front: Clinical presentation of an ASA Class 3 patient?
Back: A patient with severe systemic disease that limits activity but is not constantly life-threatening.

Front: Clinical presentation of an ASA Class 4 patient?
Back: A patient with severe systemic disease that is a constant threat to life.

Front: Primary perioperative management tool for a patient with Child-Pugh Class C hepatic cirrhosis?
Back: Postpone all elective surgical procedures due to an expected mortality rate exceeding 80%.

Front: First-line screening test for patients with suspected underlying bleeding disorders prior to major surgery?
Back: Thorough clinical history of bleeding tendencies and family history.

Front: Management of a patient taking chronic therapeutic warfarin who needs to undergo a major elective operation?
Back: Stop warfarin 5 days prior and bridge with low-molecular-weight heparin once INR drops below 2.0.

Front: Target international normalized ratio before a patient can safely undergo general elective major surgery?
Back: An INR less than or equal to 1.5.

Front: Preoperative management strategy for a patient on chronic therapeutic aspirin undergoing high-bleeding-risk surgery?
Back: Hold aspirin for 7 to 10 days prior to the operation.

Front: Preoperative management strategy for a patient taking clopidogrel prior to elective major surgery?
Back: Discontinue the medication 5 to 7 days before the planned procedure.

Front: Perioperative target range for blood glucose levels in a diabetic patient undergoing major surgery?
Back: Between 140 and 180 milligrams per deciliter.

Front: Preoperative adjustment required for a patient taking long-acting insulin the night before major surgery?
Back: Administer 60% to 80% of the normal evening dose.

Front: Preoperative adjustment required for a patient taking oral hypoglycemic medications the morning of surgery?
Back: Hold the medication the morning of the procedure.

Front: Primary rationale for giving stress-dose corticosteroids perioperatively to patients on chronic steroid therapy?
Back: Prevention of acute adrenal crisis due to secondary adrenal insufficiency.

Front: Standard perioperative stress-dose hydrocortisone regimen for a patient undergoing a high-stress major operation?
Back: Administer 100 milligrams intravenously before induction, followed by 50 milligrams every 8 hours for 24 hours.

Module 2: Fluids, Electrolytes, & Acid-Base Balance
Front: Standard formula used to calculate hourly maintenance fluid requirements in an adult patient?
Back: The 4-2-1 rule.

Front: Hourly maintenance fluid rate for a 70-kilogram adult calculated by the standard formula?
Back: 110 milliliters per hour.

Front: Most accurate clinical method for monitoring real-time volume status in a critically ill surgical patient?
Back: Measurement of hourly urine output via an indwelling Foley catheter.

Front: Target minimum hourly urine output for an adult patient receiving standard fluid maintenance or resuscitation?
Back: 0.5 milliliters per kilogram per hour.

Front: Target minimum hourly urine output for a pediatric patient or an adult experiencing severe rhadbomyolysis?
Back: 1.0 milliliter per kilogram per hour.

Front: Preferred initial intravenous crystalloid solution for volume resuscitation in a trauma patient with hemorrhagic shock?
Back: Lactated Ringer's solution.

Front: Primary risk associated with rapid, large-volume resuscitation using 0.9% normal saline?
Back: Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis.

Front: Earliest clinical sign of progressive hypernatremia in a postoperative ward patient?
Back: Altered mental status ranging from lethargy to confusion.

Front: Maximum safe rate of serum sodium correction in a patient with severe chronic hyponatremia?
Back: No faster than 10 to 12 milliequivalents per liter over 24 hours.

Front: Central nervous system catastrophe caused by the overly rapid correction of chronic severe hyponatremia?
Back: Central pontine myelinolysis.

Front: Life-threatening complication caused by the overly rapid correction of chronic severe hypernatremia?
Back: Cerebral edema and brain herniation.

Front: Earliest electrocardiogram manifestation seen in a patient with progressive hyperkalemia?
Back: Peaked T waves.

Front: Progression of electrocardiogram changes in worsening hyperkalemia after peaked T waves appear?
Back: PR interval prolongation, QRS widening, loss of P waves, and a sine wave pattern.

Front: First-line medication administered to a hyperkalemic patient demonstrating acute electrocardiogram changes?
Back: Intravenous calcium gluconate to stabilize the cardiac myocyte membrane.

Front: Rapid-acting medical therapies used to transiently shift extracellular potassium into the cells?
Back: Intravenous insulin administered concurrently with dextrose, and nebulized albuterol.

Front: Most common electrolyte abnormality responsible for a persistent postoperative adynamic ileus?
Back: Hypokalemia.

Front: Co-existing electrolyte deficiency that makes refractory hypokalemia impossible to correct with potassium supplementation alone?
Back: Hypomagnesemia.

Front: Primary physiological reason hypomagnesemia prevents successful correction of a potassium deficit?
Back: Low magnesium causes uninhibited potassium wasting through the renal outer medullary potassium channels.

Front: Classic electrocardiogram finding diagnostic of severe hypokalemia?
Back: Flattened T waves, ST-segment depression, and prominent U waves.

Front: Acid-base disturbance classically caused by prolonged nasogastric tube suctioning or severe persistent vomiting?
Back: Hypokalemic, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis with paradoxical aciduria.

Front: Primary renal mechanism driving paradoxical aciduria in severe hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis?
Back: Volume depletion forces aldosterone to retain sodium at the direct expense of excreting hydrogen ions into urine.

Module 3: Surgical Nutrition & Metabolism
Front: Timeline after which a completely fasting postoperative patient must receive nutritional support?
Back: 7 to 10 days of inadequate oral intake.

Front: Preferred route of nutritional support in a surgical patient with a functional gastrointestinal tract?
Back: Enteral nutrition.

Front: Primary physiological advantage of choosing enteral nutrition over total parenteral nutrition?
Back: Preserves mucosal barrier integrity and prevents bacterial translocation from the gut lumen.

Front: Principal route of administration for nutritional formulas with an osmolarity exceeding 900 milliosmoles per liter?
Back: Central venous access.

Front: Most common metabolic complication associated with the initiation of total parenteral nutrition?
Back: Hyperglycemia.

Front: Primary trace mineral deficiency associated with long-term parenteral nutrition lacking adequate supplementation?
Back: Zinc deficiency.

Front: Classic clinical presentation of a patient suffering from zinc deficiency during chronic parenteral nutrition?
Back: Perioral acrodermatitis, poor wound healing, alopecia, and altered taste.

Front: Pathophysiology behind the development of refeeding syndrome in a severely malnourished surgical patient?
Back: Carbohydrate loading induces an insulin surge that drives phosphate, potassium, and magnesium into the intracellular space.

Front: Most critical electrolyte abnormality to monitor and correct in refeeding syndrome to prevent cardiac failure?
Back: Hypophosphatemia.

Front: Preferred energy substrate for the specialized enterocytes of the small bowel mucosa?
Back: Glutamine.

Front: Preferred energy substrate for the specialized colonocytes of the large bowel mucosa?
Back: Short-chain fatty acids like butyrate.

Module 4: Perioperative Complications & Ward Emergencies
Front: Most common cause of a postoperative fever occurring within the first 24 to 48 hours?
Back: Atelectasis.

Front: Most common cause of a postoperative fever manifesting between postoperative days 3 and 5?
Back: Urinary tract infection.

Front: Most common cause of a postoperative fever manifesting between postoperative days 5 and 7?
Back: Deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism.

Front: Most common cause of a postoperative fever developing after postoperative day 7?
Back: Surgical site infection or deep intra-abdominal abscess.

Front: Immediate initial diagnostic step for a patient suspected of developing a postoperative pulmonary embolism?
Back: Computed tomography pulmonary angiography.

Front: First-line medical management for an unstable ward patient with a confirmed acute pulmonary embolism?
Back: Intravenous systemic anticoagulation with unfractionated heparin.

Front: Define the clinical condition known as postoperative deep wound dehiscence?
Back: Disruption of the fascial layer of the operative wound without evisceration.

Front: Define the clinical emergency known as postoperative wound evisceration?
Back: Disruption of all layers of the abdominal wall resulting in the protrusion of visceral organs.

Front: Immediate bedside management for a patient who suffers an acute abdominal wound evisceration on the ward?
Back: Cover the exposed viscera with sterile towels soaked in warm normal saline and arrange immediate return to the OR.

Front: Most common etiology of a postoperative wound infection occurring within 24 hours of skin closure?
Back: Infection caused by either Streptococcus pyogenes or Clostridium species.

Front: Distinctive physical exam finding associated with a clostridial wound infection?
Back: Crepitus within the surrounding subcutaneous tissues and thin, dishwater-like foul discharge.

Front: Diagnostic criteria used to differentiate a postoperative adynamic ileus from a mechanical small bowel obstruction?
Back: Radiographs showing diffuse gas throughout both the small and large bowel, including the rectum.

Front: Radiographic hallmark diagnostic of a mechanical small bowel obstruction?
Back: Dilated loops of small bowel with multiple air-fluid levels and a complete absence of gas in the colon.

Front: Initial management strategy for a patient diagnosed with a non-complicated postoperative adynamic ileus?
Back: Nothing-by-mouth status, intravenous fluid maintenance, electrolyte correction, and avoidance of opioid medications.

Front: Diagnostic step required for a postoperative patient who presents with sudden, unexplained oliguria?
Back: Immediate placement or flushing of a Foley catheter to rule out mechanical obstruction.

Front: Primary physiological driver of postoperative urinary retention?
Back: Detrusor muscle dysfunction caused by the parasympatholytic effects of anesthesia and perioperative opioids.

Front: Diagnostic criteria for a patient suspected of having abdominal compartment syndrome?
Back: Sustained intra-abdominal pressure greater than 20 millimeters of mercury associated with new organ dysfunction.

Front: Standard bedside method used to indirectly measure intra-abdominal pressure?
Back: Measurement of the transurethral bladder pressure via a pressure transducer hooked to a Foley catheter.

Front: Definitive management for a patient with refractory abdominal compartment syndrome?
Back: Emergent surgical decompression via a midline laparotomy leaving the abdomen open.

Module 5: Shock & Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) Principles
Front: Primary physiological definition of shock?
Back: Systemic tissue hypoperfusion leading to inadequate cellular oxygen delivery and cellular hypoxia.

Front: Earliest measurable vital sign change indicative of Class II hemorrhagic shock?
Back: Tachycardia with a heart rate exceeding 100 beats per minute.

Front: Percentage of total intravascular blood volume loss required before clinical hypotension manifests in hemorrhagic shock?
Back: Loss of greater than 15% to 30% of total blood volume, corresponding to Class III shock.

Front: Formula used to calculate the shock index as a screening tool for occult hemorrhage?
Back: Heart rate divided by systolic blood pressure.

Front: Clinical significance of a shock index value greater than 0.9?
Back: Strongly indicates significant occult systemic hypovolemia requiring urgent blood product resuscitation.

Front: Definition of the lethal triad of trauma?
Back: Hypothermia, metabolic acidosis, and coagulopathy.

Front: Ratio of blood products administered in a standard damage control resuscitation protocol for massive hemorrhage?
Back: A balanced 1:1:1 ratio of packed red blood cells, fresh frozen plasma, and platelets.

Front: Target physiological endpoints when utilizing a strategy of permissive hypotension in a trauma patient?
Back: A systolic blood pressure of 80 to 90 millimeters of mercury until surgical hemostasis is achieved.

Front: Absolute contraindication to the use of a permissive hypotension resuscitation strategy in a trauma patient?
Back: Presence of a concomitant severe traumatic brain injury.

Front: First step in the primary survey of a trauma patient according to Advanced Trauma Life Support protocols?
Back: Airway maintenance with strict restriction of cervical spine motion.

Front: Absolute clinical indication for securing a definitive surgical airway via cricothyroidotomy?
Back: Inability to intubate the trachea due to severe facial fractures, upper airway hemorrhage, or edema.

Front: Clinical triad diagnostic of a tension pneumothorax?
Back: Respiratory distress, unilateral absent breath sounds, and hemodynamic instability with tracheal deviation.

Front: Immediate management for a patient diagnosed with a tension pneumothorax before obtaining any imaging?
Back: Needle decompression via the fifth intercostal space just anterior to the midaxillary line.

Front: Diagnostic clinical signs that constitute Beck's triad for cardiac tamponade?
Back: Hypotension, muffled heart sounds, and jugular venous distention.

Front: Distinctive arterial pulse change seen in a patient with cardiac tamponade?
Back: Pulsus paradoxus, defined as a drop in systolic blood pressure greater than 10 millimeters of mercury during inspiration.

Front: Define the specific structural injury known as a flail chest?
Back: Two or more ribs fractured in two or more places, causing a segment of the chest wall to move paradoxically.

Front: Primary physiological cause of hypoxia in a patient with a flail chest injury?
Back: Underlying pulmonary contusion rather than the mechanical restriction of the paradoxical chest wall movement.

Front: Diagnostic parameter that defines a massive hemothorax upon chest tube insertion?
Back: Immediate evacuation of 1500 milliliters or more of blood, or ongoing output of 200 milliliters per hour for 2 to 4 consecutive hours.

Front: Next immediate therapeutic step for a patient who meets the criteria for a massive hemothorax?
Back: Urgent operative thoracotomy to achieve surgical hemostasis.

Front: Anatomical landmarks defining the borders of the "cardiac box" in trauma evaluations?
Back: Area bounded by the clavicles superiorly, costal margins inferiorly, and the midclavicular lines bilaterally.

Front: Next diagnostic step for a hemodynamically stable blunt abdominal trauma patient with an equivocal physical exam?
Back: Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma or a computed tomography scan of the abdomen.

Front: Name the four anatomical views imaged during a standard Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma exam?
Back: Perihepatic view, perisplenic view, pelvic view, and pericardial view.

Front: Next immediate step for a hemodynamically unstable blunt abdominal trauma patient with a positive FAST examination?
Back: Emergent exploratory laparotomy.

Front: Next immediate step for a hemodynamically unstable pelvic fracture patient with ongoing retroperitoneal hemorrhage?
Back: Mechanical stabilization of the pelvic ring via an external pelvic binder placed at the level of the greater trochanters.

Module 6: Surgical Infections, Antibiotic Prophylaxis, & Wound Healing
Front: Timeline requirement for the administration of a systemic prophylactic preoperative antibiotic?
Back: Within 60 minutes prior to the making of the surgical skin incision.

Front: Preferred first-line antibiotic choice for standard clean general surgical procedures?
Back: Cefazolin.

Front: Recommended alternative prophylactic antibiotic for clean operations in a patient with a severe anaphylactic penicillin allergy?
Back: Clindamycin or vancomycin.

Front: Standard timing requirement for the redosing of a prophylactic antibiotic during a prolonged operation?
Back: Redose after two half-lives of the specific drug have elapsed, or after a blood loss exceeding 1500 milliliters.

Front: Definition of a clean surgical wound category?
Back: An uninfected operative wound in which no inflammation is encountered and the respiratory, alimentary, or genitourinary tracts are not entered.

Front: Definition of a clean-contaminated surgical wound category?
Back: An operative wound in which the respiratory, alimentary, or genitourinary tracts are entered under controlled conditions without unusual contamination.

Front: Definition of a contaminated surgical wound category?
Back: Open, fresh, accidental wounds or operations with major breaks in sterile technique or gross gastrointestinal spillage.

Front: Definition of a dirty surgical wound category?
Back: Old traumatic wounds with retained devitalized tissue or wounds that involve existing clinical infection or perforated viscera.

Front: Standard wound closure strategy recommended for an operation classified as a dirty surgical wound?
Back: Leave the skin and subcutaneous tissues open for healing by secondary intention or delayed primary closure.

Front: Primary mechanism of wound healing classified as healing by primary intention?
Back: Edges of the clean wound are mechanically approximated using sutures, staples, or adhesive tape immediately.

Front: Primary mechanism of wound healing classified as healing by secondary intention?
Back: Wound edges are left separate, allowing the wound cavity to fill via granulation tissue formation, contraction, and epithelialization.

Front: Principal cell type responsible for coordinating the initial inflammatory phase of normal wound healing?
Back: Platelets initially, followed closely by tissue macrophages.

Front: Primary cell type responsible for extracellular matrix deposition during the proliferative phase of wound healing?
Back: Fibroblasts.

Front: Type of collagen initially deposited in high amounts during the proliferative phase of wound healing?
Back: Type III collagen.

Front: Type of collagen that replaces temporary collagen during the remodeling phase of normal wound healing?
Back: Type I collagen.

Front: Timeline peak for the maximum deposition of collagen within a healing surgical wound?
Back: Between 2 and 3 weeks post-injury.

Front: Ultimate maximum tensile strength achieved by a fully remodeled scar compared to normal uninjured skin?
Back: Approximately 80% of original uninjured skin tensile strength.

Front: Most common localized complication that halts normal wound healing progress?
Back: Tissue ischemia or high local bacterial bioburden exceeding 100,000 organisms per gram of tissue.

Module 7: Anesthesia & Hemostasis Principles
Front: Pathophysiology driving the development of malignant hyperthermia during general anesthesia?
Back: Uncontrolled release of calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum via mutated ryanodine receptors.

Front: Earliest and most sensitive clinical indicator of an impending malignant hyperthermia crisis?
Back: A sudden, unexplained rise in end-tidal carbon dioxide levels refractory to increased ventilation.

Front: Definitive pharmacological antidote for a patient experiencing malignant hyperthermia?
Back: Intravenous dantrolene.

Front: Mechanism of action of the muscle relaxant succinylcholine?
Back: Depolarizing neuromuscular blocker that binds to nicotinic acetylcholine receptors causing persistent depolarization.

Front: Major metabolic electrolyte risk associated with the administration of succinylcholine to a severe burn or crush injury patient?
Back: Life-threatening hyperkalemia due to widespread upregulation of extrajunctional acetylcholine receptors.

Front: Mechanism of action of local anesthetics like lidocaine?
Back: Reversibly block voltage-gated sodium channels along the neuronal axon membrane to prevent depolarization.

Front: Rationale for combining epinephrine with a local anesthetic solution for tissue infiltration?
Back: Induces localized vasoconstriction to prolong the anesthetic duration, decrease systemic absorption, and improve local hemostasis.

Front: Absolute anatomical contraindications to using a local anesthetic solution mixed with epinephrine?
Back: Infiltration into terminal vascular beds including the fingers, toes, nose, ears, and penis.

Front: Maximum safe dose of plain lidocaine without epinephrine for local infiltration in an adult?
Back: 4.5 milligrams per kilogram.

Front: Maximum safe dose of lidocaine mixed with epinephrine for local infiltration in an adult?
Back: 7 milligrams per kilogram.

Front: Earliest central nervous system signs indicative of systemic local anesthetic toxicity?
Back: Perioral numbness, metallic taste, tinnitus, and tongue paresthesias.

Front: Definitive rescue therapy for a patient suffering from severe cardiovascular collapse due to local anesthetic systemic toxicity?
Back: Intravenous infusion of a 20% lipid emulsion solution.

Front: Primary mechanism of action of topical hemostatic agents like oxidized regenerated cellulose?
Back: Provides a physical matrix that activates the intrinsic pathway of the coagulation cascade upon contact with blood.

Front: Mechanism of action of topical bovine thrombin used intraoperatively for hemostasis?
Back: Directly cleaves fibrinogen into fibrin to bypass all upstream clotting factor deficiencies.

Front: Most common cause of post-transfusion hemolytic reaction in the operating room?
Back: Clerical error leading to ABO incompatibility.

Front: Earliest clinical sign of an acute hemolytic transfusion reaction in an anesthetized patient?
Back: Unexplained hypotension and dark-colored urine indicating hemoglobinuria.

Front: Initial diagnostic test to confirm an acute hemolytic transfusion reaction?
Back: Direct Antiglobulin Test, also known as the Coombs test, performed on a post-transfusion blood sample.

Front: Immediate management step when a transfusion reaction is suspected in the operating room?
Back: Halt the transfusion immediately, disconnect the tubing, and run intravenous normal saline through a fresh line.

Front: Clinical presentation of Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury?
Back: Non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema causing severe hypoxia and bilateral infiltrates within 6 hours of blood product administration.

Front: Pathophysiology driving Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury?
Back: Donor antileukocyte antibodies activate host neutrophils inside the pulmonary vasculature, causing endothelial damage and capillary leak.

Front: Clinical feature distinguishing Transfusion-Associated Circulatory Overload from TRALI?
Back: Elevated central venous pressure, jugular venous distention, and a positive response to diuretic therapy.

Front: Define the specific diagnostic parameters for Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia?
Back: A greater than 50% drop in platelet count occurring 5 to 10 days after starting heparin therapy.

Front: Immediate medical management for a patient diagnosed with Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia?
Back: Stop all heparin products completely and immediately initiate a non-heparin anticoagulant such as argatroban.

Front: Gold standard confirmatory laboratory assay for Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia?
Back: Serotonin release assay.

Front: Most common early clinical manifestation of a systemic air embolism during central venous catheter placement?
Back: Sudden, severe respiratory distress accompanied by a distinctive "mill-wheel" cardiac murmur.

Front: Immediate bedside positioning for a patient with a suspected central venous air embolism?
Back: Left lateral decubitus in the Trendelenburg position, also known as the Durant maneuver.

Front: Primary physiological reason for placing an air embolism patient in the left lateral Trendelenburg position?
Back: Traps the air bolus in the apex of the right ventricle, preventing it from obstructing the pulmonary artery outflow tract.

Front: Formula used by the Advanced Trauma Life Support protocol to estimate initial fluid resuscitation volume in adult burn patients?
Back: The Parkland formula, which dictates 2 to 4 milliliters of Lactated Ringer's per kilogram per percent of total body surface area burned.

Front: Timeframe requirement for the delivery of the fluid volume calculated by the Parkland formula?
Back: Give the first half of the total volume over the first 8 hours from the time of injury, and the remaining half over the next 16 hours.

Front: Depth criteria that define a second-degree partial-thickness burn?
Back: Blistering, painful wounds that blanch with pressure, indicating intact dermal capillary networks.

Front: Depth criteria that define a third-degree full-thickness burn?
Back: Leathery, painless, inelastic wounds that do not blanch, indicating complete destruction of the dermis and nerve endings.

Front: Earliest absolute clinical indication for an emergent escharotomy in a circumferential burn patient?
Back: Loss of distal pulses or structural restriction of chest wall expansion causing ventilatory failure.

Front: Most common life-threatening systemic complication specifically associated with electrical burns?
Back: Acute kidney injury secondary to profound rhabdomyolysis and myoglobinuria.

Front: Target urine output when managing a patient with severe rhabdomyolysis or electrical burn injuries?
Back: 1.0 to 1.5 milliliters per kilogram per hour, achieved via aggressive fluid resuscitation and urine alkalinization.

Front: Most reliable clinical method to distinguish a primary pneumothorax from an open sucking chest wound?
Back: An open wound demonstrates a visible defect in the chest wall that dynamically communicates with the pleural space.

Front: Immediate temporary treatment for an open pneumothorax prior to chest tube placement?
Back: Application of a sterile occlusive dressing taped securely on three sides only.

Front: Primary physiological rationale for securing an open pneumothorax dressing on three sides only?
Back: Creates a flutter-valve that allows air to escape during expiration but prevents ambient atmospheric air from entering during inspiration.

Front: Most sensitive initial diagnostic imaging modality for a patient with a suspected retroperitoneal hematoma?
Back: Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis.

Front: Management paradigm for a Zone 1 retroperitoneal hematoma discovered during emergency laparotomy for blunt trauma?
Back: Mandatory surgical exploration of the midline zone regardless of stability to rule out major vascular or pancreaticoduodenal injury.

Front: Define the anatomical boundaries of Zone 1 in retroperitoneal trauma?
Back: The central midline zone spanning from the aortic hiatus down to the sacral promontory.

Front: Management paradigm for a Zone 2 retroperitoneal hematoma discovered during emergency trauma laparotomy?
Back: Exploration is indicated only if the hematoma is actively expanding, pulsatile, or associated with a known penetrating mechanism.

Front: Define the anatomical boundaries of Zone 2 in retroperitoneal trauma?
Back: The lateral flanks, encompassing the perinephric spaces and the retroperitoneal portions of the colon.

Front: Classic clinical presentation of a popliteal artery injury following a posterior knee dislocation?
Back: Distal ischemia presenting with absent pedal pulses, cold extremity, delayed capillary refill, and motor deficits.

Front: Next immediate diagnostic step for a knee dislocation patient with asymmetrical or diminished pedal pulses?
Back: Urgent measurement of the ankle-brachial index followed by an arterial duplex ultrasound or formal angiography.

Front: Threshold ankle-brachial index value that necessitates mandatory formal vascular imaging in a traumatized limb?
Back: An ABI value less than 0.90.

Front: Maximum warm ischemia time a mangled extremity can tolerate before irreversible muscle necrosis begins?
Back: Approximately 4 to 6 hours.

Front: Sequence of structural repair for a mangled extremity presenting with both an unstable bone fracture and an arterial transection?
Back: Establish temporary vascular shunting, perform rigid orthopedic bone fixation, and finish with definitive microvascular arterial repair.

Front: Pathophysiology of extremity compartment syndrome following successful vascular reconstruction for acute ischemia?
Back: Reperfusion injury causing profound intracellular edema and swelling within non-yielding fascial boundaries.

Front: Most reliable early clinical symptom of an impending lower extremity compartment syndrome?
Back: Severe, intractable pain that is completely out of proportion to physical exam findings and exacerbated by passive stretch of the muscle.

Front: Intracompartmental pressure threshold that mandates an urgent surgical fasciotomy?
Back: A pressure within 30 millimeters of mercury of the patient's diastolic blood pressure, or a absolute value greater than 30 mmHg.

Front: Anatomical structures routinely released during a standard two-incision, four-compartment lower leg fasciotomy?
Back: The anterior, lateral, superficial posterior, and deep posterior fascial compartments.

Front: Clinical presentation of gas gangrene caused by Clostridium perfringens?
Back: Rapidly progressive local pain, bronzed skin discoloration, crepitus, and systemic shock within hours of injury or surgery.

Front: Immediate therapeutic intervention required for a patient with confirmed necrotizing soft tissue infection or gas gangrene?
Back: Emergent wide surgical debridement of all devitalized tissues combined with high-dose intravenous penicillin and clindamycin.

Front: Standard timing guidelines for the first post-operative surveillance colonoscopy after an R0 resection for colon adenocarcinoma?
Back: Exactly 1 year following the primary surgical resection.

Front: Most common site of distant hematogenous metastasis for colorectal cancer?
Back: The liver, due to portal venous drainage.

Front: Standard surveillance lab marker used to monitor for disease recurrence in a postoperative colon cancer patient?
Back: Carcinoembryonic antigen.

Front: Clinical presentation of a pharyngeal pouch, also known as Zenker's diverticulum?
Back: Dysphagia, regurgitation of undigested food, halitosis, and recurrent aspiration pneumonia in an elderly patient.

Front: Anatomical space through which a Zenker's diverticulum herniates?
Back: Killian's triangle, located between the thyropharyngeus and cricopharyngeus muscles of the inferior constrictor.

Front: Definitive diagnostic study to confirm the presence of a Zenker's diverticulum?
Back: Barium swallow study.

Front: Absolute contraindication to performing a diagnostic upper endoscopy in a patient with a suspected Zenker's diverticulum?
Back: High risk of instrumental diverticular perforation due to blind esophageal advancement of the endoscope scope.

Front: Classic presentation of Boorhaave syndrome?
Back: Severe retrosternal chest pain following a bout of forceful vomiting, associated with subcutaneous emphysema.

Front: Most accurate initial diagnostic imaging study to evaluate a suspected Boerhaave syndrome?
Back: Water-soluble contrast esophagram, such as Gastrografin, to avoid mediastinal irritation from barium spillage.

Front: Anatomical location where a Boerhaave syndrome esophageal perforation most commonly occurs?
Back: The left posterolateral aspect of the distal esophagus, roughly 2 to 3 centimeters proximal to the gastroesophageal junction.

Front: Immediate medical stabilization steps for a patient diagnosed with an acute esophageal perforation?
Back: Intravenous fluid resuscitation, strict nothing-by-mouth status, broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics, and prompt surgical consultation.

Front: Anatomical landmarks that define the borders of Hesselbach's triangle?
Back: The rectus abdominis muscle medially, the inferior epigastric vessels laterally, and the inguinal ligament inferiorly.

Front: Anatomical relationship of a direct inguinal hernia relative to the inferior epigastric vessels?
Back: It herniates medial to the inferior epigastric vessels through a defect in the transversalis fascia.

Front: Anatomical relationship of an indirect inguinal hernia relative to the inferior epigastric vessels?
Back: It herniates lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels through the deep inguinal ring.

Front: Primary embryological cause responsible for the development of an indirect inguinal hernia?
Back: Failure of the processes vaginalis to obliterate during fetal development.

Front: Type of groin hernia that carries the highest risk of incarceration and strangulation?
Back: Femoral hernia.

Front: Anatomical boundaries of the femoral ring through which a femoral hernia passes?
Back: The inguinal ligament anteriorly, the pectineal ligament posteriorly, the lacunar ligament medially, and the femoral vein laterally.

Front: Define the structural components of a Littre's hernia?
Back: Any groin or abdominal wall hernia that contains a Meckel's diverticulum within the hernia sac.

Front: Define the structural components of a Richter's hernia?
Back: A hernia in which only a portion of the antimesenteric wall of the bowel becomes incarcerated without causing complete luminal obstruction.

Front: Definitive surgical management for an asymptomatic femoral hernia diagnosed in an adult?
Back: Prompt elective surgical repair due to an exceedingly high risk of early strangulation.

Front: Initial management strategy for a patient presenting with an acutely incarcerated inguinal hernia without signs of systemic toxicity?
Back: Attempt gentle manual reduction at the bedside under adequate analgesia and sedation.

Front: Absolute surgical contraindication to attempting manual reduction of an incarcerated hernia?
Back: Presence of systemic toxicity, leukocytosis, or skin changes over the hernia sac suggesting bowel strangulation.

Front: Anatomical structures that must be visualized to satisfy the "Critical View of Safety" during a laparoscopic cholecystectomy?
Back: The cystic plate, the cystic duct, and the cystic artery, with the lower part of the gallbladder dissected free from the liver bed.

Front: Diagnostic criteria that define Charcot's triad for acute cholangitis?
Back: Right upper quadrant abdominal pain, fever, and jaundice.

Front: Diagnostic criteria that define Reynolds' pentad for severe, obstructive acute cholangitis?
Back: Right upper quadrant abdominal pain, fever, jaundice, hypotension, and altered mental status.

Front: Immediate first-line intervention for a patient presenting with acute suppurative cholangitis and Reynolds' pentad?
Back: Intravenous fluid resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and emergent biliary decompression via Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography.

Front: Most common composition of gallstones found in patients within Western countries?
Back: Cholesterol gallstones.

Front: Primary etiology driving the formation of black pigment gallstones within the biliary tree?
Back: Chronic intravascular hemolysis leading to an excess of unconjugated bilirubin in the bile.

Front: Primary etiology driving the formation of brown pigment gallstones within the bile ducts?
Back: Chronic biliary tract infection or infestation leading to bacterial production of glucuronidases.

Front: Initial diagnostic test of choice for a patient suspected of having acute cholecystitis?
Back: Right upper quadrant transabdominal ultrasound.

Front: Ultrasonographic hallmarks diagnostic of acute cholecystitis?
Back: Gallbladder wall thickening greater than 3 millimeters, pericholecystic fluid, gallstones, and a positive sonographic Murphy's sign.

Back: A hypertrophic scar contains parallel collagen bundles confined strictly within the boundaries of the original wound, typically regressing over time.

Front: Pathophysiology of a keloid compared to a hypertrophic scar?
Back: A keloid contains disorganized, thick Type I and III collagen bundles that characteristically expand well beyond the boundaries of the original wound and rarely regress.

Front: First-line medical therapy used to reduce the size and symptoms of a symptomatic keloid?
Back: Intralesional corticosteroid injections, such as triamcinolone acetonide.

Front: Most common long-term malignancy arising within a chronic, non-healing ulcer or burn scar, known as a Marjolin's ulcer?
Back: Squamous cell carcinoma.

Front: Most predictive clinical validation score used to estimate a patient's perioperative 30-day mortality risk across all surgical specialties?
Back: The American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification system.

Front: Define the clinical status of a patient classified as ASA Physical Status III?
Back: A patient with severe systemic disease that limits functional activity but is not constantly life-threatening.

Front: Minimum functional capacity threshold, measured in metabolic equivalents, below which a patient requires further cardiac evaluation before major non-cardiac surgery?
Back: Four metabolic equivalents, which is the energy required to climb two flights of stairs or walk up a hill without limiting symptoms.

Front: Most crucial historical risk factor included in the Revised Cardiac Risk Index for predicting postoperative major adverse cardiac events?
Back: A history of ischemic heart disease, congestive heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, or preoperative insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus.

Front: Recommended management for a patient taking a maintenance aspirin regimen for a drug-eluting coronary stent placed 3 months ago who is undergoing moderate-risk non-cardiac surgery?
Back: Continue the aspirin throughout the perioperative period to prevent acute stent thrombosis, weighing the thrombotic risk against bleeding.

Front: Standard withholding window required for oral P2Y12 inhibitors like clopidogrel prior to elective major surgery?
Back: Discontinue the medication 5 days before the scheduled operative procedure.

Front: Recommended withholding window required for direct oral anticoagulants like rivaroxaban or apixaban before a high-bleeding-risk major surgery?
Back: Discontinue the medication 48 hours prior to the procedure in patients with normal renal function.

Front: Standard preoperative management strategy for a patient on long-term warfarin therapy with a mechanical mitral valve undergoing major abdominal surgery?
Back: Discontinue warfarin 5 days preoperatively and initiate bridging therapy with therapeutic low-molecular-weight heparin once the INR drops below 2.

Front: Timing guideline for holding therapeutic subcutaneous low-molecular-weight heparin prior to a surgical incision or neuraxial anesthesia?
Back: Hold the medication for at least 24 hours before the procedure to minimize the risk of spinal hematoma or major hemorrhage.

Front: Best initial diagnostic test to evaluate an asymptomatic patient with a loud systolic murmur detected during a routine preoperative physical examination?
Back: Transthoracic echocardiography to rule out severe aortic stenosis, which carries an extreme risk of perioperative decompensation.

Front: Standard target hemoglobin A1c level recommended prior to scheduling elective major surgical interventions to reduce surgical site infections?
Back: An HbA1c of less than 7% to 8% is generally targeted for optimal long-term wound healing and immune function.

Front: Preoperative management strategy for a patient with a known history of severe adrenal suppression on chronic daily prednisone therapy?
Back: Administration of perioperative "stress-dose" IV hydrocortisone on the morning of surgery, followed by a tapering schedule.

Front: Minimum duration of smoking cessation recommended preoperatively to significantly reduce the incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications?
Back: At least 4 to 8 weeks of complete cessation to normalize ciliary function and reduce airway hyperreactivity.

Front: Most common clinical indicator of severe underlying pulmonary disease that dictates obtaining a preoperative arterial blood gas?
Back: Severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or interstitial lung disease with chronic baseline hypoxia or hypercapnia.

Front: Recommended management for a patient's routine morning angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor dose on the day of major general surgery?
Back: Hold the medication on the morning of surgery to prevent severe, refractory intraoperative hypotension following the induction of anesthesia.

Module 18: Anesthetic Agents, Airway Management, & Intraoperative Mechanics
Front: Inhaled anesthetic agent characteristically associated with a risk of diffusion hypoxia upon discontinuation if supplemental oxygen is not provided?
Back: Nitrous oxide, due to its low blood-gas partition coefficient causing rapid expansion into the alveoli.

Front: Major systemic toxicity uniquely associated with prolonged or high-dose exposure to nitrous oxide?
Back: Megaloblastic anemia and peripheral neuropathy due to the irreversible oxidation and inactivation of vitamin B12.

Front: Inhaled volatile anesthetic agent most likely to trigger a severe, immune-mediated hepatic necrosis known as "halothane hepatitis"?
Back: Halothane, driven by the formation of trifluoroacetylated liver protein neoantigens.

Front: Intravenous induction agent of choice for a patient in severe hemorrhagic shock due to its hemodynamic neutrality?
Back: Etomidate, which preserves systemic vascular resistance and cardiac output.

Front: Major endocrine adverse effect associated with even a single diagnostic or induction dose of etomidate?
Back: Transient adrenal insufficiency caused by the direct inhibition of the enzyme 11-beta-hydroxylase.

Front: Intravenous induction agent that acts as a potent bronchodilator, making it the preferred choice for patients with acute status asthmaticus?
Back: Ketamine, which stimulates sympathetic catecholamine release.

Front: Distinctive psychiatric adverse effect characteristically observed during emergence from ketamine anesthesia?
Back: Emergence delirium, characterized by vivid hallucinations, confusion, and vivid dreams, which can be mitigated with perioperative benzodiazepines.

Front: Intravenous induction agent that provides rapid awakening and antiemetic properties, but causes profound dose-dependent vasodilation and hypotension?
Back: Propofol.

Front: Depolarizing neuromuscular blocking agent characteristically associated with transient muscle fasciculations and a transient rise in serum potassium?
Back: Succinylcholine.

Front: Absolute clinical contraindication to the administration of succinylcholine due to the risk of catastrophic hyperkalemic cardiac arrest?
Back: Patients with major burn injuries, massive crush trauma, prolonged immobilization, or upper motor neuron lesions older than 24 to 48 hours.

Front: Preferred non-depolarizing neuromuscular blocking agent for a patient with end-stage renal disease and severe hepatic failure?
Back: Cisatracurium, because it undergoes spontaneous Hoffman elimination independent of renal or hepatic clearance mechanisms.

Front: Pharmacological agent utilized to rapidly reverse deep neuromuscular blockade induced by rocuronium or vecuronium via direct chelation encapsulation?
Back: Sugammadex.

Front: First-line medical therapy indicated for the treatment of a confirmed intraoperative malignant hyperthermia crisis?
Back: Intravenous dantrolene, which blocks calcium release from the ryanodine receptor channels of the sarcoplasmic reticulum.

Front: Most sensitive and earliest indicator of an evolving malignant hyperthermia crisis during volatile gas anesthesia?
Back: A sudden, rapid, and unexplained rise in end-tidal carbon dioxide on the monitor.

Front: Maximum safe dose of plain 1% lidocaine without epinephrine administered for localized infiltration anesthesia?
Back: 4 to 5 milligrams per kilogram of body weight.

Module 19: Post-Anesthesia Care, Postoperative Complications, & Shock States
Front: Most common physiological cause of early postoperative hypoxemia in the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit?
Back: Alveolar hypoventilation secondary to residual neuromuscular blockade or opioid-induced respiratory depression.

Front: Classic clinical presentation of a patient experiencing a severe hemolytic transfusion reaction intraoperatively under general anesthesia?
Back: Unexplained fever, sudden tachycardia, acute hypotension, hemoglobinuria, and diffuse microvascular bleeding or oozing from the surgical field.

Front: Most appropriate initial management strategy for a postoperative patient presenting with suspected acute urinary retention?
Back: Perform a bedside bladder ultrasound scan to quantify urine volume, followed by immediate straight or indwelling catheterization if volume exceeds 350 to 400 mL.

Front: Standard diagnostic investigation utilized to confirm an suspected acute pulmonary embolism in a stable postoperative patient?
Back: Computed tomography pulmonary angiography.

Front: Initial management strategy for a stable patient who develops atrial fibrillation with a rapid ventricular response on postoperative day 2 following major thoracic surgery?
Back: Intravenous rate control utilizing beta-blockers or nondihydropyridine calcium channel blockers like diltiazem.

Front: Primary clinical condition that dictates immediate emergent synchronized electrical cardioversion in a postoperative patient with new-onset tachydysrhythmia?
Back: Hemodynamic instability, characterized by hypotension, altered mental status, or signs of acute myocardial ischemia.

Front: Pathophysiology of neurogenic shock secondary to high spinal cord injury?
Back: Loss of sympathetic vasomotor tone leading to profound peripheral vasodilation and loss of cardiac accelerator nerve input, causing a classic presentation of bradycardia and hypotension.

Front: Hemodynamic profile that defines distributive shock compared to cardiogenic shock?
Back: Markedly decreased systemic vascular resistance accompanied by a normal or elevated cardiac output and decreased pulmonary capillary wedge pressure.

Front: Primary physiological objective of administering continuous positive airway pressure to a patient with postoperative atelectasis?
Back: Recruits collapsed alveoli, increases functional residual capacity, and decreases the work of breathing.

Front: Define the clinical term "postoperative ileus" based on normal gastrointestinal recovery timelines?
Back: Delayed return of coordinated gastrointestinal motility beyond 3 to 5 days postoperatively, characterized by obstipation, abdominal distention, and nausea.

Module 20: Intensive Care Medicine, Sepsis, & Mechanical Ventilation
Front: Primary screening tool utilized at the bedside to rapidly identify patients outside the ICU at high risk for poor outcomes from suspected sepsis?
Back: The quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score, utilizing respiratory rate, altered mentation, and systolic blood pressure.

Front: Three criteria evaluated in the quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score?
Back: Respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute or greater, altered mentation, and a systolic blood pressure of 100 millimeters of mercury or less.

Front: Definitive clinical definition of septic shock according to the Sepsis-3 guidelines?
Back: Sepsis requiring vasopressors to maintain a mean arterial pressure of 65 mmHg or greater and a serum lactate level greater than 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation.

Front: First-line vasopressor of choice recommended for the management of fluid-refractory septic shock?
Back: Norepinephrine, due to its potent alpha-1 adrenergic vasoconstriction combined with modest beta-1 inotropic activity.

Front: Diagnostic criteria that define Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome?
Back: Acute onset within 1 week of a known insult, bilateral opacities on chest imaging not fully explained by fluid overload, and a PaO2/FiO2 ratio less than or equal to 300 mmHg on a PEEP of 5 cmH2O or greater.

Front: Primary mechanical ventilation strategy proven to decrease mortality in patients with severe Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome?
Back: Low-tidal-volume ventilation targeted at 6 milliliters per kilogram of predicted body weight to minimize ventilator-induced lung injury.

Front: Target maximum plateau pressure range mandated during mechanical ventilation for Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome?
Back: Maintain plateau pressures less than or equal to 30 centimeters of water.

Front: Physiological rationale behind utilizing prone positioning in patients with severe, refractory Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome?
Back: Optimizes ventilation-perfusion matching by recruiting dependent dorsal alveoli and reducing alveolar shunt.

Front: Most effective initial intervention to correct acute respiratory acidosis in a mechanically ventilated patient with a low arterial pH and a high PaCO2?
Back: Increase the minute ventilation by either increasing the respiratory rate or increasing the tidal volume on the ventilator settings.

Front: Primary adverse effect associated with excessive positive end-expiratory pressure titration on the cardiovascular system?
Back: Increased intrathoracic pressure leading to decreased venous return, reduced right ventricular preload, and a subsequent drop in cardiac output.

Front: Most important objective clinical factor predicting significant post-operative cardiac complications?

Front: Best single laboratory test to screen for overall nutritional status and long-term surgical risk?

Back: 2 days (prealbumin) vs. 20 days (albumin), making prealbumin superior for acute nutritional monitoring.

Front: Five clinical and laboratory parameters utilized in the Child-Pugh classification system?

Front: Child-Pugh class contraindicated for elective major hepatic resections due to prohibitive mortality?

Front: Most common cause of immediate intraoperative or early postoperative adrenal crisis in a chronic steroid user?

Front: Minimum daily dose and duration of prednisone requiring perioperative stress-dose steroid coverage?

Back: Greater than or equal to 5 mg/day for more than 3 weeks within the past year.

Front: Standard empiric perioperative stress-dose steroid regimen for high-stress major surgeries?

Back: Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV preoperatively, followed by 50 mg IV every 8 hours for 24 hours.

Front: Target perioperative serum glucose range recommended for optimal wound healing and reduced infection rates?

Back: 140 to 180 mg/dL.

Front: Management of metformin the night before or morning of a major surgical procedure?

Back: Hold the medication to minimize the risk of intraoperative metformin-associated lactic acidosis.

Front: Management of long-acting insulin (e.g., glargine) the night before major surgery?

Back: Administer 60% to 80% of the normal dose.

Front: Management of short-acting sliding-scale insulin on the morning of surgery while NPO?

Back: Hold the short-acting insulin and monitor fingerstick glucose every 4 to 6 hours.

Front: Routine pre-operative requirement for a healthy, asymptomatic 22-year-old female undergoing an elective laparoscopic appendectomy?

Back: Serum or urine beta-hCG pregnancy test.

Front: Reversal agent of choice for a patient on chronic Warfarin therapy requiring immediate emergency surgery?

Back: Four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) and intravenous Vitamin K.

Front: Target INR value for safe execution of most elective major surgical procedures?

Back: Less than or equal to 1.5.

Front: Timing for discontinuation of aspirin prior to elective surgical procedures carrying high bleeding risks?

Back: 7 to 10 days before surgery.

Front: Timing for discontinuation of clopidogrel prior to elective major surgery?

Back: 5 days before surgery.

Front: Timing for discontinuation of therapeutic low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) prior to surgery?

Back: 24 hours before the procedure.

Front: Ideal timing for the administration of a pre-operative prophylactic antibiotic to prevent surgical site infection?

Back: Within 60 minutes prior to the surgical incision.

Front: Redosing parameter for prophylactic intraoperative antibiotics during a prolonged surgical procedure?

Back: Every two half-lives of the drug or after a blood loss greater than 1500 mL.

Front: Standard first-line prophylactic antibiotic choice for clean, non-GI/non-GU general surgical procedures?

Back: First-generation cephalosporin such as Cefazolin.

Front: Preferred prophylactic antibiotic choice for elective colorectal procedures due to anaerobe coverage?

Front: Universal ASA physical status classification of a patient with a severe systemic disease that is a constant threat to life?

Back: ASA Class IV.

PART 2: Anesthesia Basics & Intraoperative Management

Front: Principal physiologic feature defining General Anesthesia compared to deep sedation?

Back: Loss of protective airway reflexes and requirement for ventilatory assistance.

Front: Primary mechanism of action of local anesthetics like lidocaine?

Back: Reversible blockade of voltage-gated sodium channels along the neuronal axon.

Front: Sequence of nerve fiber block by local anesthetics?

Back: Pain (C and A-delta fibers), temperature, touch, and lastly motor function.

Front: Maximum safe dose of plain local lidocaine formulation?

Back: 4 mg/kg.

Front: Maximum safe dose of lidocaine formulated with epinephrine?

Back: 7 mg/kg.

Front: Primary clinical purpose of adding epinephrine to a local anesthetic solution?

Back: Local vasoconstriction to prolong duration of action and decrease systemic absorption/toxicity.

Front: Classic early systemic symptom of local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST)?

Back: Perioral numbness, metallic taste, and tinnitus.

Front: First-line intravenous antidote for severe local anesthetic systemic toxicity presenting with cardiac arrest?

Back: 20% Intravenous Lipid Emulsion therapy.

Front: Primary anatomical location where spinal anesthesia (intrathecal injection) is performed?

Back: Subarachnoid space below the level of the L2 nerve root.

Front: Most common physiological side effect seen immediately after induction of spinal or epidural anesthesia?

Back: Hypotension due to sympathetic blockade causing profound venodilatation.

Front: Primary cause of a postural post-dural puncture headache (PDPH) following neuroaxial anesthesia?

Back: Persistent leakage of cerebrospinal fluid through the dural puncture site.

Front: Definitive procedural treatment for a severe, refractory post-dural puncture headache?

Back: Epidural blood patch.

Front: Primary absolute contraindication to the placement of a spinal or epidural catheter?

Back: Patient refusal, localized infection at the puncture site, or severe uncorrected coagulopathy.

Front: Principal chemical compound used to induce rapid sequence intubation that is an ultra-short-acting depolarizing neuromuscular blocker?

Back: Succinylcholine.

Front: Severe electrolyte abnormality that serves as an absolute contraindication to succinylcholine administration?

Back: Hyperkalemia.

Front: Why is succinylcholine contraindicated in patients with severe burn injuries or crush trauma after 24 to 48 hours?

Back: Upregulation of extrajunctional acetylcholine receptors leads to catastrophic, life-threatening efflux of potassium.

Front: Standard pharmacological reversal agent combination for non-depolarizing neuromuscular blockers like rocuronium?

Back: Neostigmine (acetylcholinesterase inhibitor) combined with Glycopyrrolate or Atropine (anticholinergic).

Front: Novel reversal agent that encapsulates and inactivates rocuronium or vecuronium directly?

Back: Sugammadex.

Front: Inhaled anesthetic agent uniquely associated with diffusion hypoxia and expansion of closed gas-filled body spaces?

Back: Nitrous oxide.

Front: Pathophysiology of Malignant Hyperthermia?

Back: Uncontrolled calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum via altered ryanodine receptors (RYR1).

Front: Two main classes of pharmaceutical triggers for Malignant Hyperthermia?

Back: Volatile halogenated inhalational anesthetics and the depolarizing muscle relaxant succinylcholine.

Front: Earliest and most sensitive clinical sign of developing Malignant Hyperthermia in the operating room?

Back: Abrupt, unexplained rise in end-tidal carbon dioxide ($ETCO_2$).

Front: Definitive immediate pharmacological treatment for Malignant Hyperthermia?

Back: Intravenous Dantrolene.

Front: Mechanism of action of Dantrolene in treating Malignant Hyperthermia?

Back: Binds to the ryanodine receptor to directly inhibit calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum.

Front: Intravenous induction agent of choice for patients in severe hypovolemic or septic shock due to its hemodynamic neutrality?

Back: Etomidate.

Front: Primary unique endocrine side effect associated with an induction dose of Etomidate?

Back: Transient adrenal suppression via inhibition of the 11-beta-hydroxylase enzyme.

Front: Intravenous induction agent that acts as a potent bronchodilator and preserves sympathetic drive, making it ideal for asthmatics or shock states?

Back: Ketamine.

Front: Adverse CNS effect classically associated with emerging from Ketamine anesthesia?

Back: Emergence delirium and vivid hallucinations.

Front: Intravenous anesthetic agent contraindicated in patients with a severe documented egg or soy allergy?

Back: Propofol.

Front: Primary hemodynamic effect observed immediately following a bolus injection of Propofol?

Back: Significant systemic vasodilation and myocardial depression leading to hypotension.

Front: Airway classification system used to predict the technical difficulty of endotracheal intubation based on visible structures?

Back: Mallampati Classification.

Front: Structures fully visible in a Class I Mallampati airway?

Back: Soft palate, fauces, uvula, and both anterior and posterior tonsillar pillars.

PART 3: Post-Operative Management & Fluids/Electrolytes

Front: Standard maintenance fluid calculator rule for calculating hourly intravenous fluid requirements in adults?

Back: The 4-2-1 Rule.

Front: Composition of 0.9% Normal Saline relative to human plasma tonicity?

Back: Hypertonic relative to plasma due to unphysiologically high concentrations of sodium and chloride ($154\text{ mEq/L}$ each).

Front: Acid-base disturbance characteristically induced by aggressive resuscitation with 0.9% Normal Saline?

Back: Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis.

Front: Intravenous crystalloid fluid choice that most closely mirrors human physiologic electrolyte profiles?

Back: Lactated Ringer's solution.

Front: Why must Lactated Ringer's solution be avoided in the same intravenous line used for blood transfusions?

Back: Calcium present in Lactated Ringer's can bind to the citrate anticoagulant in blood products, precipitating clot formation.

Front: Standard daily basal physiological requirement for sodium in an adult?

Back: 1 to 2 mEq/kg per 24 hours.

Front: Standard daily basal physiological requirement for potassium in an adult?

Back: 0.5 to 1 mEq/kg per 24 hours.

Front: Most reliable clinical indicator of adequate end-organ perfusion and fluid resuscitation status in a post-operative patient?

Back: Urine output tracking at greater than or equal to 0.5 mL/kg/hour for adults.

Front: Earliest sign of volume overload in a post-operative cardiac patient on maintenance intravenous fluids?

Back: New-onset tachypnea and crackles on pulmonary auscultation due to interstitial edema.

Front: Primary physiological driver of post-operative fluid retention during the first 24 to 48 hours following major surgery?

Back: Physiologic stress-induced release of ADH and aldosterone.

Front: Immediate initial intervention for a post-operative patient presenting with asymptomatic mild hyponatremia ($132\text{ mEq/L}$)?

Back: Restriction of free water intake and close monitoring of serum sodium levels.

Front: Maximum safe rate of serum sodium correction in severe chronic hyponatremia to avoid neurologic devastation?

Back: Less than 8 to 12 mEq/L per 24 hours.

Front: Catastrophic neurological condition caused by overly rapid correction of severe hyponatremia?

Back: Central pontine myelinolysis (also called osmotic demyelination syndrome).

Front: Earliest electrocardiogram (ECG) changes pathognomonic for significant hyperkalemia?

Back: Symmetrical, narrow-based, peaked T waves.

Front: Immediate first-line medication administered to stabilize the cardiac myocyte membrane in severe hyperkalemia with ECG changes?

Back: Intravenous Calcium Gluconate or Calcium Chloride.

Front: Pharmacological agent that promotes intracellular shifting of potassium within minutes during an acute hyperkalemic crisis?

Back: Intravenous regular insulin administered concurrently with 50% Dextrose ($D_{50}$).

Front: Electrocardiogram findings classic for severe post-operative hypokalemia?

Back: Flattening of T waves, ST-segment depression, and prominent U waves.

Front: Refractory hypokalemia that cannot be successfully corrected with potassium supplementation is almost always due to what concurrent deficiency?

Back: Hypomagnesemia.

PART 4: Post-Operative Complications & Fever Assessment

Front: The classic clinical mnemonic framework used to diagnose post-operative fever by days?

Back: Wind (Days 1-2), Water (Days 3-5), Walking (Days 5-7), Wound (Days 7-10), Wonder drugs (Any day).

Front: Most common cause of an isolated post-operative fever occurring within the first 24 to 48 hours?

Back: Atelectasis or early systemic inflammatory response to surgical trauma.

Front: Primary intervention to prevent and treat early post-operative atelectasis?

Back: Incentivize aggressive use of incentive spirometry, chest physiotherapy, and early ambulation.

Front: Most common cause of a new-onset post-operative fever on post-op days 3 to 5?

Back: Urinary Tract Infection (UTI), frequently secondary to indwelling Foley catheters.

Front: Most common cause of a new-onset post-operative fever on post-op days 5 to 7?

Back: Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) or secondary Pulmonary Embolism (PE).

Front: Classic timing for a superficial or deep Surgical Site Infection (SSI) to present with fever?

Back: Post-operative days 7 to 10.

Front: Definitive initial treatment for a superficial surgical site wound infection demonstrating fluctuance and erythema?

Back: Removal of skin staples/sutures, opening of the wound incision, and manual drainage of the purulent collection.

Front: High-yield exception where a severe, necrotizing wound infection presents with fever within 24 hours of surgery?

Back: Necrotizing infection caused by Clostridium perfringens or Group A Streptococcus.

Front: Definitive management strategy for an intra-abdominal abscess identified by CT scan on post-operative day 10?

Back: Image-guided percutaneous drainage and targeted intravenous antibiotic therapy.

Front: Primary objective clinical sign indicating the resolution of a post-operative paralytic ileus?

Back: Return of flatus, bowel movements, and a tolerated oral diet.

Front: Normal expected timeframe for the resolution of a physiological post-operative paralytic ileus after uncomplicated open laparotomy?

Back: 3 to 5 days.

Front: Definition of Ogilvie Syndrome in postoperative patients?

Back: Acute colonic pseudo-obstruction characterized by massive dilation of the cecum and right colon without mechanical blockage.

Front: Cecal diameter threshold on abdominal X-ray in Ogilvie Syndrome that carries an imminent risk of perforation?

Back: Greater than 10 to 12 cm.

Front: First-line pharmacological agent used to decompress the colon in a patient with refractory Ogilvie Syndrome?

Back: Neostigmine.

Front: Most common cause of post-operative mechanical small bowel obstruction within the first few weeks after abdominal surgery?

Back: Early fibrinous intra-abdominal adhesions.

Front: Initial diagnostic imaging modality of choice to differentiate between paralytic ileus and early mechanical small bowel obstruction?

Back: Plain abdominal X-ray series (supine and erect).

Front: Primary risk factor for post-operative acute cholecystitis occurring in critically ill, fasted ICU patients?

Back: Gallbladder stasis and ischemia leading to Acute Acalculous Cholecystitis.

Front: Definitive emergent treatment of choice for an unstable patient with acute acalculous cholecystitis?

Back: Percutaneous transhepatic cholecystostomy tube placement.

Front: Primary physiological diagnostic criterion for Postoperative Pancreatic Fistula (POPF) based on drain fluid analysis?

Back: Drain fluid amylase concentration greater than 3 times the upper limit of normal serum amylase on or after post-op day 3.

PART 5: Wound Healing, DVT Prophylaxis & Trauma Basics

Front: Definition of wound dehiscence?

Back: Partial or complete separation of the fascial layers of a surgical wound.

Front: Immediate initial management step for a patient presenting with an acute fascial dehiscence and bowel evisceration?

Back: Cover the exposed abdominal contents with sterile, saline-moistened dressings and transfer immediately to the operating room.

Front: Three components of Virchow's Triad that predict venous thromboembolism risk?

Back: Endothelial injury, stasis of blood flow, and hypercoagulability.

Front: Standard gold standard non-invasive diagnostic modality for a suspected lower extremity deep vein thrombosis?

Back: Compression duplex venous ultrasonography.

Front: Diagnostic modality of choice to rule out a pulmonary embolism in a stable post-operative patient with acute dyspnea?

Back: Computed Tomography Pulmonary Angiography (CTPA).

Front: First-line treatment for an acute pulmonary embolism in a hemodynamically stable postoperative patient where anticoagulation is safe?

Back: Therapeutic systemic anticoagulation with unfractionated or low-molecular-weight heparin.

Front: Indication for the placement of an Inferior Vena Cava (IVC) filter in a patient with a documented acute proximal DVT?

Back: Absolute contraindication to systemic anticoagulation or failure of anticoagulation with documented recurrent emboli.

Front: Management of a hemodynamically unstable patient with a massive pulmonary embolism causing obstructive shock?

Back: Emergent surgical embolectomy or targeted catheter-directed thrombolysis.

Front: The absolute first step in the primary survey of a trauma patient according to Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) protocol?

Back: Airway maintenance with strict cervical spine motion restriction.

Front: Immediate intervention for a trauma patient presenting with tracheal deviation, absent unilateral breath sounds, and hypotension?

Back: Emergent needle decompression in the second intercostal space at the midclavicular line or fifth intercostal space at the anterior axillary line.

Front: Definitive treatment that must follow immediate needle decompression of a tension pneumothorax?

Back: Tube thoracostomy (chest tube insertion).

Front: Definition of a massive hemothorax according to ATLS guidelines?

Back: Accumulation of greater than 1500 mL of blood or greater than one-third of the patient's blood volume in the pleural cavity upon initial chest tube placement.

Front: Volume threshold of ongoing chest tube output from a hemothorax that serves as an indication for emergent thoracotomy?

Back: Greater than 200 mL/hour of blood for 2 to 4 consecutive hours.

Front: The classic clinical triad of physical findings diagnostic of cardiac tamponade?

Back: Beck's Triad: Hypotension, muffled heart sounds, and jugular venous distention.

Front: Immediate diagnostic test used at the bedside to screen for intra-abdominal free fluid or cardiac tamponade in acute trauma?

Back: Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST) exam.

Front: The four distinct anatomical zones evaluated during a standard FAST exam?

Back: Perihepatic (RUQ/Hepatorenal space), Perisplenic (LUQ), Pelvic (Suprapubic space), and Pericardial views.

Front: Next immediate step in managing an hemodynamically unstable blunt trauma patient with a positive FAST exam?

Back: Emergent exploratory laparotomy.

Front: Next step in managing an hemodynamically stable trauma patient with a positive FAST exam?

Back: Contrast-enhanced Computed Tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen and pelvis.

Front: Definition of a positive diagnostic peritoneal lavage (DPL) in a blunt trauma patient?

Back: Aspiration of 10 mL of gross blood or laboratory analysis showing greater than 100,000 RBCs/mm³ or greater than 500 WBCs/mm³.

Front: Definitive management for any patient presenting with a penetrating abdominal stab wound and signs of peritonitis or hemodynamic instability?

Back: Emergent exploratory laparotomy.

Front: Classic sign of urethral injury in a male pelvic fracture patient that serves as an absolute contraindication to blind Foley catheterization?

Back: High-riding prostate on rectal exam, blood at the urethral meatus, or perineal ecchymosis.

Front: Diagnostic test required to evaluate for urethral integrity prior to catheterization if urethral injury is suspected?

Back: Retrograde Urethrogram (RUG).

Front: Primary goal of Lethal Triad management in trauma resuscitation?

Back: Simultaneous correction of Hypothermia, Acidosis, and Coagulopathy.

Front: Standard target ratio of packed red blood cells, fresh frozen plasma, and platelets in a massive transfusion protocol?

Back: Balanced 1:1:1 ratio.

PART 6: Post-Operative Pain & Specialized Critical Care

Front: Primary adverse respiratory effect of systemic opioid analgesics used for post-operative pain control?

Back: Dose-dependent depression of the central respiratory drive and decreased hypercapnic ventillatory response.

Front: The most common gastrointestinal side effect of chronic post-operative opioid use?

Back: Constipation due to decreased intestinal motility and delayed colonic transit.

Front: Primary benefit of incorporating non-opioid adjuvants (e.g., Ketorolac, Acetaminophen) into a post-operative pain regimen?

Back: Opioid-sparing effect which minimizes opioid-related side effects and accelerates bowel function return.

Front: Contraindication to the use of Scheduled NSAIDs like Ketorolac in the early post-operative period?

Back: Acute kidney injury, active gastrointestinal ulceration, or operations with high risks of severe bleeding complications.

Front: Definition of post-operative delirium?

Back: An acute, fluctuating disturbance in attention, awareness, and baseline cognition occurring after surgery.

Front: First-line non-pharmacological management for hyperactive post-operative delirium in an elderly patient?

Back: Reorientation, restoration of sleep-wake cycles, minimization of tethering lines, and avoidance of physical restraints.

Front: Class of medications that must be avoided in elderly patients with post-operative delirium due to the risk of worsening confusion?

Back: Benzodiazepines (unless delirium is due to alcohol or sedative withdrawal).

Front: Diagnostic criteria for Abdominal Compartment Syndrome (ACS)?

Back: Sustained intra-abdominal pressure greater than 20 mmHg associated with new onset organ dysfunction.

Front: Standard gold-standard clinical method used to measure intra-abdominal pressure at the bedside?

Back: Transurethral bladder pressure manometry.

Front: Definitive management for severe, refractory Abdominal Compartment Syndrome causing renal or respiratory failure?

Back: Emergent surgical decompression via open laparotomy.

Front: Most common cause of immediate postoperative hypoxemia in the PACU while a patient is still emerging from general anesthesia?

Back: Hypoventilation secondary to residual neuromuscular blockade or opioid-induced respiratory depression.

Front: Primary clinical indicator that a postoperative patient is experiencing severe, symptomatic hypocalcemia after total thyroidectomy?

Back: Perioral numbness, tingling in fingertips, and positive Chvostek or Trousseau signs.

Front: Electrocardiogram finding pathognomonic for severe hypocalcemia?

Back: QT interval prolongation, which predisposes the patient to torsades de pointes.

Front: Fastest, most direct way to correct life-threatening hypermagnesemia presenting with loss of deep tendon reflexes?

Back: Intravenous Calcium Gluconate to antagonize the neuromuscular effects of magnesium.

Front: Most common non-iatrogenic cause of acute postoperative hypernatremia?

Back: Unreplaced free water losses from unmonitored diabetes insipidus, excessive sweating, or prolonged tachypnea.

Front: Pathophysiology of Post-Op Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT)?

Back: IgG antibodies form against the Heparin-Platelet Factor 4 (PF4) complex, leading to massive platelet activation.

Front: Classic timing and presentation of Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) type 2?

Back: A 50% or greater drop in baseline platelet count occurring 5 to 10 days after starting heparin therapy.

Front: Paradoxical clinical complication that makes Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) highly dangerous?

Back: Prothrombotic state causing severe venous or arterial thrombosis, rather than active clinical bleeding.

Front: Immediate initial step in the management of suspected Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT)?

Back: Stop all heparin-containing products immediately and initiate an alternative non-heparin anticoagulant like Argatroban.



PART 8: Surgical Infection, Antibiotic Management & Lines

Front: Definition of a "Clean-Contaminated" surgical wound according to CDC classifications?

Back: An operative wound where the respiratory, alimentary, genital, or urinary tracts are entered under controlled conditions without unusual contamination.

Front: Standard timing for the removal of a prophylactic surgical line or urinary catheter to prevent hospital-acquired infection?

Back: Within 24 hours postoperatively, unless there is a documented structural or monitoring requirement.

Front: Most common bacterial pathogen isolated from surgical site infections following clean orthopedic or cardiac procedures?

Back: Staphylococcus aureus (including MRSA).

Front: Primary bacterial pathogen suspected in a postoperative wound infection following a contaminated colorectal or pelvic operation?

Back: Bacteroides fragilis or Escherichia coli.

Front: Classic clinical presentation of a central venous catheter-associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI)?

Back: Unexplained fever, chills, and hemodynamic instability in a patient with an indwelling central line and no other clear source of infection.

Front: Diagnostic criteria for a confirmed Central Line-Associated Bloodstream Infection (CLABSI)?

Back: Positive blood cultures from a peripheral vein and the central line catheter tip growing the exact same organism.



PART 9: Wound Complications, Hernias & Tubes

Front: Primary mechanical difference between a seroma and a hematoma at a surgical incision site?

Back: A seroma is a collection of acellular, straw-colored lymphatic fluid, whereas a hematoma is a localized collection of liquid or clotted blood.

Front: Initial management for a large, symptomatic subcutaneous postoperative hematoma that compromises overlying skin perfusion?

Back: Sterile opening of the incision, evacuation of the blood clot, and control of any active bleeding vessels.

Front: Pathophysiology of a postoperative incisional hernia?

Back: Iatrogenic failure of the fascial closure due to technical failure, wound infection, tissue ischemia, or increased intra-abdominal pressure.

Front: The single most critical risk factor for the development of an incisional hernia following an open laparotomy?

Back: Deep postoperative surgical site wound infection.

Front: Primary physiological indication for utilizing a closed-suction drain (e.g., Jackson-Pratt) rather than an open drain (e.g., Penrose)?

Back: Closed-suction prevents the atmospheric entry of bacteria into a deep dead space while actively evacuating accumulating fluid.



PART 10: Pulmonary, Thromboembolic & Transfusion Nuances

Front: Clinical presentation distinguishing Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury (TRALI) from Transfusion-Associated Circulatory Overload (TACO)?

Back: TRALI presents with non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema (normal PCWP/no JVD), whereas TACO presents with volume overload (elevated PCWP/distended JVD).

Front: Pathophysiology of Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury (TRALI)?

Back: Neutrophil activation in the pulmonary vasculature triggered by donor anti-HLA or anti-neutrophil antibodies.

Front: Most common infectious risk associated with the administration of allogeneic packed red blood cell transfusions?

Back: Bacterial contamination, which occurs more frequently in platelets than in packed red blood cells due to room temperature storage.

Front: Classic clinical presentation of a localized postoperative deep vein thrombosis in a lower extremity?

Back: Unilateral calf swelling, erythema, warmth, and tenderness along the distribution of the deep venous system.

Front: Primary absolute contraindication to pharmacologic DVT prophylaxis using low-molecular-weight heparin in a postoperative patient?

Back: Active un-uncontrolled bleeding, severe uncorrected coagulopathy, or a platelet count less than 50,000.



PART 11: Trauma, Airway & Emergency Surgical Scenarios

Front: Immediate initial step in managing an open pneumothorax ("sucking chest wound") identified during the primary survey?

Back: Application of a sterile, occlusive dressing taped securely on three sides to create a flutter-valve effect.

Front: Why is a sterile occlusive dressing for an open pneumothorax taped on only three sides?

Back: It prevents air entry during inspiration but allows trapped air to escape during expiration, preventing conversion into a tension pneumothorax.

Front: Definition of a flail chest injury?

Back: Fractures of two or more contiguous ribs in two or more places, resulting in a free-floating segment of the chest wall.

Front: Pathognomonic physical examination finding seen in a patient with a flail chest?

Back: Paradoxical chest wall movement, where the flail segment moves inward during inspiration and outward during expiration.

Front: Primary physiological cause of hypoxia in a patient with a flail chest injury?

Back: Underlying pulmonary contusion caused by the initial compressive force, rather than the mechanical movement of the chest wall.

Front: Immediate management for a patient with a confirmed major blunt pulmonary contusion?

Back: Aggressive pain optimization, strict fluid restriction to minimize alveolar edema, and targeted pulmonary toilet.

Front: Classic signs of a basilar skull fracture that contraindicate the placement of a nasogastric (NG) tube?

Back: Battle sign (mastoid ecchymosis), Raccoon eyes (periorbital ecchymosis), CSF rhinorrhea/otorrhea, or hemotympanum.

Front: Definitive diagnostic imaging modality of choice to evaluate for blunt aortic injury in a hemodynamically stable deceleration trauma patient?

Back: Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) of the chest.

Front: Anatomical site where the vast majority of blunt traumatic aortic disruptions occur?

Back: The aortic isthmus, located immediately distal to the origin of the left subclavian artery.

Front: Definitive airway of choice in a maxillofacial trauma patient where standard endotracheal intubation has failed or is anatomically impossible?

Back: Emergent surgical cricothyroidotomy.

Front: Why is a surgical cricothyroidotomy contraindicated in children under the age of 12?

Back: The cricoid cartilage is the only circumferential support for the upper airway in children, and damaging it carries a high risk of subglottic stenosis.

Front: Definitive alternative emergent airway procedure for a child under the age of 12 requiring a surgical airway?

Back: Needle cricothyroidotomy with jet ventilation.

Front: Primary physical exam sign that differentiates a complete urethral transection from a partial urethral tear in a male pelvic trauma patient?

Back: High-riding prostate on digital rectal exam combined with a large pelvic hematoma.

Front: First-line fluid resuscitation strategy for an adult trauma patient in hemorrhagic shock prior to the arrival of blood products?

Back: Administration of a warm 1-liter bolus of isotonic crystalloid (Lactated Ringer's or Normal Saline).

Front: Definition of a positive diagnostic peritoneal aspirate (DPA) indicating immediate laparotomy?

Back: Free aspiration of greater than or equal to 10 mL of gross blood or enteric contents immediately upon catheter insertion into the peritoneal cavity.

Section:  General Surgery

Topic: Abdominal surgery — appendicitis, hernias, bowel obstruction


Front: Most common cause of acute appendicitis in adults?
Back: Fecalith (appending obstruction).

Front: Most common cause of acute appendicitis in children?
Back: Lymphoid hyperplasia (often post-viral).

Front: Classic chronological order of appendicitis symptoms?
Back: Periumbilical pain, nausea/vomiting, migration to right lower quadrant (RLQ), fever.

Front: Why is appendicitis pain initially periumbilical?
Back: Luminal distension stimulates visceral afferent pain fibers at T10 level.

Front: Why does appendicitis pain later localize to the RLQ?
Back: Progressive inflammation irritates the parietal peritoneum.

Front: Definition of McBurney's point?
Back: Two-thirds of the distance from the umbilicus to the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS).

Front: Positive Rovsing sign definition?
Back: Deep palpation of the left lower quadrant causes pain in the right lower quadrant.

Front: Positive Psoas sign mechanism?
Back: RLQ pain triggered by passive right hip extension, indicating a retrocecal appendix.

Front: Positive Obturator sign mechanism?
Back: RLQ pain triggered by internal rotation of the flexed right hip, indicating a pelvic appendix.

Diagnostics & Special Populations
Front: Initial imaging of choice for suspected appendicitis in pregnant women or children?
Back: Ultrasound.

Front: Gold standard imaging modality for appendicitis in adult males and non-pregnant females?
Back: CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis with contrast.

Front: Key CT scan findings diagnostic of acute appendicitis?
Back: Appendix diameter greater than 6 mm, wall thickening (greater than 2 mm), and periappendiceal fat stranding.

Front: Classically expected finding on a complete blood count (CBC) in acute appendicitis?
Back: Leukocytosis with a left shift (increased band cells).

Front: Why is a urinalysis mandatory in suspected appendicitis?
Back: To rule out nephrolithiasis or urinary tract infection, though a pelvic appendix can cause sterile pyuria.

Front: Most common misdiagnosis of appendicitis in young children?
Back: Gastroenteritis or mesenteric adenitis.

Front: Most common misdiagnosis of appendicitis in young adult females?
Back: Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) or ruptured ovarian cyst.

Front: Clinical presentation of appendicitis in elderly patients?
Back: Often atypical with minimal pain, absence of fever, and delayed presentation leading to higher perforation rates.

Management & Complications
Front: Primary definitive management for acute uncomplicated appendicitis?
Back: Urgent laparoscopic (or open) appendectomy.

Front: Preoperative medication required for all patients undergoing appendectomy?
Back: Single dose of prophylactic intravenous antibiotics covering gram-negative and anaerobic organisms.

Front: Management of an appendiceal abscess or stable perforated appendicitis with a contained phlegmon?
Back: Intravenous antibiotics, percutaneous drainage if fluid collection is manageable, and interval appendectomy in 6 to 8 weeks.

Front: Management of perforated appendicitis presenting with generalized peritonitis?
Back: Emergent fluid resuscitation, emergency exploratory laparotomy or laparoscopy, appendectomy, and peritoneal irrigation.

Front: Most common post-operative complication following an appendectomy for perforated appendicitis?
Back: Intra-abdominal or pelvic abscess.

Front: Most common tumor found inside an extirpated appendix?
Back: Neuroendocrine tumor (formerly carcinoid tumor).

Front: Surgical management of an appendiceal neuroendocrine tumor less than 2 cm located at the tip?
Back: Simple appendectomy.

Front: Surgical management of an appendiceal neuroendocrine tumor greater than 2 cm or involving the base?
Back: Right hemicolectomy.

Hernias
Inguinal Hernia Anatomy & Types
Front: Boundaries of Hesselbach's triangle?
Back: Inferior epigastric vessels (lateral), rectus abdominis sheath (medial), and inguinal ligament (inferior).

Front: Anatomical defect location in a direct inguinal hernia?
Back: Medial to the inferior epigastric vessels, directly through the posterior wall of the inguinal canal (transversalis fascia).

Front: Anatomical defect location in an indirect inguinal hernia?
Back: Lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels, passing through the deep inguinal ring.

Front: Embryological cause of an indirect inguinal hernia?
Back: Patent processus vaginalis.

Front: Which type of inguinal hernia is most common in both males and females?
Back: Indirect inguinal hernia.

Front: Nerve most commonly injured during open anterior inguinal hernia repair (Lichtenstein)?
Back: Ilioinguinal nerve.

Front: Sensory deficit associated with ilioinguinal nerve injury during hernia repair?
Back: Numbness over the base of the penis, scrotum (or labium majus), and medial thigh.

Front: Nerve injured when a patient experiences burning pain radiating to the lateral thigh post-hernia repair?
Back: Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve.

Front: Contents of the inguinal canal in males?
Back: Spermatic cord and the ilioinguinal nerve.

Front: Contents of the inguinal canal in females?
Back: Round ligament of the uterus and the ilioinguinal nerve.

Femoral & Ventral Hernias
Front: Anatomical location of a femoral hernia relative to the inguinal ligament?
Back: Inferior to the inguinal ligament.

Front: Anatomical boundaries of the femoral ring?
Back: Inguinal ligament (anterior), Cooper's ligament (posterior), femoral vein (lateral), and lacunar ligament (medial).

Front: Patient demographic with the highest relative incidence of femoral hernias?
Back: Elderly females.

Front: Why do femoral hernias have the highest rate of incarceration and strangulation?
Back: The femoral ring boundaries are rigid and unyielding.

Front: Primary risk factor for the development of an incisional hernia?
Back: Postoperative wound infection.

Front: Define an umbilical hernia in adults?
Back: A ventral hernia occurring through the umbilical ring, highly associated with ascites, obesity, and pregnancy.

Front: Management of asymptomatic umbilical hernias in newborns?
Back: Observation; most close spontaneously by age 2 to 5 years.

Front: Indication for surgical repair of an infantile umbilical hernia?
Back: Persistence past age 5, or if the defect is greater than 2 cm, or if incarceration occurs.

Complex Hernia Variants
Front: Definition of a Richter hernia?
Back: Only a portion of the antimesenteric border of the bowel wall herniates through the defect without causing complete obstruction.

Front: Danger of a Richter hernia?
Back: Bowel strangulation and necrosis can occur silently without standard clinical signs of bowel obstruction.

Front: Definition of a Littre hernia?
Back: Any hernia containing a Meckel's diverticulum.

Front: Definition of a Amyand hernia?
Back: An inguinal hernia containing the urinary bladder or acute appendicitis inside the sac.

Front: Definition of a sliding hernia?
Back: A hernia where a portion of the wall of the hernia sac is formed by an intra-abdominal organ (e.g., cecum or sigmoid colon).

Front: Definition of a Pantaloon hernia?
Back: Coexisting direct and indirect inguinal hernias on the same side, straddling the inferior epigastric vessels.

Front: Definition of a Spigelian hernia?
Back: A hernia through the spigelian fascia, located between the rectus abdominis muscle medially and the semilunar line laterally.

Clinical States & Management
Front: Definition of an incarcerated hernia?
Back: A hernia whose contents cannot be manually reduced back into the abdominal cavity.

Front: Definition of a strangulated hernia?
Back: An incarcerated hernia with compromised blood supply leading to ischemia and potential necrosis.

Front: Cardinal clinical signs differentiating strangulated hernia from incarcerated hernia?
Back: Severe pain out of proportion, skin erythema/warmth over the sac, fever, leukocytosis, and systemic toxicity.

Front: Immediate management of a suspected strangulated hernia?
Back: Emergent surgical exploration; manual reduction is strictly contraindicated.

Front: Why is manual reduction contraindicated in strangulated hernias?
Back: It risks returning necrotic, perforated bowel back into the peritoneal cavity, causing systemic sepsis.

Front: Standard approach for elective, symptomatic inguinal hernia repair in adults?
Back: Tension-free repair utilizing a prosthetic mesh (open Lichtenstein or laparoscopic preperitoneal repair).

Front: Primary contraindication to using prosthetic mesh during hernia repair?
Back: Contaminated or infected surgical fields (e.g., strangulated bowel with perforation).

Bowel Obstruction
Small Bowel Obstruction (SBO) Etiology & Presentation
Front: Most common cause of small bowel obstruction in industrialized nations?
Back: Postoperative intra-abdominal adhesions.

Front: Most common cause of small bowel obstruction worldwide?
Back: Incarcerated hernias.

Front: Second most common cause of small bowel obstruction in adults?
Back: Neoplasms (primary or metastatic).

Front: Classic triad of clinical symptoms in a small bowel obstruction?
Back: Colicky abdominal pain, abdominal distension, nausea/vomiting, and obstipation.

Front: Define obstipation?
Back: The absence of both stool and flatus passage, indicating a complete bowel obstruction.

Front: Early vs. late bowel sounds heard on auscultation in acute mechanical SBO?
Back: Early sounds are high-pitched, hyperactive, and tinkling; late sounds are hypoactive or absent.

Front: Difference in emesis presentation between proximal and distal small bowel obstructions?
Back: Proximal SBO presents with early, bilious emesis; distal SBO presents with late, feculent emesis.

SBO Diagnostics & Lab Findings
Front: Initial diagnostic workup for a suspected small bowel obstruction?
Back: Abdominal plain radiographs (supine and erect views) alongside a chest X-ray.

Front: Classic diagnostic findings on an erect abdominal X-ray for SBO?
Back: Dilated loops of small bowel (greater than 3 cm), air-fluid levels in a stepped pattern, and an absence of colonic gas.

Front: Definitive imaging of choice to differentiate partial from complete SBO and locate the transition point?
Back: CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis with oral and intravenous contrast.

Front: Definition of a "transition point" on a CT scan for bowel obstruction?
Back: A distinct zone where dilated proximal bowel loops abruptly narrow into decompressed distal bowel loops.

Front: Metabolic abnormality characteristically caused by severe vomiting in high small bowel obstruction?
Back: Hypokalemic, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis.

Front: What does an elevated serum lactate or worsening leukocytosis indicate in a patient with an SBO?
Back: Intestinal ischemia, strangulation, or impending necrosis.

SBO Management & Complications
Front: Initial conservative management steps for an uncomplicated, partial small bowel obstruction?
Back: Fluid resuscitation, electrolyte repletion, bowel rest (NPO status), and nasogastric (NG) tube decompression.

Front: Most reliable clinical parameter to monitor fluid resuscitation adequacy in SBO?
Back: Foley catheter insertion to measure hourly urine output (target greater than 0.5 mL/kg/hour).

Front: Direct absolute indication for immediate operative intervention in small bowel obstruction?
Back: Signs of strangulation, peritonitis, closed-loop obstruction, or a completely virgin abdomen with signs of ischemia.

Front: Definition and risk of a closed-loop obstruction?
Back: A segment of bowel obstructed at two proximal and distal points simultaneously, causing rapid pressures and early ischemia without proximal decompression.

Front: Classical cause of closed-loop small bowel obstruction?
Back: Volvulus or an internal hernia trapped via an adhesive band.

Front: Classic diagnostic finding of gallstone ileus on a plain abdominal radiograph?
Back: Rigler's triad: pneumobilia (air in biliary tree), small bowel obstruction, and an ectopic radiopaque gallstone.

Front: Mechanism behind the development of gallstone ileus?
Back: Cholecystitis leads to a cholecystenteric fistula, allowing a large gallstone to enter the bowel and impact at the ileocecal valve.

Large Bowel Obstruction (LBO)
LBO Etiology & Clinical Differentiation
Front: Most common cause of large bowel obstruction in adults?
Back: Colorectal adenocarcinoma.

Front: Second most common cause of large bowel obstruction in adults?
Back: Volvulus (sigmoid or cecal).

Front: Third most common cause of large bowel obstruction in adults?
Back: Diverticulitis with fibrotic stricture formation.

Front: Key clinical features differentiating large bowel obstruction from small bowel obstruction?
Back: LBO features more pronounced abdominal distension, later onset of pain/vomiting, and longer periods of obstipation.

Front: Role of a competent ileocecal valve in a large bowel obstruction?
Back: It creates a closed-loop obstruction between the valve and the distal block, massively increasing cecal perforation risk.

Front: Laplace's law applied to the large bowel during obstruction?
Back: Wall tension is proportional to pressure times radius; therefore, the segment with the largest radius (the cecum) is most prone to perforation.

Front: Critical cecal diameter threshold on imaging indicating imminent risk of perforation?
Back: Ten to twelve centimeters (10-12 cm).

Volvulus & Ogilvie Syndrome
Front: Definition of a volvulus?
Back: Torsion or twisting of a loop of bowel around its mesenteric axis, leading to mechanical obstruction and ischemia.

Front: Most common location for a volvulus in the gastrointestinal tract?
Back: Sigmoid colon.

Front: Classic appearance of a sigmoid volvulus on an abdominal plain radiograph?
Back: "Coffee bean" sign or inverted U-shape pointing toward the right upper quadrant.

Front: Initial management for an unperforated, stable sigmoid volvulus?
Back: Flexible sigmoidoscopy for reduction and detorsion, followed by elective rectal tube placement and subsequent semi-elective resection.

Front: Classic radiographic appearance of a cecal volvulus?
Back: "Teardrop" or "embryo" shaped air fluid pattern pointing toward the left upper quadrant.

Front: Definitive management for a cecal volvulus?
Back: Emergent surgical resection (ileocecal resection or right hemicolectomy); endoscopic reduction is rarely successful.

Front: Definition of Ogilvie syndrome (acute colonic pseudo-obstruction)?
Back: Massive dilation of the colon (usually cecum and right colon) in the complete absence of any mechanical obstruction.

Front: Typical patient profile and triggers for Ogilvie syndrome?
Back: Elderly, bedridden patients hospitalized with severe systemic illness, trauma, major orthopedic surgery, or narcotic use.

Front: First-line pharmacotherapy for a patient with Ogilvie syndrome failing conservative measures with a cecum greater than 10 cm?
Back: Neostigmine (acetylcholinesterase inhibitor).

Front: Absolute contraindications to administering neostigmine for pseudo-obstruction?
Back: Active bradycardia, bronchospasm (asthma), or mechanical bowel obstruction.

Front: Next step in management for Ogilvie syndrome when neostigmine fails or is contraindicated?
Back: Endoscopic colonic decompression.

Comparative & Cross-Cutting Topics
Mechanical Obstruction vs. Paralytic Ileus
Front: Definition of a paralytic ileus?
Back: Non-mechanical, uniform adynamic failure of gastrointestinal motility.

Front: Key radiographic differentiator between mechanical obstruction and paralytic ileus?
Back: Mechanical shows localized dilation proximal to a transition point; ileus shows diffuse gas throughout both small and large bowel including the rectum.

Front: Most common causes of temporary postoperative paralytic ileus?
Back: Intraoperative bowel manipulation, narcotic use, hypokalemia, and localized peritoneal inflammation.

Front: Expected timeframes for normal physiological recovery of bowel motility post-laparotomy?
Back: Small bowel recovers within 24 hours, stomach within 48 hours, and colon within 3 to 5 days.

Intestinal Ischemia & Peritonitis
Front: Classic physical exam finding in early acute mesenteric ischemia?
Back: Severe abdominal pain out of proportion to physical exam findings.

Front: Definitive diagnostic imaging modality for suspected mesenteric ischemia?
Back: CT angiography (CTA) of the abdomen and pelvis.

Front: Define primary peritonitis (Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis)?
Back: Diffuse peritoneal infection without an evident intra-abdominal source of perforation, typically seen in cirrhotic patients with ascites.

Front: Diagnostic criteria for Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis on paracentesis?
Back: Polymorphonuclear leukocyte (PMN) count equal to or greater than 250 cells/mm³.

Front: Define secondary peritonitis?
Back: Peritoneal inflammation and infection resulting directly from perforation or rupture of an intra-abdominal hollow viscus.

Front: Primary surgical intervention for localized peritonitis versus generalized peritonitis?
Back: Localized can often be managed with targeted image-guided drainage; generalized requires emergency exploratory laparoscopy or laparotomy.

Internal & Rare Hernias
Front: Definition of an internal hernia?
Back: Protrusion of a viscus through a congenital or acquired mesenteric or peritoneal defect within the peritoneal cavity.

Front: Common surgical history predisposing a patient to internal hernias?
Back: Prior Roux-en-Y gastric bypass surgery creating a Petersen's space defect.

Front: Definition of a Petit hernia?
Back: A lumbar hernia occurring through the inferior lumbar triangle.

Front: Boundaries of the inferior lumbar triangle (Petit)?
Back: Iliac crest (inferior), latissimus dorsi (posterior), and external oblique muscle (anterior).

Front: Definition of a Grynfelt-Lesshaft hernia?
Back: A lumbar hernia occurring through the superior lumbar triangle.

Front: Definition of an obturator hernia?
Back: Herniation of bowel through the obturator canal, classically seen in elderly, emaciated females.

Front: Diagnostic sign associated with an obturator hernia?
Back: Howship-Romberg sign (pain in the medial thigh exacerbated by internal rotation of the hip due to obturator nerve compression).

Miscellaneous High-Yield Concepts
Front: Definition of Meckel's diverticulum rule of 2s?
Back: Occurs in 2% of the population, located 2 feet proximal to the ileocecal valve, 2 inches long, contains 2 types of ectopic tissue (gastric/pancreatic), presents before age 2.

Front: Most common clinical presentation of Meckel's diverticulum in children vs. adults?
Back: Painless lower GI bleeding in children (due to acid secretion from ectopic gastric mucosa causing ileal ulceration); bowel obstruction in adults.

Front: Definitive test for diagnosing Meckel's diverticulum in pediatric patients presenting with hematochezia?
Back: Technetium-99m pertechnetate scan (Meckel's scan).

Front: Pathophysiology of intussusception?
Back: Invagination or telescoping of one segment of bowel into an adjacent segment, leading to venous congestion and bowel ischemia.

Front: Classic physical examination findings and stool appearance in pediatric intussusception?
Back: Sausage-shaped mass in the right upper quadrant, emptiness in the RLQ (Dance sign), and "currant jelly" stools.

Front: Diagnostic and initial therapeutic modality for uncomplicated pediatric intussusception?
Back: Air or contrast pneumatic enema under fluoroscopic or ultrasound guidance.

Front: Primary cause of intussusception in adult populations?
Back: A pathological lead point, most commonly a primary bowel neoplasm (malignancy or polyp).

Surgical Anatomy & Technical Core
Inguinal Canal Anatomy Details
Front: Composition of the anterior wall of the inguinal canal?
Back: External oblique aponeurosis (and internal oblique muscle laterally).

Front: Composition of the posterior wall of the inguinal canal?
Back: Transversalis fascia and conjoint tendon medially.

Front: Composition of the roof of the inguinal canal?
Back: Arching fibers of the internal oblique and transversus abdominis muscles.

Front: Composition of the floor of the inguinal canal?
Back: Inguinal ligament (Poupart's) and lacunar ligament medially.

Front: The superficial inguinal ring is an opening in which structural layer?
Back: External oblique aponeurosis.

Front: The deep inguinal ring is an opening in which structural layer?
Back: Transversalis fascia.

Front: Structure running alongside the spermatic cord that is outside the internal spermatic fascia?
Back: Ilioinguinal nerve.

Tissue Layers & Coverings
Front: The three fascial layers derived from abdominal walls covering an indirect hernia?
Back: External spermatic fascia (external oblique), cremasteric fascia (internal oblique), and internal spermatic fascia (transversalis fascia).

Front: Which inguinal hernia type is covered by all three layers of spermatic fascia?
Back: Indirect inguinal hernia (because it travels inside the cord).

Front: Why does a direct inguinal hernia only possess external spermatic fascia covering?
Back: It punches directly through the posterior wall, bypassing the internal rings and cord structures.

Front: Pathological name for a persistent, fluid-filled patency of the processus vaginalis?
Back: Hydrocele.

Front: How to clinically differentiate a hydrocele from an incarcerated inguinal hernia in an infant?
Back: Hydroceles transilluminate completely with light, whereas hernias do not transilluminate.

Peritoneal Lines & Attachments
Front: Pathological structure that forms the band causing a Ladd's bands obstruction?
Back: Congenital peritoneal attachments running from an undescended cecum across the duodenum in malrotation.

Front: Surgical maneuver used to mobilize the duodenum and head of the pancreas during emergency laparotomy?
Back: Kocher maneuver.

Front: Surgical maneuver used to mobilize the right colon to access retroperitoneal vessels?
Back: Cattell-Braasch maneuver.

Front: Anatomical landmark used during surgery to differentiate small bowel from large bowel?
Back: Large bowel features teniae coli, haustra, and epiploic appendages; small bowel lacks these.

Front: Define the line of Toldt?
Back: The avascular lateral peritoneal reflection that must be incised to mobilize the colon.

Critical Care & Systemic Support
Fluid Management & Shock
Front: Initial fluid resuscitation fluid of choice for a patient presenting with an obstructing bowel or peritonitis?
Back: Isotonic crystalloids (Lactated Ringer's or Normal Saline).

Front: Why is Lactated Ringer's often preferred over Normal Saline for massive fluid resuscitation in surgical patients?
Back: Normal Saline can induce hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis when given in large volumes.

Front: Primary clinical indicator that a patient with a bowel obstruction is developing hypovolemic shock?
Back: Tachycardia, followed by orthostatic hypotension and delayed capillary refill.

Front: Define the urine output requirement for an adult patient in the immediate postoperative period?
Back: Minimum 0.5 mL/kg/hour.

Front: First line of intervention for a postoperative patient showing low urine output and tachycardia?
Back: Fluid challenge bolus with isotonic crystalloids, assuming normal cardiopulmonary function.

Prophylaxis & Pre-Op Optimization
Front: Why must patients with severe bowel obstructions remain strictly NPO?
Back: To prevent progressive luminal distension, limit vomiting, and reduce the risk of aspiration pneumonia during anesthesia.

Front: Standard timing for the administration of preoperative prophylactic antibiotics?
Back: Within 60 minutes prior to the surgical incision.

Front: Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) prophylaxis standard for patients undergoing major abdominal surgery?
Back: Sequential compression devices (SCDs) paired with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) or unfractionated heparin subcutaneously.

Front: When should mechanical DVT prophylaxis be applied?
Back: Prior to the induction of anesthesia.

Front: Standard intervention to correct a prolonged Prothrombin Time (PT/INR) prior to emergency abdominal surgery?
Back: Administration of Vitamin K or Prothrombin Complex Concentrate (PCC) / Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP).

Complication Identification
Front: Most common cause of a fever within the first 24 to 48 hours post-abdominal surgery?
Back: Atelectasis (or early cytokine release due to tissue trauma).

Front: Most common cause of a surgical site infection (SSI) appearing 5 to 7 days postoperatively?
Back: Skin flora contamination (Staphylococcus aureus) or enteric organisms (E. coli, B. fragilis) following dirty abdominal cases.

Front: Primary clinical sign of fascial dehiscence post-laparotomy?
Back: Sudden discharge of large volumes of pink, serosanguinous fluid from the abdominal wound.

Front: Immediate management of complete fascial dehiscence with eviscration?
Back: Cover exposed bowel with sterile, saline-moistened dressings, and transfer the patient immediately to the operating room.

Front: Definition of abdominal compartment syndrome?
Back: Sustained intra-abdominal pressure greater than 20 mmHg associated with new-onset organ dysfunction (e.g., decreased urine output, high airway pressures).

Extended Pathology Details
Appendicitis Variants
Front: Why does an appendicitis patient with a retrocecal appendix lack typical RLQ guarding?
Back: The inflamed appendix is shielded from the anterior parietal peritoneum by the overlying cecum.

Front: Clinical presentation of appendicitis during the third trimester of pregnancy?
Back: Right upper quadrant (RUQ) or flank pain, caused by the gravid uterus displacing the appendix superiorly.

Front: Why is the perforation rate of appendicitis significantly higher in preschool children?
Back: Underdeveloped greater omentum is unable to wall off the infection effectively.

Front: Classic sign of appendiceal perforation during acute appendicitis?
Back: Sudden, temporary relief of localized abdominal pain, rapidly followed by generalized peritonitis.

Front: Organism traditionally associated with appendiceal perforation and abscess formation?
Back: Bacteroides fragilis (and Escherichia coli).

Hernia Pitfalls
Front: How to clinically differentiate a femoral hernia from an inguinal hernia on physical examination?
Back: Femoral hernias emerge inferior and lateral to the pubic tubercle; inguinal hernias emerge superior and medial to it.

Front: Definition of a Littre hernia wall vulnerability?
Back: It can undergo isolated strangulation of the diverticulum without causing standard signs of mechanical bowel obstruction.

Front: Primary structural failure causing a direct inguinal hernia in older men?
Back: Age-related attenuation and weakening of the transversalis fascia within Hesselbach's triangle.

Front: Most common site for an incisional hernia to form?
Back: Midline vertical laparotomy incisions.

Front: Why are synthetic meshes avoided in the repair of groin hernias in pediatric patients?
Back: Mesh does not grow with the child and can disrupt normal developmental structures like the vas deferens.

Complex Obstruction Mechanics
Front: Pathophysiology of toxic megacolon?
Back: Severe, non-obstructive colonic dilation paired with systemic toxicity, typically complicating ulcerative colitis or C. difficile infection.

Front: Initial diagnostic test to evaluate for suspected free air from a perforated hollow viscus?
Back: Upright chest radiograph showing free air under the diaphragm (pneumoperitoneum).

Front: Alternative position to identify pneumoperitoneum if the patient cannot stand or sit upright?
Back: Left lateral decubitus abdominal X-ray (looking for air rising over the liver shadow).

Front: Definition of a "sentinel loop" on an abdominal radiograph?
Back: A localized, dilated loop of bowel near an inflamed intra-abdominal organ (e.g., seen in acute pancreatitis or appendicitis).

Front: Pathophysiology of Bouveret syndrome?
Back: A rare variant of gallstone ileus where a gallstone impacts directly within the duodenum, causing a gastric outlet obstruction.

Front: What does pneumatosis intestinalis signify on an abdominal CT scan?
Back: Gas within the bowel wall, indicating advanced intestinal ischemia and necrosis.

Front: Define portal venous gas on a CT scan?
Back: Gas within the portal venous system, a late and ominous sign of advanced mesenteric ischemia and bowel necrosis.

Front: Management of a patient with a partial small bowel obstruction whose symptoms do not resolve after 3 to 5 days of conservative management?
Back: Surgical exploration (laparoscopy or laparotomy) to lyse the obstructing adhesions.

Front: What CT finding rules out acute appendicitis?
Back: A gas-filled appendix with a total luminal diameter of less than 6 mm.

Front: Why can acute appendicitis present with microscopic hematuria or pyuria?
Back: An inflamed appendix resting directly on the ureter or bladder causes contiguous inflammation.

Front: What is the significance of an isolated elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) combined with normal WBC in suspected appendicitis?
Back: It has a high negative predictive value, making acute appendicitis highly unlikely.

Front: Which specific physical exam maneuver is most sensitive for an appendix located entirely within the true pelvis?
Back: Rectal examination eliciting severe tenderness on the right side.

Front: How does the presentation of appendicitis change in a patient with situs inversus totalis?
Back: The patient presents with acute left lower quadrant (LLQ) pain.

Complex Management Pathways
Front: What is the optimal time window to perform an interval appendectomy after successful non-operative management of a phlegmon?
Back: Six to eight weeks after the acute episode resolves.

Front: Why do some modern guidelines support avoiding interval appendectomy in asymptomatic adults over 40 after a conservative cure?
Back: The recurrence rate is low, though underlying neoplasm must be completely ruled out via colonoscopy.

Front: What must be done if a normal-appearing appendix is found intraoperatively during surgery for presumed acute appendicitis?
Back: Remove the appendix anyway (appendectomy) and thoroughly inspect the terminal ileum for Meckel's diverticulum or Crohn's disease.

Front: What is the management of a patient who develops a stump appendicitis?
Back: Completion appendectomy via laparoscopy or laparotomy.

Front: Define stump appendicitis?
Back: A rare recurrence of appendicitis due to inflammation of residual appendiceal tissue left behind from a previous incomplete appendectomy.

Front: If an appendiceal tumor is found to be a goblet cell adenocarcinoma, what is the mandatory next surgical step?
Back: Right hemicolectomy, regardless of the tumor size.

Hernias: Technical Subtleties & Advanced Variants
Groin Anatomy & Alternative Repairs
Front: What constitutes the "MPO" (Myopectineal Orifice) of Fruchaud?
Back: The distinct, weak anatomical zone of the lower abdominal wall where all groin hernias (indirect, direct, femoral) originate.

Front: Which anatomical landmark separates a direct inguinal hernia from a femoral hernia?
Back: The inguinal ligament (direct is superior to it, femoral is inferior to it).

Front: What structure forms the posterior boundary of the preperitoneal space during laparoscopic hernia repairs?
Back: The parietal peritoneum.

Front: Define a Cooper's ligament repair (McVay repair)?
Back: A tissue-based open hernia repair where the transversalis fascia is sutured directly to Cooper's ligament, ideal for femoral hernias.

Front: Why is a femoral hernia repair considered an absolute surgical priority even if completely asymptomatic?
Back: It carries an exceptionally high 20% to 40% risk of incarceration and strangulation within the first year of diagnosis.

Incisional & Ventral Hernia Nuances
Front: Define a component separation technique in ventral hernia repair?
Back: A structural surgical method where the abdominal wall muscle layers are dissected and translated medially to close massive fascial defects without tension.

Front: What is the major long-term risk of repairing a large ventral hernia using a synthetic mesh placed in direct contact with the viscera?
Back: Fistula formation between the prosthetic mesh and the underlying loops of bowel.

Front: What is an "underlay" or "sublay" mesh placement?
Back: Placing the hernia mesh deep to the rectus abdominis muscle or fascia, which uses natural intra-abdominal pressure to keep the mesh secured.

Front: What is an "onlay" mesh placement?
Back: Securing the hernia mesh superficial to the anterior rectus sheath, which carries a higher risk of postoperative seroma and infection.

Rare Presentation Details
Front: What is a Grynfeltt-Lesshaft hernia?
Back: A rare lumbar hernia emerging specifically through the superior lumbar triangle.

Front: What are the boundaries of the superior lumbar triangle (Grynfeltt)?
Back: Twelfth rib (superior), erector spinae muscle (medial), and internal oblique muscle (lateral).

Front: What is a sciatic hernia?
Back: A rare hernia where pelvic contents protrude through the greater or lesser sciatic foramen.

Front: How does an obturator hernia characteristically cause knee pain?
Back: Compression of the obturator nerve causes referred sensory pain along the medial thigh down to the knee (Howship-Romberg sign).

Small Bowel Obstruction (SBO): Advanced Dynamics
Pathophysiological Progressions
Front: What is the primary fluid shifts consequence of a prolonged small bowel obstruction?
Back: Intravascular volume depletion due to massive third-spacing of fluid into the bowel lumen and peritoneal cavity.

Front: Why does systemic hypovolemia occur rapidly in a proximal (high) small bowel obstruction compared to a distal one?
Back: Proximal obstructions trigger early, persistent vomiting, preventing any mucosal reabsorption of gastric, pancreatic, and biliary secretions.

Front: What is the clinical significance of a patient losing the "string of beads" sign and developing a completely fluid-filled bowel loop on X-ray?
Back: It suggests a high risk of closed-loop obstruction or a late-stage mechanical block with failing peristalsis.

Front: Define the "small bowel feces sign" on a abdominal CT scan?
Back: The presence of particulate material mixed with gas bubbles inside the small bowel, indicating chronic, low-grade, or subacute SBO.

Gastric & Intestinal Motility Traps
Front: What is the classic water-soluble contrast challenge (Gastrografin challenge) used for in SBO?
Back: To predict the success of non-operative management; if contrast reaches the colon within 24 hours on X-ray, the SBO will likely resolve safely without surgery.

Front: Aside from diagnostic utility, what therapeutic benefit does Gastrografin offer in partial SBO?
Back: Its high osmolarity draws water into the bowel lumen, reducing wall edema and stimulating regular peristalsis.

Front: What is the maximal safe duration for conservative trial management of an uncomplicated adhesive partial SBO?
Back: 48 to 72 hours; continuing past this without objective clinical improvement increases the risk of unrecognized ischemia.

Front: Define Superior Mesenteric Artery (SMA) syndrome (Wilkie's syndrome)?
Back: Compression of the third portion of the duodenum between the abdominal aorta and the overriding SMA, causing proximal bowel obstruction.

Front: What classic patient demographic develops SMA syndrome?
Back: Individuals who have experienced rapid, massive weight loss (e.g., from anorexia, burns, or prolonged bed rest), losing the protective retroperitoneal fat pad.

Large Bowel Obstruction (LBO): Critical Care & Oncology
Ischemic Profiles & Malignancy Management
Front: Why is an emergency loop transverse colostomy preferred over an immediate resection during a left-sided malignant LBO with massive fecal loading?
Back: It provides instant, life-saving decompression of the colon without risking an immediate breakdown of a primary anastomosis in an unprepared, contaminated field.

Front: Define the Hartmann's procedure?
Back: Resection of the diseased rectosigmoid colon, creation of a temporary proximal descending/sigmoid colostomy, and stapling/sewing off of the distal rectal stump.

Front: What is the preferred definitive surgical intervention for a resectable, obstructing carcinoma located at the splenic flexure?
Back: Extended right hemicolectomy or subtotal colectomy with primary ileosigmoid anastomosis.

Front: What is the primary utility of self-expanding metallic stents (SEMS) in large bowel obstructions?
Back: It acts as a non-surgical bridge to convert an emergency case into an elective, one-stage oncological surgery, or serves as final palliation for inoperable tumors.

Volvulus Advanced Mechanics
Front: What underlying anatomical variation is absolutely required for a sigmoid volvulus to occur?
Back: A redundant, elongated sigmoid colon attached to a narrow, hyper-mobile mesenteric base.

Front: What underlying anatomical variation is absolutely required for a cecal volvulus to occur?
Back: Incomplete embryological fixation of the ascending colon and cecum to the posterior parietal peritoneum.

Front: Why is endoscopic detorsion strictly contraindicated if a sigmoid volvulus patient displays bloody rectal discharge or peritoneal signs?
Back: These findings indicate frank bowel necrosis or perforation; instrumentation risks immediate catastrophic free rupture.

Front: What is a cecal bascule?
Back: A rare variant of cecal volvulus where the cecum folds anteriorly and superiorly over the ascending colon without an axial twist along the mesentery.

Cross-Cutting Concepts: Post-Op Care, Diagnostics & Complications
Fluid Mechanics & Shock Differentiations
Front: Why does metabolic acidosis develop late in a strangulated bowel obstruction?
Back: Anaerobic metabolism within ischemic tissues generates excess lactic acid, which spills into the systemic circulation.

Front: What physiological state is suggested by a high central venous pressure (CVP) coupled with low urine output in a postoperative abdominal surgery patient?
Back: Acute kidney injury (AKI) or fluid overload/cardiac dysfunction rather than simple volume depletion.

Front: What is the preferred method of nutritional support for a patient facing a prolonged bowel obstruction lasting more than 7 to 10 days?
Back: Total Parenteral Nutrition (TPN) via a dedicated central venous line.

Front: Why must TPN be initiated slowly and carefully in chronically malnourished surgical patients?
Back: To avoid refeeding syndrome, which causes dangerous intracellular shifts of phosphate, potassium, and magnesium.

Critical Abdominal Complications
Front: What is the normal physiological range for intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) in a critically ill, supine adult?
Back: Five to seven millimeters of mercury (5-7 mmHg).

Front: At what specific bladder pressure threshold must active medical decompression be initiated for intra-abdominal hypertension?
Back: Twelve millimeters of mercury (12 mmHg).

Front: What is the ultimate definitive treatment for refractory Abdominal Compartment Syndrome (ACS)?
Back: Decompressive laparotomy, leaving the abdomen completely open with a temporary vacuum closure system.

Front: How does Abdominal Compartment Syndrome directly impair cardiac output?
Back: Elevated pressure compresses the inferior vena cava, drastically reducing venous return (preload) to the heart.

Front: How does Abdominal Compartment Syndrome affect mechanical ventilation parameters?
Back: Diaphragmatic elevation increases peak airway pressures, requiring higher ventilatory adjustments to prevent hypoxia.

Peritonitis & Post-Surgical Intricacies
Front: What is the definitive management of an asymptomatic, localized 3 cm intra-abdominal fluid collection discovered on CT 6 days post-appendectomy?
Back: Close observation and oral antibiotics; it does not require invasive percutaneous drainage unless it becomes larger or symptomatic.

Front: What is the classical antibiotic regimen used to provide comprehensive empiric coverage for secondary peritonitis from a lower GI tract perforation?
Back: Ceftriaxone combined with Metronidazole, or single-agent Piperacillin-Tazobactam.

Front: Which specific organism must be accounted for when choosing antibiotics for a pelvic abscess following a dirty colorectal surgery?
Back: Bacteroides fragilis (obligate anaerobe).

Front: What is the significance of "free air" seen on a CT scan or X-ray 48 hours after an uncomplicated open laparotomy?
Back: It is a normal expected physiological finding; residual postoperative pneumoperitoneum can take up to 5 to 7 days to fully resorb.

Front: What physical exam finding differentiates a deep fascial dehiscence from a superficial wound infection?
Back: Fascial dehiscence demonstrates an intact skin layer but a palpable, deep fascial defect, often producing massive serosanguinous fluid output.

Front: What is the Aaron sign?
Back: Pain or pressure felt in the epigastrium or precordium when continuous pressure is applied over McBurney’s point.

Front: What is the Dunphy sign?
Back: An increase in right lower quadrant abdominal pain triggered specifically by coughing.

Front: What is the Ten Horn sign?
Back: Pain caused by applying gentle traction on the right spermatic cord.

Front: What pathological finding defines a "gangrenous appendicitis" during histopathological evaluation?
Back: Microscopic transmural necrosis of the appendiceal wall with localized microperforations.

Front: What unusual anatomic location can an appendix mimic if the patient has an exceptionally long cecum or incomplete intestinal rotation?
Back: Left lower quadrant appendicitis or right upper quadrant cholecystitis.

Incisional and Access Choices
Front: What is a Rocky-Davis incision?
Back: A muscle-splitting, transverse incision made in the right lower quadrant, located slightly more horizontal than a classic McBurney incision.

Front: What major abdominal wall muscle layers are split, rather than transected, during an open McBurney appendectomy?
Back: External oblique aponeurosis, internal oblique muscle, and transversus abdominis muscle.

Front: What structure must be safely isolated and doubly ligated during an appendectomy to avoid significant retrocecal hemorrhage?
Back: The appendiceal artery, running within the mesoappendix.

Front: From which larger blood vessel is the appendiceal artery directly derived?
Back: The ileocolic artery.

Hernias: Anatomical Spaces & Specialized Procedures
Advanced Spaces and Repair Terminology
Front: What is the "Triangle of Doom" in laparoscopic inguinal hernia repairs?
Back: An anatomical space bounded by the vas deferens medially and spermatic vessels laterally, containing the external iliac vessels.

Front: What is the "Triangle of Pain" in laparoscopic inguinal hernia repairs?
Back: An anatomical space bounded by the spermatic vessels medially and the iliopubic tract laterally, containing major nerves.

Front: Which specific nerves run within the laparoscopic Triangle of Pain?
Back: Femoral branch of the genitofemoral nerve, lateral femoral cutaneous nerve, and the femoral nerve.

Front: Why is stapling or tacking a hernia mesh strictly forbidden within the Triangle of Pain?
Back: It carries a high risk of causing permanent nerve entrapment, chronic postoperative pain, or paresthesias.

Front: Why is stapling or tacking a hernia mesh strictly forbidden within the Triangle of Doom?
Back: It can cause catastrophic, life-threatening hemorrhage by lacerating the external iliac artery or vein.

Front: Define a Shouldice hernia repair?
Back: A traditional, non-mesh, multi-layered imbricated suture repair of the posterior inguinal canal wall using continuous running sutures.

Front: Define a Bassini hernia repair?
Back: A tissue-based repair where the conjoined tendon (internal oblique and transversus abdominis) is sutured directly to the inguinal ligament.

Uncommon Inguinal Hernia Mechanics
Front: What is a "sliding" inguinal hernia most commonly composed of in female infants?
Back: The fallopian tube or ovary sliding down and forming part of the posterior wall of the hernia sac.

Front: What is a "sliding" inguinal hernia most commonly composed of on the right side in adults?
Back: The cecum.

Front: What is a "sliding" inguinal hernia most commonly composed of on the left side in adults?
Back: The sigmoid colon.

Small Bowel Obstruction (SBO): Special Circumstances
Uncommon Intestinal Obstruction Variants
Front: Define a Maydl hernia?
Back: A rare hernia containing two distinct loops of small bowel within a single sac, leaving the intervening loop intra-abdominal and highly prone to isolated strangulation.

Front: Define an intraluminal gallstone ileus mass location?
Back: The terminal ileum, because it possesses the narrowest internal lumen diameter of the entire small bowel.

Front: What is the primary cause of small bowel obstruction in a patient with a history of Roux-en-Y gastric bypass presenting years after their weight loss surgery?
Back: Internal hernia through a mesenteric defect (such as Petersen’s space).

Front: What classic plain-film X-ray finding is highly suggestive of a midgut volvulus in an infant or young child?
Back: A "corkscrew" appearance of the distal duodenum and jejunum.

Front: What is the definitive emergency surgical management for pediatric midgut volvulus?
Back: Ladd’s procedure (counterclockwise detorsion, division of Ladd’s bands, widening of the mesenteric base, and placing the small bowel on the right and colon on the left).

Large Bowel Obstruction (LBO): Advanced Pathological Mechanics
Pathological Subtleties of the Colon
Front: What is the primary risk of attempting endoscopic stenting (SEMS) on an obstructing colon cancer located in the low rectum?
Back: High rates of severe tenesmus, rectal pain, incontinence, and structural stent migration.

Front: Why is a cecal volvulus less responsive to endoscopic detorsion compared to a sigmoid volvulus?
Back: Cecal volvulus involves a hyper-mobile, poorly fixed right colon that tends to quickly re-twist or rapidly progress to ischemic necrosis.

Front: What distinct physical finding indicates that an obstructing tumor of the descending colon has caused complete competence failure of the ileocecal valve?
Back: SBO symptoms (early-onset vomiting and extensive small bowel dilation on X-ray) alongside colonic distension.

Front: Define a colonic stricture due to chronic diverticulitis?
Back: A fibrotic, non-malignant narrowing of the colonic lumen resulting from recurrent, healed episodes of transmural diverticular inflammation.

Front: How do you definitively differentiate a benign diverticular stricture from an obstructing colon cancer on a diagnostic imaging workup?
Back: Direct visualization and structural biopsy via flexible sigmoidoscopy or colonoscopy.

Cross-Cutting Surgical Management Protocols
Perioperative Electrolytes & Advanced Monitoring
Front: What is the physiological effect of severe uncorrected hypokalemia on a mechanical small bowel obstruction?
Back: It paralyzes any remaining working smooth muscle, converting a partial mechanical obstruction into a complete adynamic ileus.

Front: What is the target intra-abdominal pressure range when establishing a pneumoperitoneum with carbon dioxide gas during a laparoscopic appendectomy?
Back: Twelve to fifteen millimeters of mercury (12-15 mmHg).

Front: Why is carbon dioxide gas universally chosen to establish a pneumoperitoneum in modern abdominal surgeries?
Back: It is highly soluble in blood, non-combustible, and easily exhaled via respiration if absorbed across the peritoneum.

Front: What metabolic state can develop if massive amounts of carbon dioxide are absorbed systemically during a prolonged laparoscopic operation?
Back: Respiratory acidosis.

Front: How does an anesthesia team compensate for the systematic absorption of CO2 during laparoscopic abdominal surgery?
Back: By increasing the mechanical ventilator's minute ventilation (increasing respiratory rate or tidal volume).

Section:  General Surgery

Topic: Hepatobiliary — cholecystitis, liver abscess, biliary disease


Front: What structural anatomy defines the borders of the Triangle of Calot?
Back: The cystic duct laterally, the common hepatic duct medially, and the inferior edge of the liver superiorly.

Front: What vital structure runs directly through the Triangle of Calot?
Back: The cystic artery.

Front: What is the most common source of the cystic artery?
Back: The right hepatic artery.

Front: What structure protects the common bile duct from ischemic stricture during dissection?
Back: The 3 o'clock and 9 o'clock marginal arteries running longitudinally along its walls.

Front: What primary component makes up the majority of gallstones in Western countries?
Back: Cholesterol.

Front: What are the four classic risk factors ("4 Fs") for cholesterol gallstones?
Back: Female, Fat, Fertile (multiparity), and Forty.

Front: What is the primary cause of black pigment gallstones?
Back: Chronic hemolysis or cirrhosis, leading to increased unconjugated bilirubin.

Front: Where do brown pigment gallstones typically form and what triggers them?
Back: They form primarily in the bile ducts due to biliary stasis and bacterial infection.

Front: What hormone causes gallbladder contraction and relaxation of the Sphincter of Oddi?
Back: Cholecystokinin (CCK), released by duodenal I-cells in response to fat and protein.

Front: What is the normal maximum diameter of the common bile duct on ultrasound in a patient under 60?
Back: Six millimeters, with an additional one millimeter allowed per decade of life after 60.

Part 2: Asymptomatic Cholelithiasis & Biliary Colic
Front: What is the standard management for an asymptomatic patient with incidentally discovered gallstones?
Back: Expectant management and reassurance.

Front: Name three absolute indications for prophylactic cholecystectomy in asymptomatic cholelithiasis.
Back: Porcelain gallbladder, stones larger than three centimeters, or patients undergoing bariatric surgery or transplant.

Front: What pathognomonic mechanism causes the pain of biliary colic?
Back: Transient obstruction of the gallbladder neck or cystic duct by a gallstone during contraction.

Front: What is the classic presentation and timing of biliary colic pain?
Back: Dull, aching right upper quadrant pain radiating to the right scapula, typically peaking within one hour after fatty meals.

Front: What is the diagnostic test of choice for suspected biliary colic?
Back: Right upper quadrant transabdominal ultrasound.

Front: What are the expected liver function tests and leukocyte counts in uncomplicated biliary colic?
Back: Completely normal liver function tests and normal white blood cell counts.

Front: What is the definitive treatment for recurrent, disabling biliary colic?
Back: Elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

Part 3: Acute Cholecystitis (Calculous & Acalculous)
Front: What primary physiological process initiates acute calculous cholecystitis?
Back: Persistent, complete obstruction of the cystic duct leading to gallbladder distension, ischemia, and chemical inflammation.

Front: What clinical sign is characterized by abrupt cessation of inspiration during deep palpation of the right upper quadrant?
Back: Murphy's sign.

Front: Name the three classic sonographic findings that diagnostic criteria require for acute cholecystitis.
Back: Gallstones, gallbladder wall thickening greater than three millimeters, and pericolecystic fluid.

Front: What is the next diagnostic step if ultrasound is equivocal but clinical suspicion for acute cholecystitis remains high?
Back: Hepatobiliary Iminodiacetic Acid (HIDA) scan.

Front: What constitutes a positive HIDA scan for acute cholecystitis?
Back: Non-visualization of the gallbladder after four hours, indicating cystic duct occlusion.

Front: What are the three most common bacterial organisms isolated from the gallbladder in acute cholecystitis?
Back: Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Enterococcus faecalis.

Front: What is the preferred timing for cholecystectomy in a patient admitted with acute uncomplicated cholecystitis?
Back: Early laparoscopic cholecystectomy, ideally within 72 hours of admission.

Front: What defines acute acalculous cholecystitis?
Back: Gallbladder inflammation and ischemia occurring in the absence of mechanical gallstone obstruction.

Front: In what patient population does acute acalculous cholecystitis typically present?
Back: Critically ill, severely burned, traumatized, or prolonged total parenteral nutrition (TPN) patients in the intensive care unit.

Front: What is the immediate treatment of choice for a critically ill patient with acute acalculous cholecystitis who cannot tolerate general anesthesia?
Back: Percutaneous transhepatic cholecystostomy tube placement.

Front: What is emphysematous cholecystitis?
Back: A severe variant of acute cholecystitis caused by gas-forming organisms like Clostridium perfringens.

Front: What pathognomonic finding on plain abdominal X-ray or CT confirms emphysematous cholecystitis?
Back: Air inside the gallbladder wall or lumen.

Front: What is the definitive management strategy for emphysematous cholecystitis?
Back: Emergent cholecystectomy combined with broad-spectrum anaerobic antibiotics due to high gangrene risk.

Front: What condition describes chronic cholecystitis with extensive intramural calcification of the gallbladder wall?
Back: Porcelain gallbladder.

Front: Why does a porcelain gallbladder necessitate mandatory surgical resection?
Back: It carries a highly significant association with the development of gallbladder carcinoma.

Front: Define Mirizzi syndrome.
Back: Extrinsic compression of the common hepatic duct caused by a large gallstone impacted in the cystic duct or Hartmann's pouch.

Part 4: Choledocholithiasis & Gallstone Ileus
Front: Define choledocholithiasis.
Back: The presence of one or more gallstones within the common bile duct.

Front: What distinguishes the pain of choledocholithiasis from simple biliary colic?
Back: The pain of choledocholithiasis is prolonged, persistent, and accompanied by clinical jaundice.

Front: What specific pattern of liver function test elevation characterizes choledocholithiasis?
Back: A cholestatic pattern, featuring marked elevation of alkaline phosphatase and direct bilirubin out of proportion to AST and ALT.

Front: What is the initial imaging study performed for suspected choledocholithiasis?
Back: Right upper quadrant transabdominal ultrasound.

Front: What secondary sign on ultrasound highly suggests the presence of a stone in the common bile duct?
Back: Dilation of the common bile duct greater than six millimeters.

Front: What non-invasive imaging modality offers the highest sensitivity for mapping common bile duct stones without therapeutic capability?
Back: Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP).

Front: What is the preferred gold-standard therapeutic intervention to clear a common bile duct stone?
Back: Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with endoscopic sphincterotomy.

Front: What mandatory subsequent procedure must follow a successful ERCP clearance of choledocholithiasis?
Back: Laparoscopic cholecystectomy during the same hospital admission to prevent recurrent biliary events.

Front: Define gallstone ileus.
Back: Mechanical small bowel obstruction caused by a large gallstone impaction, usually at the ileocecal valve.

Front: Through what anatomical anomaly does a gallstone pass to cause a gallstone ileus?
Back: A cholecystenteric fistula, most commonly forming between the inflamed gallbladder and the duodenum.

Front: Name the components of Rigler's triad seen on an abdominal radiograph.
Back: Pneumobilia (air in the biliary tree), mechanical small bowel obstruction, and an ectopic radiopaque gallstone.

Front: What is the primary, immediate surgical goal when treating a patient with acute gallstone ileus?
Back: An enterolithotomy to extract the obstructing stone through a proximal enterotomy.

Front: Should the cholecystenteric fistula be routinely repaired during an emergency enterolithotomy for gallstone ileus?
Back: No, the fistula is left alone during the acute setting to minimize operative time and complications in an unstable patient.

Part 5: Acute Cholangitis
Front: What two distinct pathophysiological factors must coexist to produce acute cholangitis?
Back: Biliary stasis/obstruction and bacterial contamination of the biliary tree.

Front: What clinical triad describes the classic presentation of acute cholangitis?
Back: Charcot's triad.

Front: Name the components of Charcot's triad.
Back: Right upper quadrant abdominal pain, fever/chills, and jaundice.

Front: What clinical pentad signifies severe, life-threatening suppurative cholangitis?
Back: Reynolds' pentad.

Front: Name the components of Reynolds' pentad.
Back: Charcot's triad plus hypotension (shock) and altered mental status.

Front: What is the most critical initial step in managing an unstable patient with acute cholangitis?
Back: Aggressive fluid resuscitation and immediate administration of broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics.

Front: What is the definitive emergency intervention required if a patient with acute cholangitis fails to respond to initial medical stabilization?
Back: Urgent biliary decompression, most commonly achieved via therapeutic ERCP.

Part 6: Pyogenic & Amebic Liver Abscesses
Front: What is the most common route of bacterial entry resulting in a pyogenic liver abscess?
Back: Ascending biliary tract infection, such as cholangitis secondary to stone obstruction.

Front: Name the second most common route of infection causing pyogenic liver abscesses, frequently tied to appendicitis or diverticulitis.
Back: Portal vein pylephlebitis draining an intra-abdominal infectious focus.

Front: What bacterial species is the most common organism isolated from pyogenic liver abscesses globally?
Back: Escherichia coli.

Front: What bacterial species is a major cause of cryptogenic pyogenic liver abscesses, particularly in patients with diabetes mellitus or of Asian descent?
Back: Klebsiella pneumoniae.

Front: What are the primary clinical signs and symptoms of a pyogenic liver abscess?
Back: High swinging fevers, right upper quadrant pain, leukocytosis, and tender hepatomegaly.

Front: What is the imaging modality of choice to diagnose and characterize a suspected liver abscess?
Back: Contrast-enhanced Computed Tomography (CT) of the abdomen.

Front: What is the standard management protocol for a pyogenic liver abscess larger than three to five centimeters?
Back: Intravenous antibiotics combined with percutaneous CT- or ultrasound-guided catheter drainage.

Front: Under what specific clinical circumstance does a pyogenic liver abscess require open surgical drainage?
Back: When the abscess is multiloculated, ruptures into the peritoneum, or when percutaneous drainage fails.

Front: What specific organism is the causative agent of an amebic liver abscess?
Back: Entamoeba histolytica.

Front: How do trophozoites of Entamoeba histolytica reach the hepatic parenchyma from the gut?
Back: They invade the colonic mucosa and travel through the portal venous system.

Front: What is the classic gross appearance of the fluid aspirated from an amebic liver abscess?
Back: Anchovy paste or chocolate sauce appearance, consisting of necrotic hepatocytes.

Front: What non-invasive diagnostic test is preferred over aspiration to confirm an amebic liver abscess?
Back: Serum amebic serology (EIA/ELISA) combined with fluid imaging.

Front: What is the primary first-line medical treatment for an uncomplicated amebic liver abscess?
Back: Oral or intravenous Metronidazole, followed by a luminal amebicide like paromomycin.

Front: When is percutaneous drainage indicated for an amebic liver abscess?
Back: Only when there is an imminent risk of rupture, suspicion of secondary bacterial infection, or a large abscess in the left lobe.

Part 7: Hydatid Liver Disease
Front: What organism is responsible for hydatid cyst disease of the liver?
Back: Echinococcus granulosus.

Front: What is the primary definitive host and transmission vector for Echinococcus granulosus?
Back: Canines (dogs), via the ingestion of food contaminated with fecal ova.

Front: What characteristic finding is seen on a CT scan of a patient with a hydatid liver cyst?
Back: A well-defined cyst wall with internal septations showing a "cyst-within-a-cyst" or "daughter cyst" pattern.

Front: What catastrophic complication can occur if a hydatid cyst accidentally ruptures or leaks during intervention?
Back: Severe anaphylactic shock due to systemic exposure to highly antigenic hydatid fluid.

Front: What medical therapy must always accompany surgical intervention for hydatid cysts to prevent recurrence?
Back: Perisurgical treatment with anthelminthic medications, specifically Albendazole.

Front: What does the acronym PAIR stand for in the minimally invasive treatment of hydatid cysts?
Back: Puncture, Aspiration, Injection (of scolicidal agent like hypertonic saline), and Re-aspiration.

Part 8: Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis & Primary Biliary Cholangitis
Front: Define Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis (PSC).
Back: A chronic cholestatic disease characterized by progressive fibrosing inflammation and destruction of both intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts.

Front: With what specific gastrointestinal disease process is Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis most strongly associated?
Back: Inflammatory bowel disease, specifically Ulcerative Colitis in roughly seventy to eighty percent of cases.

Front: What pathognomonic appearance does an ERCP or MRCP demonstrate in a patient with PSC?
Back: A "beaded" appearance of the bile ducts caused by alternating segments of stricture and dilation.

Front: What diagnostic liver biopsy finding is classically described in cases of Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis?
Back: Periductal "onion-skin" concentric fibrosis.

Front: Patients diagnosed with Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis carry a heavily increased lifetime risk for developing which deadly malignancy?
Back: Cholangiocarcinoma.

Front: What is the definitive, curative treatment for end-stage liver failure secondary to Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis?
Back: Orthotopic liver transplantation.

Front: Define Primary Biliary Cholangitis (PBC).
Back: An autoimmune destruction of the small, intrahepatic interlobular bile ducts, primarily affecting middle-aged females.

Front: What serum autoantibody is highly specific and diagnostic for Primary Biliary Cholangitis?
Back: Anti-Mitochondrial Antibody (AMA).

Front: What medical therapy is used to slow disease progression and improve liver chemistry in patients with PBC?
Back: Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA).

Part 9: Biliary Neoplasms & Gallbladder Malignancy
Front: What is the most common histological subtype of gallbladder cancer?
Back: Adenocarcinoma.

Front: Name three major risk factors linked to the development of gallbladder adenocarcinoma.
Back: Gallstones greater than three centimeters, porcelain gallbladder, and chronic Salmonella typhi carrier status.

Front: Why does gallbladder cancer carry a notoriously poor overall prognosis?
Back: It is clinically silent in early stages, leading to late presentations with direct local invasion into adjacent segments IV and V of the liver.

Front: What surgical management is appropriate for gallbladder cancer limited strictly to the muscularis layer (Stage T1a) found incidentally on pathology?
Back: Simple cholecystectomy alone is curative and sufficient.

Front: What extended surgical procedure is required for gallbladder cancer that invades past the muscularis layer (Stage T1b or T2)?
Back: Radical cholecystectomy, which includes a wedge resection of liver segments IVb and V along with a regional lymphadenectomy.

Front: What is a Klatskin tumor?
Back: A cholangiocarcinoma located precisely at the confluence of the right and left hepatic ducts.

Front: How does a patient with a Klatskin tumor characteristically present clinically?
Back: With painless, progressive jaundice, profound weight loss, and Courvoisier's sign.

Front: Define Courvoisier's sign.
Back: A palpably enlarged, non-tender gallbladder in a jaundiced patient, strongly suggesting malignant distal biliary obstruction.

Part 10: Surgical Intraoperative Complications & Post-Operative Management
Front: What is the most critical step a surgeon must take to establish the "Critical View of Safety" during laparoscopic cholecystectomy?
Back: Clear the fat from Calot's triangle until only two structures—the cystic duct and the cystic artery—are seen entering the gallbladder.

Front: What immediate step should a surgeon take if anatomical structures are obscured by massive inflammation during a laparoscopic cholecystectomy?
Back: Convert to an open cholecystectomy or perform a subtotal fenestrating cholecystectomy.

Front: A patient presents with persistent abdominal pain, fever, and bilious drain output three days after a laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Diagnosis?
Back: An iatrogenic bile duct leak or major bile duct injury.

Front: What initial diagnostic test should be ordered to evaluate for a suspected post-operative bile leak or fluid collection?
Back: An ultrasound or CT scan of the abdomen to look for a subhepatic fluid collection (biloma).

Front: If a post-operative biloma is confirmed and the patient is stable, what is the best next diagnostic and therapeutic step?
Back: ERCP to define the leak site and place a temporary biliary stent to lower intraductal pressure and allow healing.

Front: What is post-cholecystectomy syndrome?
Back: Persistent or recurrent right upper quadrant abdominal symptoms weeks to months following gallbladder removal.

Front: Name two common structural or mechanical causes of post-cholecystectomy syndrome.
Back: A retained common bile duct stone or biliary stricture.

Part 11: Trauma, Biliary Atresia, and Choledochal Cysts
Front: According to ATLS guidelines, what injury must be suspected when a blunt abdominal trauma patient presents with isolated, delayed right upper quadrant pain and bile peritonitis?
Back: Traumatic avulsion or rupture of the extrahepatic biliary tree or gallbladder.

Front: What classic triad characterizes Hemobilia (bleeding into the biliary tree) following liver trauma or biopsy?
Back: Right upper quadrant pain, jaundice, and upper gastrointestinal bleeding (melena or hematemesis).

Front: What is the diagnostic test of choice to confirm and treat severe hemobilia?
Back: Hepatic angiography with selective transcatheter arterial embolization.

Front: What congenital condition is characterized by progressive idiopathic fibro-obliteration of the extrahepatic biliary tree in neonates?
Back: Biliary atresia.

Front: What is the definitive first-line surgical intervention for a newborn diagnosed with biliary atresia?
Back: The Kasai procedure (hepatoportoenterostomy).

Front: Within what crucial timeframe must a Kasai procedure be performed to maximize success?
Back: Before the infant reaches 60 days of life, before irreversible cirrhosis develops.

Front: What classification system is used to categorize congenital cystic dilations of the biliary tree?
Back: The Todani classification system.

Front: What is the most common type of choledochal cyst according to the Todani classification?
Back: Type I cyst, which features fusiform dilation of the common bile duct.

Front: Why must all choledochal cysts undergo complete surgical excision rather than simple observation?
Back: They carry an extremely high lifelong risk of malignant transformation into cholangiocarcinoma.

Front: What reconstruction is required after the complete excision of a Type I choledochal cyst?
Back: A Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy to restore biliary-enteric continuity.

Part 12: High-Yield Rapid Assessment Scenarios
Front: Jaundiced patient with palpable gallbladder and weight loss. Most likely diagnosis?
Back: Pancreatic head adenocarcinoma or distal cholangiocarcinoma.

Front: Severe right upper quadrant pain, fever, jaundice, hypotension, and confusion. What is the immediate treatment priority?
Back: Intravenous fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and urgent biliary decompression via ERCP.

Front: Post-operative day two after cholecystectomy with low-grade fever and mild jaundice. Retained stone suspected. Next step?
Back: Obtain an ultrasound to evaluate common bile duct diameter and perform ERCP if a stone is visualized.

Front: High fever, leukocytosis, and tender hepatomegaly two weeks after recovering from acute diverticulitis. Diagnosis?
Back: Pyogenic liver abscess via portal vein pylephlebitis.

Front: Patient with a history of immigration from Mexico presents with a single right-lobe liver cyst. Serology is positive for amebiasis. First-line therapy?
Back: Metronidazole therapy alone.

Front: Incidentally found liver cyst on ultrasound showing a thick wall and internal septations. Patient raises sheep. Diagnosis?
Back: Hydatid cyst disease caused by Echinococcus granulosus.

Front: Forty-year-old female with intense pruritus, fatigue, and highly elevated alkaline phosphatase. AMA positive. Diagnosis?
Back: Primary Biliary Cholangitis.

Front: Young male with long-standing ulcerative colitis presents with worsening jaundice and pruritus. MRCP shows a beaded biliary pattern. Diagnosis?
Back: Primary Sclerosing Cholangitis.

Front: Elderly female presents with small bowel obstruction. X-ray shows gas in the biliary tree. Diagnosis?
Back: Gallstone ileus.

Front: What is the exact source of gas in the biliary tree in a patient with gallstone ileus?
Back: Retrograde flow of air from the duodenum through a cholecystoduodenal fistula.

Part 13: Technical Pearl & Management Nuances
Front: What specific layer of the gallbladder wall lacks a submucosa, making it distinct from the rest of the gastrointestinal tract?
Back: The lamina propria interfaces directly with the muscularis layer, facilitating rapid local tumor invasion.

Front: What structures are at risk if a surgeon applies blind electrocautery to control hemorrhage in the Triangle of Calot?
Back: The right hepatic artery and the common hepatic duct.

Front: What is the management of choice for a high-grade common bile duct stricture secondary to long-standing chronic pancreatitis?
Back: Surgical bypass via a choledochoduodenostomy or Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy.

Front: What symptom is the earliest and most sensitive indicator of successful biliary decompression in a jaundiced patient?
Back: Resolution or significant reduction of generalized pruritus.

Front: Why is an intraoperative cholangiogram (IOC) performed during a laparoscopic cholecystectomy?
Back: To delineate biliary anatomy clearly and screen for asymptomatic retained common bile duct stones.

Front: What finding on an intraoperative cholangiogram indicates a retained stone in the distal common bile duct?
Back: A persistent filling defect with a failure of contrast medium to flow freely into the duodenum.

Front: What is the definitive treatment for a patient with symptomatic cholelithiasis who is a candidate for surgery but absolutely refuses an operation?
Back: Oral bile acid dissolution therapy using ursodeoxycholic acid, though it has high failure and recurrence rates.

Front: What does a significantly elevated serum CA 19-9 level indicate in a patient with chronic primary sclerosing cholangitis?
Back: Malignant transformation into a cholangiocarcinoma.

Front: What type of incision is classically used for an open cholecystectomy or complex open biliary reconstruction?
Back: A right subcostal incision, also known as a Kocher incision.

Front: What is the most reliable clinical sign distinguishing an intrahepatic abscess from acute cholecystitis on physical examination?
Back: Direct percussion tenderness over the right lower rib cage indicates a liver abscess.

Part 14: Final Integration & Summary Pearls
Front: What is the clinical significance of a phrygian cap on a diagnostic gallbladder ultrasound?
Back: It is a normal anatomical variant caused by folding of the gallbladder fundus with no pathological consequence.

Front: Name the classic source of pain radiation in diaphragmatic irritation caused by a ruptured hepatic abscess or inflamed gallbladder.
Back: Right shoulder pain mediated by the phrenic nerve via spinal cords segments C3, C4, and C5.

Front: In a patient with acute cholecystitis, what clinical finding strongly indicates progression to gallbladder gangrene or free perforation?
Back: The development of diffuse peritonitis, high unremitting fever, and marked hemodynamic instability.

Front: What antibiotic regimen is preferred for initial empirical coverage of severe acute cholangitis?
Back: Piperacillin-tazobactam monotherapy, or a combination of ceftriaxone and metronidazole.

Front: What is the primary long-term complication of an unrecognized or untreated common bile duct stricture?
Back: Secondary biliary cirrhosis and subsequent portal hypertension.

Part 15: Critical Differential Diagnosis Flashcards
Front: Jaundiced patient with epigastric pain radiating to the back and elevated serum lipase. Diagnosis?
Back: Gallstone pancreatitis.

Front: What is the recommended timing of cholecystectomy for a patient with mild gallstone pancreatitis?
Back: Laparoscopic cholecystectomy during the same hospital admission once the clinical pancreatitis resolves.

Front: What severe complication must be suspected when an elderly diabetic patient with acute cholecystitis fails to improve on broad-spectrum antibiotics?
Back: Perforation or gangrenous cholecystitis.

Front: What specific physical exam finding separates mechanical biliary obstruction from intrinsic hepatocellular jaundice like acute hepatitis?
Back: Acholic (clay-colored) stools and dark tea-colored urine favor mechanical biliary obstruction.

Front: Why does mechanical biliary obstruction cause pale, clay-colored stools?
Back: Conjugated bilirubin cannot enter the duodenum to be converted into stercobilin by gut bacteria.

Part 16: Refined Surgical Core Knowledge
Front: What anatomical line divides the liver into true functional right and left lobes?
Back: Cantlie's line, running from the middle hepatic vein fossa to the gallbladder fossa.

Front: Which hepatic segments are removed during a standard formal right hepatectomy?
Back: Segments V, VI, VII, and VIII.

Front: What is the definitive management of an incidental, asymptomatic one-centimeter gallbladder polyp found on routine ultrasound?
Back: Serial surveillance ultrasounds every six to twelve months to monitor for growth.

Front: At what specific size threshold does an asymptomatic gallbladder polyp warrant definitive laparoscopic cholecystectomy?
Back: When the polyp reaches or exceeds ten millimeters in size, due to increased malignancy risk.

Front: What is the most common cause of non-traumatic hemobilia in Western nations?
Back: Iatrogenic trauma following instrumentation like ERCP, percutaneous liver biopsy, or transhepatic cholangiography.

Part 17: Final Mastery Set
Front: A patient with a history of ulcerative colitis presents with a single dominant stricture on ERCP. What must be ruled out?
Back: Cholangiocarcinoma, via brush cytology or fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH).

Front: What physiological phenomenon causes the increased risk of gallstones during pregnancy?
Back: Progesterone-induced gallbladder hypomotility leading to bile stasis, combined with estrogen-induced cholesterol supersaturation.

Front: What is the primary mechanism of gallstone formation in patients on prolonged total parenteral nutrition (TPN)?
Back: Lack of enteral stimulation leads to a decrease in CCK release, causing gallbladder stasis and sludge formation.

Front: What is the management for an asymptomatic, clean-walled simple hepatic cyst found incidentally on CT?
Back: No treatment or follow-up is necessary.

Front: What defining clinical feature distinguishes a choledochal cyst from a simple hepatic cyst on cross-sectional imaging?
Back: A choledochal cyst clearly communicates directly with the biliary tree.

Part 18: Clinical Wrap-Up
Front: What is the most common serious complication associated with therapeutic ERCP?
Back: Post-ERCP pancreatitis.

Front: What intraoperative maneuver can a surgeon use to identify the exact location of a major bile duct injury during open repair?
Back: Retrograde injection of safe dye or direct tracking of bile leak points after drying the operative field.

Front: What type of biliary enteric anastomosis is preferred to reconstruct a completely transected common bile duct?
Back: A Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy.

Front: What defines a Type V choledochal cyst according to the Todani classification?
Back: Caroli's disease, which features multiple intrahepatic bile duct ectasias or cysts.

Front: What long-term nutritional deficiency must be monitored in patients with chronic external biliary fistulas or long-standing biliary obstruction?
Back: Deficiencies in fat-soluble vitamins, specifically vitamins A, D, E, and K.

Section:  General Surgery

Topic: Breast surgery — benign vs malignant disease

Front: What three components constitute the "triple assessment" for a breast mass?
Back: Clinical breast examination, diagnostic imaging (mammography/ultrasound), and tissue biopsy.

Front: What is the preferred diagnostic imaging modality for a breast mass in a woman under 30 years old?
Back: Target breast ultrasound.

Front: Why is ultrasound preferred over mammography in women younger than 30?
Back: Dense breast tissue in younger women limits the diagnostic sensitivity of mammography.

Front: What is the preferred diagnostic imaging modality for an initial breast mass workup in a woman aged 30 or older?
Back: Diagnostic mammography paired with an ultrasound.

Front: What specific biopsy technique is the standard first-line choice to diagnose a solid breast mass?
Back: Core needle biopsy.

Front: Why is fine-needle aspiration (FNA) generally insufficient for an initial diagnosis of suspected breast cancer?
Back: Fine-needle aspiration cannot differentiate between in situ and invasive disease due to the lack of intact architecture.

Front: What does a BI-RADS category 0 score signify?
Back: Incomplete assessment requiring additional imaging evaluation.

Front: What does a BI-RADS category 1 score signify?
Back: Negative screening with symmetric, unremarkable breast tissue.

Front: What does a BI-RADS category 2 score signify?
Back: Benign findings with zero malignant potential.

Front: What does a BI-RADS category 3 score signify?
Back: Probably benign finding requiring a short-interval follow-up in six months.

Front: What does a BI-RADS category 4 score signify?
Back: Suspicious abnormality warranting a tissue biopsy.

Front: What does a BI-RADS category 5 score signify?
Back: Highly suggestive of malignancy requiring definitive tissue diagnosis.

Front: What does a BI-RADS category 6 score signify?
Back: Known, biopsy-proven malignant disease.

Front: When is a diagnostic breast MRI indicated in standard clinical workups?
Back: Screening high-risk patients with genetic mutations, evaluating occult primary tumors, or assessing silicone implant ruptures.

Front: What surgical intervention must be performed if a core needle biopsy yields a pathology report that is discordant with highly suspicious imaging?
Back: Surgical excisional biopsy.

Module 2: Benign Breast Diseases
Front: What is the most common benign breast tumor in women younger than 30 years old?
Back: Fibroadenoma.

Front: What are the classic physical exam features of a breast fibroadenoma?
Back: Firm, painless, smooth, highly mobile, and well-circumscribed mass.

Front: What classic ultrasound feature characterizes a benign fibroadenoma?
Back: Well-circumscribed, uniform, hypoechoic, oval mass oriented parallel to the tissue planes.

Front: What is the definitive management for an asymptomatic, biopsy-confirmed fibroadenoma under 2 centimeters?
Back: Conservative clinical observation and periodic reassurance.

Front: When is surgical excision indicated for a suspected fibroadenoma?
Back: Rapid tumor growth, patient anxiety, severe cosmetic distortion, or size exceeding 3 centimeters.

Front: What benign breast condition presents with painful, bilateral, cyclic breast lumps that fluctuate with the menstrual cycle?
Back: Fibrocystic changes.

Front: What fluid color is typically obtained during the aspiration of a simple breast cyst?
Back: Straw-colored, green, or dark brown clear fluid.

Front: When must the fluid from a breast cyst aspiration be sent for cytological analysis?
Back: If the aspirated fluid is frankly bloody or grossly purulent.

Front: What is the next management step if a breast cyst mass does not completely resolve after needle aspiration?
Back: Core needle biopsy or formal surgical excision.

Front: What benign condition is the most common cause of spontaneous, unilateral, bloody nipple discharge from a single duct?
Back: Intraductal papilloma.

Front: What is the standard diagnostic and therapeutic surgical intervention for an intraductal papilloma?
Back: Proximal subareolar duct excision of the involved major ductal system.

Front: What benign condition presents with thick, green or cheesy nipple discharge and a tender, subareolar mass in a perimenopausal woman?
Back: Mammary duct ectasia.

Front: What is the underlying pathology of mammary duct ectasia?
Back: Subacute, non-bacterial inflammation causing dilation of the major subareolar lactiferous ducts.

Front: What benign breast lesion mimics invasive carcinoma on imaging by presenting as a stellate mass with central architectural distortion?
Back: Radial scar.

Front: Why must a radial scar identified on a core biopsy undergo complete surgical excision?
Back: It carries a high rate of sampling error and can harbor occult tubular carcinoma or DCIS.

Module 3: Infectious & Inflammatory Conditions
Front: What pathogen is the most common causative agent of acute puerperal mastitis?
Back: Staphylococcus aureus.

Front: What is the first-line management for uncomplicated lactational mastitis?
Back: Empirical oral dicloxacillin or cephalexin alongside continuous, uninterrupted breastfeeding or pumping.

Front: What complication should be suspected if lactational mastitis fails to improve after 48 hours of appropriate antibiotic therapy?
Back: Breast abscess.

Front: What is the preferred first-line management for a fluctuant, non-complicated breast abscess?
Back: Serial ultrasound-guided needle aspirations combined with targeted antibiotic therapy.

Front: When is open surgical incision and drainage indicated for a breast abscess?
Back: When the abscess is multiloculated, very large, or refractory to multiple ultrasound-guided aspiration attempts.

Front: What benign, painful breast condition mimics locally advanced cancer by causing skin retraction, erythema, and axillary adenopathy following localized chest wall trauma?
Back: Fat necrosis.

Front: What classic microscopic pathology finding confirms a diagnosis of breast fat necrosis?
Back: Lipid-laden macrophages, foamy histiocytes, giant cells, and chronic inflammatory fibrosis.

Front: What is the medical eponym for superficial thrombophlebitis of the lateral thoracic or thoracoepigastric veins?
Back: Mondor's disease.

Front: What is the standard therapeutic management for a patient diagnosed with Mondor's disease?
Back: Reassurance, warm compresses, and oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.

Module 4: High-Risk Lesions & Premalignancy
Front: How does the finding of atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH) on a core biopsy alter subsequent management?
Back: It mandates formal surgical excisional biopsy due to a high risk of upgrading to ductal carcinoma in situ or invasive disease.

Front: What is the long-term cancer risk associated with a confirmed diagnosis of atypical ductal hyperplasia?
Back: A four- to five-fold relative increase in lifetime risk for developing breast cancer in either breast.

Front: How is lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS) classically characterized regarding its biological nature?
Back: It is primarily a non-palpable, incidental histological finding that acts as a marker of bilateral, multi-centric cancer risk.

Front: What is the initial preferred management for classic lobular carcinoma in situ discovered on a core needle biopsy?
Back: Excisional biopsy to rule out adjacent invasive disease, followed by close surveillance and risk-reduction strategies.

Front: What chemical risk-reduction therapy is routinely offered to premenopausal women with high-risk lesions like atypia or LCIS?
Back: Tamoxifen therapy for 5 years.

Front: What chemical risk-reduction therapy is preferred for postmenopausal women with high-risk benign breast lesions?
Back: Anastrozole or exemestane.

Module 5: Malignant Diseases — Epidemiology, Risk, & Genetics
Front: What is the most significant non-modifiable risk factor for developing breast cancer, excluding female sex?
Back: Advancing age.

Front: What autosomal dominant genetic mutations carry the highest lifetime risk for developing both breast and ovarian malignancies?
Back: BRCA1 and BRCA2 tumor suppressor gene mutations.

Front: What is the estimated lifetime risk of developing breast cancer for a woman carrying a pathogenic BRCA1 mutation?
Back: Between 60% and 80%.

Front: With what other specific cancers is the BRCA2 mutation classically associated?
Back: Male breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, prostate cancer, and melanoma.

Front: What specific screening protocol is recommended for women with a known, pathogenic BRCA mutation starting at age 25?
Back: Annual screening breast MRI alternated with annual screening mammography every six months.

Front: What preventive surgical options dramatically reduce breast cancer risk in BRCA mutation carriers?
Back: Bilateral risk-reducing prophylactic mastectomies.

Front: What syndrome characterized by TP53 mutations presents with early-onset breast cancer, sarcomas, and adrenocortical carcinomas?
Back: Li-Fraumeni syndrome.

Front: What germline mutation causes Cowden syndrome, which features trichilemmomas, macrocephaly, and increased risks of breast and thyroid cancers?
Back: PTEN gene mutation.

Module 6: Pathology & Molecular Subtypes
Front: What is the most common histological type of non-invasive breast malignancy?
Back: Ductal Carcinoma In Situ (DCIS).

Front: What is the pathognomonic mammographic signature of ductal carcinoma in situ?
Back: Pleomorphic, linear, or branching microcalcifications without a discrete soft tissue mass.

Front: What high-grade subtype of DCIS is characterized by central necrosis, calcification, and an aggressive localized nature?
Back: Comedocarcinoma.

Front: What is the most common histological variant of all invasive breast cancers?
Back: Invasive Ductal Carcinoma (IDC), Not Otherwise Specified.

Front: What histological feature is uniquely characteristic of invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) on biopsy?
Back: Small, uniform tumor cells invading in a single-file, linear pattern due to a loss of E-cadherin expression.

Front: What unique clinical distribution pattern characterizes invasive lobular carcinoma compared to invasive ductal carcinoma?
Back: A significantly higher incidence of bilateral, multifocal, and diffuse multicentric disease.

Front: What malignant breast tumor presents as an eczematous, scaling, ulcerated lesion of the nipple-areolar complex?
Back: Paget's disease of the breast.

Front: What does the clinical presence of Paget's disease of the breast almost universally signify?
Back: An underlying ductal carcinoma in situ or an invasive ductal carcinoma in the deeper breast parenchyma.

Front: What large, fast-growing stromal breast tumor mimics a giant fibroadenoma but exhibits a leaf-like growth pattern on histology?
Back: Phyllodes tumor.

Front: What is the mandatory surgical margin required when resecting a localized benign or malignant phyllodes tumor?
Back: Wide local excision with negative margins of at least 1 centimeter.

Front: Why is routine axillary lymph node dissection not indicated for malignant phyllodes tumors?
Back: Phyllodes tumors are sarcomatous stromal malignancies that metastasize hematogenously rather than via lymphatics.

Front: What aggressive variant of breast cancer presents with rapid-onset erythema, warmth, diffuse edema, and skin induration?
Back: Inflammatory Breast Cancer (IBC).

Front: What is the mechanical cause of the "peau d'orange" skin appearance seen in inflammatory breast cancer?
Back: Tumor emboli blocking the dermal lymphatic channels, causing localized lymphedema around tethered hair follicles.

Front: What molecular marker is overexpressed in approximately 15% to 20% of breast cancers and drives aggressive cell proliferation via tyrosine kinase pathways?
Back: HER2/neu (human epidermal growth factor receptor 2).

Front: What molecular profile defines a "triple-negative" breast cancer?
Back: Absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and HER2/neu expression.

Module 7: Staging & Prognostic Factors
Front: What staging system is universally used to classify breast cancer?
Back: The TNM staging system based on tumor size, nodal status, and distant metastasis.

Front: What is the single most powerful independent prognostic indicator for long-term survival in patients with early-stage invasive breast cancer?
Back: Axillary lymph node status.

Front: How is a T1 vocalization defined in the TNM breast cancer staging framework?
Back: A primary tumor that is 2 centimeters or less in its greatest dimension.

Front: How is a T2 vocalization defined in the TNM breast cancer staging framework?
Back: A primary tumor greater than 2 centimeters but not exceeding 5 centimeters.

Front: How is a T3 vocalization defined in the TNM breast cancer staging framework?
Back: A primary tumor that is greater than 5 centimeters in its greatest dimension.

Front: How is a T4 vocalization defined in the TNM breast cancer staging framework?
Back: A tumor of any size with direct extension to the chest wall or involvement of the skin.

Front: What constitutes an N1 nodal classification in clinically apparent early breast cancer?
Back: Metastasis to movable, ipsilateral level I or level II axillary lymph nodes.

Front: What anatomical landmark differentiates the three structural levels of axillary lymph nodes?
Back: The pectoralis minor muscle.

Front: Where are Level I axillary lymph nodes situated anatomically?
Back: Lateral and inferior to the lower border of the pectoralis minor muscle.

Front: Where are Level II axillary lymph nodes situated anatomically?
Back: Directly deep to, or posterior to, the pectoralis minor muscle.

Front: Where are Level III axillary lymph nodes situated anatomically?
Back: Medial and superior to the upper border of the pectoralis minor muscle.

Front: What is Rotter's node?
Back: An interpectoral lymph node located between the pectoralis major and pectoralis minor muscles.

Module 8: Operative Management & Local Therapy
Front: What two primary surgical strategies provide equivalent long-term survival for early-stage (Stage I and II) breast cancers?
Back: Breast-conserving surgery combined with radiation therapy versus a total mastectomy alone.

Front: What surgical technique defines a lumpectomy or wide local excision?
Back: Removal of the primary breast tumor along with a circumferential cuff of normal, tumor-free surrounding tissue.

Front: What is an absolute contraindication to choosing breast-conserving surgery?
Back: Diffuse malignant microcalcifications, multicentric disease in multiple quadrants, or a history of prior chest radiation.

Front: What constitutes a standard total (simple) mastectomy?
Back: Complete removal of the entire breast parenchyma, overlying skin ellipse, and the nipple-areolar complex, without removing axillary lymph nodes.

Front: What tissue layers are completely resected during a modified radical mastectomy?
Back: The entire breast parenchyma, skin, nipple-areolar complex, and a formal Level I/II axillary lymph node dissection.

Front: How does a radical mastectomy (Halsted mastectomy) differ from a modified radical mastectomy?
Back: The radical mastectomy removes both the pectoralis major and minor muscles alongside the breast and complete axillary contents.

Front: What is the current standard margin definition for a negative margin in the surgical excision of invasive breast cancer?
Back: "No ink on the tumor" cells at the margin interface.

Front: What constitutes an acceptable negative margin width when performing a lumpectomy for pure ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS)?
Back: A minimum width of 2 millimeters from the inked margin boundary.

Front: What primary procedure avoids empiric axillary dissection by identifying the initial draining lymph nodes of a primary breast tumor?
Back: Sentinel Lymph Node Biopsy (SLNB).

Front: What two mapping agents are injected into the peri-areolar or peritumoral space to localize the sentinel lymph node?
Back: Technetium-99m labeled sulfur colloid radiotracer and isosulfan blue dye.

Front: What specific criteria dictate the intraoperative removal of a lymph node during a sentinel lymph node mapping procedure?
Back: Any node that is visually blue, radioactive with a count of 10% or more of the hottest node, or clinically firm/palpable.

Front: When can a formal axillary lymph node dissection be safely omitted in a patient with 1 or 2 positive sentinel lymph nodes?
Back: When the patient undergoes breast-conserving surgery followed by planned whole-breast irradiation and systemic adjuvant therapy.

Module 9: Systemic & Adjuvant Therapies
Front: What is the primary clinical rationale for administering neoadjuvant chemotherapy in large or locally advanced breast cancers?
Back: To downstage the primary tumor size to allow for breast-conserving surgery instead of a mandatory mastectomy.

Front: What is the standard management sequence for a patient presenting with biopsy-confirmed inflammatory breast cancer?
Back: Neoadjuvant systemic chemotherapy, followed by a modified radical mastectomy, and completed with adjuvant radiation therapy.

Front: What cardiotoxic anthracycline agent is a cornerstone of standard multi-agent breast cancer chemotherapy regimens?
Back: Doxorubicin.

Front: What specific adverse effect is the major dose-limiting toxicity of doxorubicin therapy?
Back: Irreversible dilated cardiomyopathy.

Front: What class of chemotherapy agents includes Paclitaxel and Docetaxel, frequently used in high-risk node-positive breast cancer?
Back: Taxanes.

Front: What is the mechanism of action of Tamoxifen in breast tissue?
Back: Competitive antagonist of the estrogen receptor in breast tissue.

Front: What serious systemic adverse effects are associated with the use of Tamoxifen?
Back: Increased risk of endometrial carcinoma and deep vein thromboembolism.

Front: Why do aromatase inhibitors fail to work effectively in premenopausal breast cancer patients?
Back: They cannot inhibit the major source of estrogen produced directly by functional, cycling ovaries.

Front: What is the primary bone-related adverse effect associated with long-term aromatase inhibitor therapy?
Back: Accelerated bone mineral density loss leading to osteoporosis and fragility fractures.

Front: What monoclonal antibody targeted therapy is indicated for patients with confirmed HER2/neu-positive breast cancer?
Back: Trastuzumab.

Front: What is the key toxicological concern associated with Trastuzumab therapy?
Back: Reversible, non-dose-dependent cardiotoxicity presenting as a decline in left ventricular ejection fraction.

Front: What diagnostic test must be performed periodically to monitor a patient undergoing Trastuzumab therapy?
Back: Echocardiogram or MUGA scan to monitor left ventricular ejection fraction.

Module 10: Surgical Anatomy & Complications
Front: Injury to what nerve during axillary dissection causes a "winged scapula"?
Back: Long thoracic nerve.

Front: What muscle is directly innervated by the long thoracic nerve?
Back: Serratus anterior muscle.

Front: Injury to what nerve during axillary clearance leads to weak arm extension, adduction, and internal rotation?
Back: Thoracodorsal nerve.

Front: What major muscle is innervated by the thoracodorsal nerve?
Back: Latissimus dorsi muscle.

Front: What nerve is frequently transected or injured during axillary procedures, causing numbness to the skin of the upper inner arm?
Back: Intercostobrachial nerve.

Front: Injury to the medial pectoral nerve during radical surgery impairs the function of what muscles?
Back: Pectoralis major and pectoralis minor muscles.

Front: What complication presents as a localized, painless, fluctuant fluid collection under the mastectomy skin flaps or axilla postoperatively?
Back: Seroma.

Front: What is the initial appropriate management for a symptomatic or tense postoperative breast seroma?
Back: Sterile percutaneous needle aspiration in the outpatient clinic setting.

Front: What chronic, debilitating complication occurs in up to 20% of patients who undergo a full axillary lymph node dissection?
Back: Ipsilateral upper extremity lymphedema.

Front: What rare, highly lethal vascular malignancy can develop in an upper extremity affected by long-standing, chronic lymphedema?
Back: Lymphangiosarcoma (Stewart-Treves syndrome).

Front: What anatomical structure marks the superior boundary of a standard axillary lymph node dissection?
Back: The axillary vein.

Front: What anatomical structure defines the posterior boundary of the axillary dissection space?
Back: The anterior surface of the subscapularis muscle.

Front: What anatomical structure marks the medial border of a formal axillary lymph node clearance?
Back: The chest wall and the serratus anterior muscle line.

Front: What immediate intraoperative action must be taken if a minor laceration occurs to the axillary vein during dissection?
Back: Application of direct pressure followed by fine, primary vascular repair using monofilament polypropylene suture.

Module 11: Special Populations & Clinical Scenarios
Front: What is the most common cause of a breast mass in a lactating or pregnant patient?
Back: Galactocele (a benign, milk-filled retention cyst).

Front: How should a solid, highly suspicious breast mass discovered during pregnancy be diagnosed?
Back: Core needle biopsy under ultrasound guidance.

Front: What staging or diagnostic imaging component is strictly contraindicated during pregnancy due to fetal risk?
Back: Bone scan or abdominal CT imaging without shielding.

Front: Can breast cancer patients safely undergo systemic chemotherapy while pregnant?
Back: Yes, but it is strictly contraindicated during the first trimester due to teratogenicity.

Front: What localized breast cancer therapeutic modality is absolutely contraindicated during pregnancy?
Back: Radiation therapy to the chest wall or breast.

Front: What is the most common clinical presentation of male breast cancer?
Back: A painless, firm, unilateral subareolar mass with or without skin fixation or nipple inversion.

Front: What systemic risk factor significantly increases a male's risk for developing breast carcinoma?
Back: Klinefelter syndrome (47, XXY) or a family history carrying a BRCA2 mutation.

Front: What is the primary surgical management strategy for a male patient diagnosed with invasive breast cancer?
Back: Total mastectomy with sentinel lymph node evaluation or axillary dissection.

Module 12: High-Yield Recall & Case Files Vignette Pearls
Front: What diagnosis must be ruled out in an older woman presenting with an inverted nipple and a hard axillary mass?
Back: Invasive ductal carcinoma.

Front: What diagnosis must be ruled out if an apparent breast infection fails to respond to two sequential courses of oral antibiotics?
Back: Inflammatory breast cancer.

Front: What does a "popcorn-like" calcification pattern on a mammogram classically indicate?
Back: A degenerating, calcified benign fibroadenoma.

Front: What type of biopsy should be avoided for a highly suspicious, non-palpable mammographic lesion?
Back: Blind incisional or excisional biopsy without needle or wire localization.

Front: What is the management for an asymptomatic patient who has an architectural distortion on mammography but a negative ultrasound?
Back: Stereotactic core needle biopsy.

Front: What benign condition can present with skin dimpling that perfectly mimics advanced breast carcinoma?
Back: Fat necrosis with severe localized scarring.

Front: What type of breast reconstruction uses an autologous tissue flap based on the lower abdominal wall fat and skin?
Back: Deep Inferior Epigastric Perforator (DIEP) flap or Transverse Rectus Abdominis Myocutaneous (TRAM) flap.

Front: What is the primary advantage of a DIEP flap over a TRAM flap in breast reconstruction?
Back: The DIEP flap spares the rectus abdominis muscle, reducing the risk of postoperative abdominal wall hernias and weakness.

Front: What physical exam finding indicates that a breast tumor has directly invaded the underlying pectoralis major muscle fascia?
Back: The mass becomes fixed and immobile when the patient places their hands on their hips and contracts the pectoralis muscles.

Front: What structure is attached to the skin of the breast and can cause skin dimpling when pulled by an underlying tumor?
Back: Cooper's suspensory ligaments.

Front: What is the preferred diagnostic test to confirm a suspected silent rupture of a silicone breast implant?
Back: A dedicated non-contrast breast MRI.

Front: What benign lesion is characterized by a "cookie-cutter" look of normal lobules packed tightly together with sclerotic stroma on histology?
Back: Sclerosing adenosis.

Front: What is the significance of finding sclerosing adenosis on a breast biopsy?
Back: It is a proliferative lesion that conveys a minimal, roughly 1.5- to 2-fold increase in future cancer risk.

Front: What surgical step is required if a core biopsy of a breast mass returns a diagnosis of "atypical lobular hyperplasia" (ALH)?
Back: Surgical excisional biopsy to definitively rule out adjacent lobular carcinoma in situ or invasive lobular cancer.

Front: What does the term "multicentric breast cancer" mean?
Back: The presence of two or more distinct primary tumors in entirely different quadrants of the same breast.

Front: What does the term "multifocal breast cancer" mean?
Back: The presence of two or more distinct tumor foci within the same quadrant of the breast.

Front: What is the management of a screening mammogram showing architectural distortion without a corresponding sonographic mass?
Back: Digital breast tomosynthesis-guided (3D) or stereotactic core needle biopsy.

Front: What is the definitive surgical management for biopsy-proven Ductal Carcinoma in Situ (DCIS) that is multicentric or spans greater than 4 centimeters?
Back: Total (simple) mastectomy.

Front: Why is a sentinel lymph node biopsy indicated during a total mastectomy for pure DCIS, but not during a lumpectomy for pure DCIS?
Back: If the mastectomy specimen reveals occult invasive cancer, a sentinel node biopsy cannot be performed later because the breast lymphatics have been disrupted.

Front: When is post-mastectomy radiation therapy (PMRT) explicitly indicated?
Back: Primary tumor greater than 5 centimeters (T3/T4), positive chest wall/skin margins, or four or more positive axillary lymph nodes.

Front: What is the significance of a 21-gene expression assay (Oncotype DX) score in early-stage, ER-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer?
Back: It predicts the likelihood of distant recurrence and determines whether the patient will derive a survival benefit from adding adjuvant chemotherapy to hormone therapy.

Back: Breast Implant-Associated Anaplastic Large Cell Lymphoma (BIA-ALCL).

Module 17: Benign Micro-Pathology & Surgical Margins

Front: What landmark clinical trial proved that axillary lymph node dissection can be safely omitted in specific patients with 1 to 2 positive sentinel nodes undergoing lumpectomy?
Back: The ACOSOG Z0011 trial.

Front: What are the strict criteria a patient must meet to safely omit an axillary lymph node dissection despite having 1 or 2 positive sentinel lymph nodes?
Back: T1-T2 tumor, undergoing breast-conserving surgery, receiving whole-breast radiation, and receiving systemic adjuvant therapy.

Front: Why must a patient with 1 to 2 positive sentinel lymph nodes undergo a full axillary dissection if they choose a mastectomy over a lumpectomy?
Back: They do not meet the ACOSOG Z0011 criteria because they do not routinely receive whole-breast irradiation after surgery.

Front: What landmark clinical trial established that axillary radiotherapy provides equivalent regional control to axillary lymph node dissection for positive sentinel nodes with significantly less lymphedema?
Back: The AMAROS trial.

Section:  General Surgery

Topic: Trauma — ATLS principles, damage control surgery


Front: What is the very first clinical priority when managing a trauma patient upon arrival?

Back: Ensuring personal safety of the medical team, followed immediately by assessing the patient's airway.

Front: What mechanism must be presumed present in any patient with multisystem trauma above the clavicle?

Back: Unstable cervical spine injury.

Front: What is the correct manual technique to stabilize the cervical spine during airway management?

Back: Inline manual stabilization, avoiding hyperextension of the neck.

Front: What basic airway maneuver should be performed first in an obtunded trauma patient with suspected C-spine injury?

Back: Jaw thrust maneuver without head tilt.

Front: What absolute GCS score dictates immediate definitive airway management via endotracheal intubation?

Back: A Glasgow Coma Scale score of 8 or less.

Front: What is the definitive method used to confirm correct endotracheal tube placement in the trauma bay?

Back: Waveform capnography detecting end-tidal carbon dioxide ($ETCO_2$).

Front: What surgical airway procedure is indicated if endotracheal intubation fails or is impossible due to severe maxillofacial trauma?

Back: Emergency surgical cricothyroidotomy.

Front: Why is a surgical tracheostomy preferred against in the hyperacute emergency trauma resuscitation setting?

Back: It is technically difficult, time-consuming, and carries a high risk of significant hemorrhage.

Front: What lethal thoracic condition presents with unilateral absent breath sounds, hyperresonance, and tracheal deviation?

Back: Tension pneumothorax.

Front: What is the immediate treatment for a suspected tension pneumothorax before obtaining an X-ray?

Back: Needle decompression followed immediately by finger thoracostomy or chest tube insertion.

Front: What is the preferred anatomical site for needle decompression in adults according to updated ATLS guidelines?

Back: The fifth intercostal space just anterior to the midaxillary line.

Front: What classic triad of physical signs indicates the presence of acute cardiac tamponade?

Back: Beck's triad, consisting of hypotension, muffled heart sounds, and distended neck veins.

Front: How does a massive hemothorax clinically differentiate from a tension pneumothorax on physical exam?

Back: A massive hemothorax presents with dullness to percussion instead of hyperresonance.

Front: What is the quantitative definition of a massive hemothorax upon initial chest tube placement?

Back: Immediate drainage of 1,500 mL or more of blood, or more than 200 mL per hour for 2 to 4 hours.

Front: What defines a flail chest on physical examination of the thorax?

Back: Paradoxical inward movement of a chest wall segment during inspiration and outward movement during expiration.

Front: What is the primary underlying cause of severe hypoxia in a patient with a flail chest?

Back: Pulmonary contusion restricting gas exchange across the alveolar membrane.

Front: What are the clinical signs of a sucking chest wound or open pneumothorax?

Back: A visible chest wall defect with bubbling or whistling sounds during respiration.

Front: How should an open pneumothorax be temporarily dressed in the emergency setting?

Back: A sterile occlusive dressing taped securely on three sides to create a one-way flutter valve.

Front: What is the most reliable clinical sign of early, compensated hypovolemic shock in a young trauma patient?

Back: Persistent tachycardia.

Front: Why is blood pressure an unreliable standalone indicator of early hemorrhagic shock?

Back: Compensatory mechanisms can maintain a normal blood pressure until up to 30% of circulating blood volume is lost.

Front: What is the target systolic blood pressure in a bleeding trauma patient without traumatic brain injury under "permissive hypotension" protocols?

Back: A systolic blood pressure of 80 to 90 mmHg to prevent disrupting formed clots.

Front: What simple bedside diagnostic tool is used to screen for free intra-abdominal fluid in the resuscitation bay?

Back: Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma, commonly called the FAST scan.

Front: What are the four anatomical windows evaluated during a standard FAST scan?

Back: Perihepatic (Morison’s pouch), perisplenic, pelvic (retrovesical/rectouterine), and pericardial windows.

Front: What is the immediate intervention for a hemodynamically unstable patient with an obviously fractured, unstable pelvic ring?

Back: Application of a pelvic binder or sheet wrapped tightly around the greater trochanters.

Front: What classic physical exam finding dictates an immediate retrograde urethrogram before inserting a Foley catheter?

Back: High-riding prostate, blood at the urethral meatus, or severe perineal ecchymosis.

Front: What components comprise the "D" or Disability phase of the ATLS Primary Survey?

Back: Assessment of pupillary response, gross motor movement, and calculating the Glasgow Coma Scale score.

Front: What essential step must be performed during the "E" or Exposure phase of the primary survey?

Back: Completely undressing the patient while maintaining normothermia via external warming devices.

Front: What does a positive eFAST scan look for that a standard FAST scan does not?

Back: The presence of a pneumothorax via the loss of normal lung sliding at the anterior chest wall.

Front: When is a stable trauma patient cleared to proceed to a contrast-enhanced CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis?

Back: Only when they are completely hemodynamically stable after initial resuscitation.

Front: What diagnostic procedure is indicated in a hemodynamically unstable patient with a suspected intra-abdominal injury and a negative or equivocal FAST scan?

Back: Diagnostic Peritoneal Aspirate or Diagnostic Peritoneal Lavage.

Front: What constitutes a positive gross aspirate during a Diagnostic Peritoneal Aspirate?

Back: Aspiration of 10 mL or more of gross free blood or enteric contents upon initial catheter insertion.

Front: What is the next clinical step if a trauma patient exhibits a transient response to initial fluid resuscitation?

Back: Initiate blood product transfusion and prepare the patient for definitive operative or angiographic intervention.

Front: What marks the formal transition from the Primary Survey to the Secondary Survey?

Back: The completion of ABCDEs, stabilization of vital signs, and initiation of any primary resuscitation adjuncts.

Front: What is the fundamental objective of the ATLS Secondary Survey?

Back: A comprehensive, head-to-toe physical examination and history to identify all co-existing injuries.

Front: What mnemonic summarizes the key historical components obtained during the secondary survey?

Back: AMPLE, standing for Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last meal, and Events surrounding the injury.

Front: What injury must be highly suspected in a patient with a steering wheel imprint on the chest and new-onset cardiac arrhythmias?

Back: Blunt cardiac injury or myocardial contusion.

Front: What is the most common site of traumatic aortic disruption or rupture in blunt deceleration injuries?

Back: The aortic isthmus, located just distal to the origin of the left subclavian artery.

Front: What classic radiologic finding on an anteroposterior chest X-ray suggests a traumatic aortic injury?

Back: Widened mediastinum greater than 8 cm.

Front: What is the preferred gold-standard diagnostic imaging modality to confirm a traumatic blunt aortic injury?

Back: Computed Tomography Angiography of the chest.

Front: What medication class is indicated in a stable patient with a blunt aortic injury to prevent absolute rupture?

Back: Short-acting intravenous beta-blockers to lower heart rate and reduce sheer stress.

Front: What condition should be suspected in a patient who develops sudden, profound subcutaneous emphysema after severe blunt chest trauma?

Back: Tracheobronchial tree disruption or major airway injury.

Front: What is the definitive diagnostic test to confirm a suspected tracheobronchial injury?

Back: Direct visualization via flexible bronchoscopy.

Front: What defines a massive hemothorax in terms of ongoing hourly output from a correctly placed chest tube?

Back: Ongoing blood loss greater than 200 mL per hour for 3 to 4 consecutive hours.

Front: What is the primary indication for emergency resuscitative thoracotomy performed directly in the emergency department?

Back: Penetrating thoracic trauma with witnessed loss of vital signs during transport or within the bay.

Front: What are the key therapeutic maneuvers executed during an emergency resuscitative thoracotomy?

Back: Pericardiotomy to relieve tamponade, direct cardiac massage, and cross-clamping of the descending thoracic aorta.

Front: Why is the descending thoracic aorta cross-clamped during an emergency resuscitative thoracotomy?

Back: To redistribute limited blood volume to the cerebral and coronary circulations while stopping infradiaphragmatic hemorrhage.



Front: Which solid intra-abdominal organ is most frequently injured in the setting of severe blunt abdominal trauma?

Back: The spleen.

Front: What clinical sign describes referred left shoulder pain caused by diaphragmatic irritation from a ruptured spleen?

Back: Kehr's sign.

Front: What is the primary management strategy for a hemodynamically stable patient with a Grade I or II liver or spleen laceration?

Back: Non-operative management with serial physical exams, bed rest, and serial hemoglobin monitoring.

Front: What intervention is indicated for a hemodynamically stable trauma patient with active contrast extravasation seen on an abdominal CT scan?

Back: Transcatheter arterial embolization via interventional radiology.

Front: What clinical triad suggests a retroperitoneal hematoma secondary to pelvic or renal injury?

Back: Flank ecchymosis, palpable flank mass, and progressive hemodynamic instability.

Front: Into how many zones is the retroperitoneum divided for surgical management during laparotomy?

Back: Three distinct zones.

Front: What retroperitoneal zone contains the abdominal aorta, inferior vena cava, and duodenum?

Back: Zone 1, which is the central-medial retroperitoneum.

Front: What is the management mandate for a Zone 1 retroperitoneal hematoma discovered during exploratory laparotomy for blunt trauma?

Back: It must always be surgically explored and inspected.

Front: What organs are located within Zone 2 of the retroperitoneum?

Back: The kidneys, adrenal glands, and the ascending or descending colon.

Front: When should a Zone 2 retroperitoneal hematoma from blunt trauma be surgically explored?

Back: Only if it is actively expanding, pulsatile, or if there is a known preoperative renal vascular injury.

Front: What area does Zone 3 of the retroperitoneum encompass?

Back: The pelvic retroperitoneum.

Front: What is the preferred initial management for an expanding retroperitoneal Zone 3 hematoma caused by a pelvic fracture?

Back: External pelvic stabilization and angiographic embolization, avoiding direct surgical opening of the hematoma.

Front: What injury must be ruled out in any patient presenting with a seatbelt sign across the lower abdomen?

Back: Hollow viscus perforation or mesenteric tearing.

Front: What classic chest X-ray finding indicates a traumatic diaphragmatic rupture?

Back: Elevation of the left hemidiaphragm or the presence of the stomach/nasogastric tube within the thoracic cavity.



Front: What is Cushing’s triad and what pathological state does it signify?

Back: Bradycardia, hypertension with a widened pulse pressure, and irregular respirations, signifying dangerously elevated intracranial pressure.

Front: What is the target cerebral perfusion pressure ($CPP$) in a patient with a severe traumatic brain injury?

Back: Between 60 and 70 mmHg.

Front: How is cerebral perfusion pressure calculated mathematically?

Back: Cerebral Perfusion Pressure equals Mean Arterial Pressure minus Intracranial Pressure ($CPP = MAP - ICP$).

Front: What type of intracranial hemorrhage classically presents with a lucid interval followed by rapid neurological decline?

Back: Epidural hematoma.

Front: What vascular structure is most commonly lacerated in an acute epidural hematoma?

Back: The middle meningeal artery, typically secondary to a temporal bone fracture.

Front: What classic shape does an epidural hematoma exhibit on a non-contrast head CT scan?

Back: A hyperdense, biconvex or lens-shaped (lenticular) collection that does not cross cranial sutures.

Front: What type of vascular injury causes an acute subdural hematoma?

Back: Tearing of the fragile bridging cortical veins.

Front: What appearance does a subdural hematoma display on a non-contrast head CT scan?

Back: A crescent-shaped hyperdensity that can cross cranial suture lines but is limited by the dural reflections.

Front: What is the first-line medical therapy used to acutely lower intracranial pressure by drawing free fluid out of brain parenchyma?

Back: Intravenous hypertonic saline or osmotic diuretics like Mannitol.

Front: Under what specific neurological condition is Mannitol strictly contraindicated in a head-injured patient?

Back: Hypotension or profound hypovolemic shock, due to its potent diuretic effects.

Front: Into how many anatomical zones is the neck divided for trauma management?

Back: Three distinct zones defined by anatomical landmarks.

Front: What are the borders of Neck Zone I?

Back: From the clavicles and sternum up to the lower border of the cricoid cartilage.

Front: What are the anatomical boundaries of Neck Zone II?

Back: From the inferior border of the cricoid cartilage up to the angle of the mandible.

Front: What are the boundaries of Neck Zone III?

Back: From the angle of the mandible up to the base of the skull.

Front: What is the immediate management for any patient with penetrating neck trauma who exhibits "hard signs" of vascular or airway injury?

Back: Immediate surgical exploration via emergency neck laparotomy/exploration.

Front: Name three clinical "hard signs" of penetrating neck trauma that mandate an immediate trip to the operating room.

Back: Pulsatile hemorrhage, expanding hematoma, active airway compromise, or a new stridor/bruit.



Front: What defines a clinical "hard sign" of arterial injury in an extremity with penetrating trauma?

Back: Absent distal pulses, active pulsatile bleeding, an expanding pulsatile hematoma, or a palpable thrill/audible bruit.

Front: What management path is mandated when a hard sign of vascular injury is identified in a mangled extremity?

Back: Immediate operative exploration and vascular repair without delaying for imaging.

Front: What is a clinical "soft sign" of extremity vascular trauma?

Back: A history of moderate bleeding, proximity of the wound to a major artery, or a mild neurological deficit.

Front: What screening index is used to evaluate for occult vascular injury in an injured extremity when pulses are palpable but asymmetric?

Back: The Arterial Pressure Index, calculated by dividing the injured extremity's systolic pressure by the uninjured arm's systolic pressure.

Front: What Arterial Pressure Index score is considered abnormal and requires formal diagnostic angiography?

Back: An Arterial Pressure Index value less than 0.9.

Front: What is the maximum allowable warm ischemia time for an extremity before irreversible muscle necrosis begins?

Back: Approximately 6 hours.

Front: In what sequence should orthopedic and vascular repairs occur in a severely mangled limb with combined unstable fractures and arterial injury?

Back: A temporary intravascular shunt is placed first to restore perfusion, followed by bone stabilization, followed by definitive vascular repair.

Front: What severe condition can occur when an ischemic extremity is suddenly reperfused, leading to systemic hyperkalemia and metabolic acidosis?

Back: Reperfusion injury or systemic crush syndrome.

Front: What are the classic "6 Ps" indicative of acute compartment syndrome in an extremity?

Back: Pain out of proportion to exam, Pain with passive stretch, Paresthesia, Paralysis, Pallor, and Pulselessness.

Front: What is the earliest, most sensitive clinical sign of developing compartment syndrome?

Back: Severe pain that is entirely out of proportion to the clinical findings and exacerbated by passive stretching of the affected muscles.

Front: What delta pressure threshold confirming compartment syndrome mandates an emergent surgical fasciotomy?

Back: A compartment pressure within 30 mmHg of the patient's diastolic blood pressure ($\Delta P \le 30 \text{ mmHg}$).



Front: What is the philosophical core of Damage Control Surgery?

Back: Prioritizing immediate survival via rapid hemorrhage control and contamination limitation over time-consuming definitive anatomic reconstruction.

Front: What three physiological derangements combine to form the classic "Lethal Triad" of trauma?

Back: Hypothermia, metabolic acidosis, and coagulopathy.

Front: How does hypothermia mechanically worsen coagulopathy during a massive resuscitation?

Back: It directly impairs the enzymatic function of the coagulation cascade and severely inhibits normal platelet aggregation.

Front: At what core body temperature does the coagulation cascade become intrinsically impaired?

Back: When the core body temperature drops below 35 degrees Celsius (95 degrees Fahrenheit).

Front: What is the root cause of metabolic acidosis in a profoundly hypotensive trauma patient?

Back: Systemic hypoperfusion leading to anaerobic metabolism and cellular lactic acid accumulation.

Front: What are the three distinct, chronological phases of a formal Damage Control Surgery protocol?

Back: Phase 1: Emergent abbreviating laparotomy; Phase 2: Intensive care unit resuscitation and warming; Phase 3: Planned return to the operating room for definitive repair.

Front: What are the primary structural goals during Phase 1 of Damage Control Surgery?

Back: Controlling active hemorrhage using ties, staples, or packing, and closing or packing any source of enteric contamination.

Front: What technique is used to temporarily close the abdomen at the end of Phase 1 of a damage control laparotomy?

Back: Temporary abdominal closure using a negative pressure dressing or Bogota bag to prevent abdominal compartment syndrome.

Front: What is the primary physiological objective during Phase 2 of Damage Control Surgery in the ICU?

Back: Reversing the lethal triad by aggressively rewarming the patient, correcting coagulopathy, and optimizing tissue perfusion.

Front: When is a trauma patient typically taken back to the operating room for Phase 3 of Damage Control Surgery?

Back: Typically within 24 to 48 hours, once baseline hemodynamics, normothermia, and normal coagulation are fully restored.



Front: What ratio of blood products defines a modern Balanced Massive Transfusion Protocol?

Back: A 1:1:1 ratio of Packed Red Blood Cells ($PRBCs$), Fresh Frozen Plasma ($FFP$), and Platelets.

Front: Why is a 1:1:1 resuscitation strategy preferred over crystalloid fluid resuscitation in severe trauma?

Back: It avoids dilutional coagulopathy and mimics the composition of lost whole blood.

Front: What medication should be administered within 3 hours of injury to adult trauma patients experiencing significant hemorrhagic shock?

Back: Tranexamic Acid ($TXA$), given as a 1-gram loading dose over 10 minutes followed by 1 gram infused over 8 hours.

Front: What is the mechanical mechanism of action of Tranexamic Acid ($TXA$) in trauma resuscitation?

Back: It acts as an antifibrinolytic by competitively blocking plasminogen activation to prevent breakdown of formed fibrin clots.

Front: What is the quantitative target for urine output when resuscitating an adult trauma patient in hemorrhagic shock?

Back: At least 0.5 mL per kilogram of body weight per hour ($0.5 \text{ mL/kg/hr}$).

Front: What calculation formula forms the basis of early fluid resuscitation planning for severe thermal burns?

Back: The Parkland Formula.

Front: What is the standard math for the Parkland Formula to calculate 24-hour fluid requirements?

Back: 4 mL times body weight in kilograms times total body surface area ($TBSA$) percentage burned ($4 \text{ mL} \times \text{kg} \times \%TBSA$).

Front: Over what specific time frame is the volume calculated from the Parkland Formula administered to a burn patient?

Back: The first half is infused over the first 8 hours from the time of injury, and the remaining half is given over the subsequent 16 hours.

Front: What visual mapping tool is utilized to estimate Total Body Surface Area ($TBSA$) burned in adults?

Back: The Wallace Rule of Nines.

Front: According to the Rule of Nines, what percentage of $TBSA$ is attributed to the entire anterior trunk?

Back: 18 percent ($18\%$).

Front: What is the target urine output guide for an adult patient being resuscitated for severe electrical burns?

Back: 1.0 to 1.5 mL per kilogram per hour ($1.0 - 1.5 \text{ mL/kg/hr}$) to clear the risk of acute tubular necrosis from myoglobinuria.

Front: What immediate intervention must be performed for a circumferential full-thickness burn of an extremity causing distal ischemia?

Back: Emergent bedside escharotomy down to subcutaneous fat to relieve mechanical compartment pressure.

Front: Why are pregnant trauma patients prone to deceptive vital signs during early hemorrhagic shock?

Back: Normal gestational hypervolemia allows them to lose up to 35% of blood volume before showing signs of hypotension.

Front: What positional maneuver prevents supine hypotensive syndrome in a third-trimester pregnant trauma patient on a backboard?

Back: Left lateral uterine displacement by tilting the board 15 to 30 degrees or manually displacing the uterus to the left.

Front: Why is the baseline respiratory rate naturally higher and functional residual capacity lower in a pregnant patient?

Back: The enlarging pregnant uterus physically elevates the diaphragm, reducing thoracic volume.

Front: What is the leading cause of fetal death following blunt maternal abdominal trauma?

Back: Placental abruption, often secondary to rapid shearing forces at the uterine wall.

Front: What laboratory test is indicated in all Rh-negative pregnant trauma patients to detect maternal-fetal hemorrhage?

Back: The Kleihauer-Betke test.

Front: How does the pediatric cardiovascular system respond uniquely to acute blood loss compared to adults?

Back: Children maintain normal blood pressure via intense vasoconstriction until severe decompensation occurs, leading to sudden, catastrophic cardiovascular collapse.



Front: What target physiological state defines Abdominal Compartment Syndrome?

Back: A sustained intra-abdominal pressure greater than 20 mmHg associated with new onset organ dysfunction.

Front: How is intra-abdominal pressure measured indirectly at the bedside?

Back: Transurethral measurement of urinary bladder pressure via a standard Foley catheter setup.

Front: What is the definitive management for refractory abdominal compartment syndrome?

Back: Emergent surgical decompression via reopening or performing an open laparotomy.

Front: What core diagnosis must be considered in a trauma patient who develops severe tachypnea, petechiae on the axillae/neck, and confusion 48 hours after a femur fracture?

Back: Fat Embolism Syndrome.

Front: What is the definitive management for a stable retrohepatic vena cava injury discovered during trauma surgery?

Back: Packing the area tightly and leaving it alone, as direct surgical exploration carries an extremely high intraoperative mortality rate.

Front: What is the primary operative approach used to expose injuries located at the bifurcation of the abdominal aorta or iliac vessels?

Back: Direct transperitoneal midline laparotomy with right-to-left medial visceral rotation.



Front: What organs are most vulnerable to primary blast injuries caused by the initial explosive overpressure wave?

Back: Gas-containing organs, specifically the tympanic membranes, lungs, and gastrointestinal tract.

Front: What constitutes a secondary blast injury according to trauma terminology?

Back: Injuries resulting from flying debris or shrapnel striking the patient.

Front: What is a tertiary blast injury?

Back: Injuries sustained when the patient's body is physically thrown by the blast wind against an environmental object or the ground.

Front: What lethal arrhythmia can be induced by aggressive, rough handling of a severely hypothermic patient?

Back: Ventricular fibrillation.

Front: What criteria must be satisfied to definitively declare a hypothermic cardiac arrest patient dead?

Back: The patient must be aggressively rewarmed to a normothermic core body temperature without return of life ("Not dead until warm and dead").



Front: What specific operative maneuver involves rotating the right colon and duodenum medially to expose the retroperitoneal structures?

Back: The Cattell-Braasch maneuver (right-sided medial visceral rotation).

Front: What operative maneuver involves rotating the spleen, pancreas, and left colon medially to expose the entire abdominal aorta?

Back: The Mattox maneuver (left-sided medial visceral rotation).

Front: What temporary maneuver is used to control hepatic hemorrhage by manually compressing the hepatoduodenal ligament?

Back: The Pringle maneuver.

Front: What structures are compressed during a Pringle maneuver?

Back: The portal vein, hepatic artery proper, and the common bile duct.

Front: If bleeding continues after a Pringle maneuver, what is the likely source of the intra-abdominal hemorrhage?

Back: The hepatic veins or the retrohepatic inferior vena cava.

Front: What diagnostic modality is preferred for evaluating a stable patient with suspected penetrating injury to the tangential flank or back?

Back: Triple-contrast CT scan (intravenous, oral, and rectal contrast).

Front: What criteria must be met to safely manage a pelvic fracture hematoma with angioembolization instead of operative exploration?

Back: Absence of an associated hollow viscus or rectourinary injury, and hemodynamic stability or stabilization following pelvic binding.



Front: What is the most common cause of shock in a pediatric trauma patient?

Back: Hemorrhage, though children maintain a normal blood pressure much longer than adults through intense compensatory vasoconstriction.

Front: What is the initial fluid resuscitation bolus volume for a pediatric trauma patient in hemorrhagic shock?

Back: 20 mL per kilogram ($20 \text{ mL/kg}$) of warmed isotonic crystalloid.

Front: If a pediatric trauma patient remains unstable after two crystalloid boluses, what is the next step?

Back: Transfusion of 10 to 20 mL per kilogram ($10 - 20 \text{ mL/kg}$) of packed red blood cells.

Front: What unique pediatric condition presents as spinal cord injury signs on physical exam without any corresponding fractures on X-ray or CT?

Back: SCIWRA (Spinal Cord Injury Without Radiologic Abnormality).

Front: What anatomical feature predisposes pediatric patients to a higher incidence of intra-abdominal organ injury from blunt trauma compared to adults?

Back: A compliant, flexible rib cage and less developed abdominal wall musculature offering less physical protection.



Front: What injury should be highly suspected when a patient presents with a left-sided hemothorax, pneumomediastinum, and severe chest pain after forceful vomiting or rapid deceleration?

Back: Traumatic esophageal perforation (or Boerhaave-like mechanism in trauma).

Front: What classic diagnostic finding on pleural fluid analysis confirms an isolated traumatic diaphragmatic or esophageal leak?

Back: Elevated pleural fluid amylase levels or gross enteric contents.

Front: What is the preferred diagnostic test to evaluate for a suspected blunt cardiac injury in a patient with an abnormal screening ECG?

Back: Transthoracic or transesophageal echocardiogram to evaluate for wall motion abnormalities or pericardial fluid.

Front: What is the diagnostic significance of a persistent, large air leak after placing a correctly positioned chest tube for a pneumothorax?

Back: It strongly indicates a major tracheobronchial tree injury.

Front: What specific operative approach is mandated for a patient presenting with an impaled object in the abdomen or thorax?

Back: Extraction exclusively under direct visualization in the operating room during formal exploration, never in the emergency bay.



Front: What clinical signs definitively mandate early, prophylactic endotracheal intubation in a burn patient?

Back: Strident respirations, severe facial burns, singed nasal hairs, or carbonaceous sputum indicating imminent airway edema.

Front: What toxic gas exposure must be suspected in any patient rescued from an enclosed space fire who presents with a normal oxygen saturation ($SaO_2$) but severe metabolic acidosis?

Back: Carbon monoxide ($CO$) poisoning or Hydrogen Cyanide ($HCN$) poisoning.

Front: Why is standard pulse oximetry unreliable in a patient suffering from acute carbon monoxide poisoning?

Back: Pulse oximeters cannot distinguish between carboxyhemoglobin and normal oxyhemoglobin, yielding falsely high $SaO_2$ readings.

Front: What is the definitive diagnostic test to confirm carbon monoxide poisoning?

Back: An arterial or venous blood gas measuring direct carboxyhemoglobin ($COHb$) levels.

Front: What is the first-line treatment for a patient with confirmed or highly suspected carbon monoxide poisoning?

Back: 100% high-flow supplemental oxygen via a non-rebreather mask to displace carbon monoxide from hemoglobin.

Front: What specific antidote therapy should be administered to a burn patient with suspected concurrent cyanide toxicity?

Back: Hydroxocobalamin (Cyanokit).

Front: What parameter determines the adequacy of fluid resuscitation in the first 24 hours of burn management?

Back: Hourly urine output tracking, not the total volume infused or blood pressure values.



Front: What metabolic derangement occurs as a consequence of massive packed red blood cell transfusions due to the preservative used?

Back: Hypocalcemia, caused by citrate binding to ionized calcium.

Front: What EKG abnormality should be monitored for during a massive transfusion protocol?

Back: QT prolongation secondary to citrate-induced hypocalcemia.

Front: What is the standard prophylactic medication class given to trauma patients to prevent deep vein thrombosis ($DVT$) once active hemorrhage is controlled?

Back: Low-molecular-weight heparin (e.g., Enoxaparin).

Front: What classic presentation suggests a missed hollow viscus injury 48 hours following a negative initial exploratory laparotomy?

Back: Unexplained, worsening tachycardia, low-grade fever, and a developing leukocytosis.

Front: What is the definitive management for a stable patient found to have a small, asymptomatic, isolated Zone 2 retroperitoneal hematoma after blunt trauma?

Back: Non-operative observation with serial abdominal exams.

Front: What fatal phenomenon can occur if an asymptomatic, stable patient with a mild concussion returns to sports prematurely and sustains a minor secondary head injury?

Back: Second-impact syndrome, leading to rapid, catastrophic cerebral edema and high mortality.

Front: What type of injury is characterized by a high-pressure injection mechanism (e.g., paint sprayers) into a digit, requiring immediate surgical decompression despite a benign initial appearance?

Back: High-pressure injection injury, an absolute surgical emergency due to rapid chemical tracking and compartment necrosis.

Section:  General Surgery

Topic: Surgical oncology basics

Front: What is the defining biological difference between a benign and a malignant tumor?
Back: Malignant tumors possess the capacity to invade local tissues and metastasize to distant sites.

Front: What process describes the programmed cell death that is typically evaded by cancer cells?
Back: Apoptosis.

Front: Which tumor suppressor gene is most commonly mutated across all human cancers?
Back: TP53 (encoding the p53 protein).

Front: What is the role of the p53 protein in normal cell biology?
Back: It arrests the cell cycle at the G1/S checkpoint to allow for DNA repair or triggers apoptosis if damage is irreversible.

Front: Name the proto-oncogene commonly mutated in pancreatic adenocarcinoma and colorectal cancer.
Back: KRAS.

Front: What is the primary mechanism of the Warburg effect in cancer metabolism?
Back: Cancer cells preferentially utilize inefficient anaerobic glycolysis for energy even in the presence of abundant oxygen.

Front: What is the term for the formation of new blood vessels that tumors initiate to sustain growth?
Back: Angiogenesis.

Front: Which growth factor is primarily responsible for driving tumor angiogenesis?
Back: Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF).

Front: What property allows cancer cells to replicate indefinitely without undergoing cellular aging?
Back: Reactivation of telomerase, which maintains chromosomal end lengths.

Front: What is the name of the process where epithelial tumor cells gain migratory properties to invade tissues?
Back: Epithelial-to-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT).

Front: In the classical multi-step model of colon carcinogenesis, what is typically the initial genetic mutation?
Back: Mutation or inactivation of the APC tumor suppressor gene.

Front: Name the classic mechanism of the "Two-Hit Hypothesis" for tumor suppressor genes.
Back: Both alleles of a tumor suppressor gene must be inactivated or mutated to lose its protective function.

Front: Give an example of a hereditary cancer syndrome that perfectly illustrates the Two-Hit Hypothesis.
Back: Hereditary Retinoblastoma (RB1 mutation) or Familial Adenomatous Polyposis (APC mutation).

Front: What does the standard "T" represent in the AJCC TNM staging system?
Back: The size or depth of local invasion of the primary tumor.

Front: What does the "N" represent in the AJCC TNM staging system?
Back: The presence, number, or location of regional lymph node metastases.

Front: What does the "M" represent in the AJCC TNM staging system?
Back: The presence or absence of distant metastatic disease.

Front: What is the key distinction between clinical staging (cTNM) and pathologic staging (pTNM)?
Back: Clinical staging is based on physical exams and imaging, while pathologic staging incorporates findings from surgical resection and tissue pathology.

Front: Define tumor "grading."
Back: The microscopic assessment of cellular differentiation and mitotic activity, indicating how much the tumor resembles normal tissue.

Front: Define tumor "staging."
Back: The clinical and pathologic assessment of the anatomic extent and spread of the cancer within the host.

Front: Does tumor grade or tumor stage generally carry more prognostic weight in solid tumors?
Back: Tumor stage.

Front: What is an "in situ" carcinoma (Tis)?
Back: A malignant epithelial tumor that has not breached the basement membrane.

Front: What is a sarcoma?
Back: A malignant tumor originating from mesenchymal (connective) tissue, such as bone, fat, or muscle.

Front: What is a carcinoma?
Back: A malignant tumor originating from epithelial tissue.

Front: What suffix is universally applied to benign tumors of mesenchymal origin?
Back: "-oma" (e.g., lipoma, fibroma, osteoma).

Front: Name two notable exceptions where the suffix "-oma" indicates a highly malignant tumor instead of a benign one.
Back: Melanoma, lymphoma, mesothelioma, and seminoma.

Front: What is the main structural disadvantage of a Fine Needle Aspiration (FNA) biopsy?
Back: It provides only cytological details (cells) and lacks tissue architecture, making it unable to distinguish in situ from invasive carcinoma.

Front: For which specific surgical oncology pathology is FNA explicitly the first-line diagnostic modality of choice?
Back: Thyroid nodules.

Front: What is the main advantage of a Core Needle Biopsy over an FNA?
Back: It preserves histological tissue architecture, allowing for assessment of tissue invasion, grading, and receptor status.

Front: Define an "incisional" biopsy.
Back: The surgical removal of only a small, representative wedge or piece of a large mass for diagnosis.

Front: Define an "excisional" biopsy.
Back: The complete surgical removal of an entire suspicious mass or lesion along with a small margin of normal tissue for diagnosis.

Front: Under what clinical circumstance is an incisional biopsy preferred over an excisional biopsy?
Back: When the mass is very large or located in an anatomically sensitive area where total removal would cause structural or cosmetic deformity before a definitive diagnosis is established.

Front: Why is it critical to orient biopsy skin or fascial incisions along standard surgical lines of resection?
Back: To ensure that the entire previous biopsy tract can be easily excised during the definitive wide local therapeutic resection.

Front: What is the major risk associated with biopsying a suspected retroperitoneal sarcoma via a percutaneous transperitoneal approach?
Back: Tumor seeding along the needle track, which can cause local peritoneal recurrence.

Front: What is the gold standard biopsy type for a highly suspicious, accessible soft tissue mass suspected to be an extremity sarcoma?
Back: Longitudinal core needle biopsy.

Front: Why must biopsy incisions on the extremities for suspected sarcomas always be oriented longitudinally rather than transversely?
Back: Transverse incisions compromise the anatomical compartments, forcing a much wider, more morbid definitive resection or amputation.

Front: What preoperative score evaluates a surgical cancer patient's functional capabilities and tolerance for aggressive surgery?
Back: ECOG Performance Status or Karnofsky Performance Status score.

Front: What constitutes an "R0" surgical resection?
Back: Microscopically negative margins, meaning no tumor cells are present at the inked margins of the resected specimen.

Front: What constitutes an "R1" surgical resection?
Back: Microscopically positive margins, where tumor cells are visible under the microscope at the edge of the resection line despite looking clear to the surgeon's naked eye.

Front: What constitutes an "R2" surgical resection?
Back: Macroscopically positive margins, where visible, gross residual tumor is intentionally or unintentionally left behind in the surgical field.

Front: What is the overarching clinical goal of "curative" oncologic surgery?
Back: Complete surgical extirpation of all known tumor mass with documented R0 microscopic negative margins.

Front: Define "palliative" cancer surgery.
Back: Surgery performed not to extend life or cure the disease, but strictly to alleviate severe symptoms like bowel obstruction, intractable pain, or massive hemorrhage.

Front: Give a classic example of a palliative surgical procedure in advanced gastric or pancreatic cancer.
Back: A palliative gastrojejunostomy (surgical bypass) to relieve gastric outlet obstruction.

Front: What technique allows a surgeon to obtain rapid, intraoperative microscopic margin assessments while the patient is still under anesthesia?
Back: Frozen section analysis.

Front: Why can frozen section analysis occasionally yield false negatives compared to permanent sections?
Back: Ice crystal artifacts can distort cellular morphology, and sampling limitations mean focal positive margins can be missed.

Front: What does the concept of a "radial margin" mean in surgical oncology specimens?
Back: The deepest perpendicular margin of the specimen, measuring how close the tumor comes to the underlying deep fascial or anatomical boundary.

Front: What is the physiological definition of a "Sentinel Lymph Node"?
Back: The very first lymph node (or group of nodes) that receives direct lymphatic drainage from a primary tumor site.

Front: What is the underlying oncologic rationale for performing a Sentinel Lymph Node Biopsy (SLNB)?
Back: If the sentinel node is completely free of tumor cells, the remaining regional lymph nodes are highly likely to be negative, sparing the patient a morbid full node dissection.

Front: For which two classic malignancies is Sentinel Lymph Node Biopsy a standard, first-line staging procedure?
Back: Breast cancer and cutaneous melanoma.

Front: Name the two standard tracing agents co-injected around a primary tumor to locate the sentinel lymph node intraoperatively.
Back: Technetium-99m (Tc-99m) radiocolloid and isosulfan/methylene blue dye.

Front: What intraoperative tool does the surgical oncologist use to detect the radiocolloid tracer in the axilla or groin?
Back: A handheld gamma probe.

Front: What absolute contraindication prevents the use of a sentinel lymph node biopsy in a breast cancer patient?
Back: Palpable, biopsy-proven positive axillary lymph nodes identified preoperatively.

Front: What is the most common, long-term chronic complication associated with a formal, full axillary or inguinal lymph node dissection?
Back: Chronic lymphedema of the affected extremity.

Front: Define "radical lymphadenectomy."
Back: The complete, systematic anatomical clearance of all regional lymph nodes within a defined anatomical compartment.

Front: Define "neoadjuvant" therapy.
Back: Systemic therapy (chemotherapy) or radiation administered before definitive surgical resection.

Front: What is the main clinical objective of neoadjuvant therapy?
Back: To shrink or "downstage" a large tumor, making it technically resectable or converting a radical procedure into a organ-sparing one.

Front: Define "adjuvant" therapy.
Back: Systemic therapy, immunotherapy, or radiation administered after complete surgical resection.

Front: What is the main clinical objective of adjuvant therapy?
Back: To eradicate occult micro-metastatic disease left behind after surgery, thereby reducing the rate of systemic recurrence.

Front: What is the primary biological mechanism of action of external beam radiation therapy?
Back: It induces lethal DNA damage, primarily via oxygen free radical production, leading to mitotic death of rapidly dividing cells.

Front: Why is radiation therapy considered a localized treatment modality rather than a systemic one?
Back: Its therapeutic effects and toxic side effects are strictly confined to the specific anatomical field targeted by the beams.

Front: What is a major wound healing concern when operating on a patient who has received prior high-dose neoadjuvant radiation?
Back: Delayed or compromised wound healing, tissue fibrosis, and high rates of anastomotic leaks due to radiation-induced microvascular damage.

Front: How long should a surgeon ideally wait to operate after a patient finishes neoadjuvant radiation therapy?
Back: Typically 6 to 8 weeks, allowing acute inflammatory changes to subside while avoiding the onset of dense, chronic tissue fibrosis.

Front: What is "brachytherapy"?
Back: The internal temporary or permanent implantation of radioactive sources directly into or immediately adjacent to a tumor site.

Front: Give an example of an organ-preserving surgery made standard by combining surgical resection with adjuvant radiation.
Back: Breast-conserving surgery (lumpectomy) followed by whole-breast radiation therapy, which yields survival rates equivalent to a total mastectomy.

Front: What is the main utility of serum tumor markers in standard oncologic practice?
Back: Monitoring a patient's response to treatment and tracking early cancer recurrence postoperatively.

Front: Why are most serum tumor markers considered poor tools for general cancer screening in asymptomatic populations?
Back: They lack the necessary sensitivity and specificity, often leading to false positives from benign inflammatory conditions.

Front: Name the primary serum tumor marker used to monitor colorectal adenocarcinoma.
Back: Carcoembryonic Antigen (CEA).

Front: Name the primary serum tumor marker elevated in pancreatic adenocarcinoma.
Back: Carbohydrate Antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9).

Front: Name the primary serum tumor marker elevated in epithelial ovarian cancer.
Back: Cancer Antigen 125 (CA-125).

Front: Name the tumor marker used to monitor breast cancer recurrence.
Back: CA 15-3 or CA 27-29.

Front: Which two serum tumor markers are classic diagnostic and surveillance indicators for non-seminomatous testicular cancer?
Back: Alpha-Fetoprotein (AFP) and Human Chorionic Gonadotropin (b-hCG).

Front: Which specific testicular malignancy is classically associated with an elevated b-hCG but a normal AFP?
Back: Seminoma.

Front: Besides testicular cancer, which primary malignancy is strongly associated with a markedly elevated Alpha-Fetoprotein (AFP)?
Back: Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC).

Front: What benign condition can cause a transient, mild elevation of serum CEA?
Back: Cigarette smoking or active inflammatory bowel disease.

Front: What benign condition can cause a false-positive elevation of serum CA 19-9?
Back: Biliary obstruction, acute cholangitis, or cirrhosis.

Front: Define the clinical term "lead-time bias" in cancer screening programs.
Back: The illusion of prolonged survival caused merely by detecting a disease earlier in its natural history without actually altering the ultimate time of death.

Front: Define the clinical term "length-time bias" in cancer screening programs.
Back: The tendency of screening tests to disproportionately detect slower-growing, less aggressive tumors, making the screening program look more effective than it is.

Front: What is the most common histological type of invasive breast cancer?
Back: Invasive Ductal Carcinoma (IDC).

Front: What is the classic mammographic finding that prompts a stereotactic core biopsy for suspected DCIS?
Back: Clustered, pleomorphic microcalcifications.

Front: What anatomical landmark dictates the boundaries of a standard Level I, II, and III axillary lymph node dissection?
Back: The Pectoralis Minor muscle.

Front: Where are Level I axillary lymph nodes located relative to the pectoralis minor muscle?
Back: Lateral and inferior to the lower border of the pectoralis minor muscle.

Front: Where are Level II axillary lymph nodes located relative to the pectoralis minor muscle?
Back: Directly posterior to the pectoralis minor muscle.

Front: Where are Level III axillary lymph nodes located relative to the pectoralis minor muscle?
Back: Medial and superior to the upper border of the pectoralis minor muscle.

Front: What nerve is at risk of injury during an axillary lymph node dissection, presenting postoperatively as a "winged scapula"?
Back: The Long Thoracic Nerve (which innervates the Serratus Anterior muscle).

Front: Injury to the Thoracodorsal Nerve during axillary dissection causes weakness in which specific arm movement?
Back: Adduction, extension, and internal rotation of the shoulder due to paralysis of the Latissimus Dorsi muscle.

Front: What is the primary hormonal therapy option for a premenopausal woman with ER/PR positive breast cancer?
Back: Tamoxifen (a Selective Estrogen Receptor Modulator).

Front: What is the primary hormonal therapy class used for a postmenopausal woman with ER/PR positive breast cancer?
Back: Aromatase Inhibitors (e.g., Anastrozole, Letrozole).

Front: Why are Aromatase Inhibitors ineffective in premenopausal women?
Back: They cannot inhibit the major source of estrogen production from functioning, cycling ovaries.

Front: Name the monoclonal antibody directed specifically against the HER2/neu receptor in overexpressing breast cancers.
Back: Trastuzumab.

Front: What screening or monitoring test must be performed regularly on patients receiving Trastuzumab?
Back: Echocardiogram or MUGA scan to monitor for drug-induced, reversible cardiomyopathy (drop in LEF).

Front: What is the standard macroscopic surgical margin required for a curative resection of a colon cancer?
Back: A minimum of 5 centimeters of normal bowel proximal and distal to the tumor edge.

Front: Why must the regional mesentery be resected intact along with the bowel segment in colon cancer surgery?
Back: To ensure full clearance of the regional lymph nodes that follow the primary mesenteric arterial supply.

Front: What is the minimum number of lymph nodes that must be pathologically examined to accurately stage a colon cancer specimen?
Back: A minimum of 12 lymph nodes.

Front: What surgical margin boundary is considered acceptable for a low rectal cancer resection where 5 cm is anatomically impossible?
Back: A 1 to 2 centimeter distal intramural margin.

Front: What is the name of the surgical technique that involves sharp, precise dissection of the fascial plane enveloping the rectum and its lymph nodes?
Back: Total Mesorectal Excision (TME).

Front: What is the major long-term structural or functional risk of damaging the pelvic autonomic nerves during a TME?
Back: Urinary retention and erectile/ejaculatory dysfunction.

Front: When is an Abdominoperineal Resection (APR) indicated for rectal cancer instead of a Low Anterior Resection (LAR)?
Back: When the tumor directly invades the external anal sphincter mechanism or levator ani muscles, preventing a functional anastomosis.

Front: What permanent structural change results from an Abdominoperineal Resection (APR)?
Back: Closure of the anus and creation of a permanent, lifelong end-colostomy.

Front: Where is the most common site of distant hematogenous metastasis for colon cancer?
Back: The liver, via the portal venous drainage system.

Front: Where does a lower rectal cancer preferentially metastasize hematogenously if it bypasses the portal system?
Back: The lungs, via the inferior rectal veins draining directly into the internal iliac veins and inferior vena cava.

Front: What is the single most important prognostic factor for localized cutaneous melanoma?
Back: Breslow thickness (measured in millimeters from the granular layer of the epidermis to the deepest point of invasion).

Front: What Breslow thickness threshold serves as the standard indication to offer a Sentinel Lymph Node Biopsy?
Back: A Breslow thickness greater than or equal to 1.0 mm (or greater than or equal to 0.8 mm if high-risk features like ulceration are present).

Front: What is the required surgical margin for a wide local excision of a melanoma in situ?
Back: A 0.5 to 1.0 centimeter margin of normal skin.

Front: What is the required surgical margin for a melanoma with a Breslow thickness less than or equal to 1.0 mm?
Back: A 1.0 centimeter surgical margin.

Front: What is the required surgical margin for a melanoma with a Breslow thickness greater than 2.0 mm?
Back: A 2.0 centimeter surgical margin.

Front: What is the classic route of metastatic spread for soft tissue sarcomas?
Back: Hematogenous spread, preferentially metastasizing directly to the lungs.

Front: Name the notable exception among sarcomas that frequently metastasizes to regional lymph nodes.
Back: Epithelioid sarcoma, clear cell sarcoma, synovial sarcoma, and rhabdomyosarcoma.

Front: What is the standard primary treatment for a resectable, high-grade extremity soft tissue sarcoma?
Back: Surgical resection with wide negative margins combined with radiation therapy to preserve the limb.

Front: What is the most common histological type of thyroid cancer?
Back: Papillary Thyroid Carcinoma.

Front: What are the pathognomonic microscopic findings of Papillary Thyroid Carcinoma on biopsy?
Back: Orphan Annie eye nuclear inclusions and Psammoma bodies.

Front: From which specific cell type does Medullary Thyroid Carcinoma arise?
Back: Parafollicular C-cells, which secrete calcitonin.

Front: Medullary Thyroid Carcinoma is a hallmark component of which autosomal dominant hereditary syndromes?
Back: MEN 2A and MEN 2B (Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia type 2).

Front: What life-threatening endocrine tumor must always be ruled out or resected before managing a thyroid mass in a patient with MEN 2?
Back: Pheochromocytoma.

Front: What is the classic clinical presentation of an insulinoma?
Back: Whipple's Triad: documented hypoglycemia symptoms, concurrent low serum glucose (less than 50 mg/dL), and immediate symptom relief with glucose administration.

Front: Where are the vast majority of Gastrinomas located anatomically?
Back: Inside the Gastrinoma Triangle (bounded by the confluence of the cystic/common bile duct, duodenum, and neck of the pancreas).

Front: Mutation of which mismatch repair genes leads to Lynch Syndrome (Hereditary Non-Polyposis Colorectal Cancer)?
Back: MLH1, MSH2, MSH3, MSH6, or PMS2.

Front: What is the classic screening recommendation for a patient diagnosed with Familial Adenomatous Polyposis (FAP)?
Back: Annual flexible sigmoidoscopy or colonoscopy starting at age 10 to 12, followed by prophylactic total proctocolectomy because colon cancer risk is 100%.

Front: Lynch syndrome places women at a dramatically increased risk for which extra-colonic malignancy?
Back: Endometrial carcinoma.

Front: What gene mutation is responsible for Li-Fraumeni syndrome?
Back: Germline mutation of the TP53 tumor suppressor gene.

Front: What constellation of malignancies characterizes Li-Fraumeni syndrome?
Back: Early-onset sarcomas, breast cancer, brain tumors, leukemia, and adrenocortical carcinomas.

Front: What genetic mutation causes Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia Type 2 (MEN 2)?
Back: Activating germline mutation of the RET proto-oncogene.

Front: What specific surgical recommendation is given to children found to carry a high-risk RET proto-oncogene mutation?
Back: Prophylactic total thyroidectomy during early childhood to prevent inevitable medullary thyroid carcinoma.

Front: What is the name of the localized hepatic therapy where a radiologist delivers high-dose chemotherapy directly into the hepatic artery supplying a tumor?
Back: Transarterial Chemoembolization (TACE).

Front: Why can the liver tolerate hepatic artery embolization during TACE without undergoing massive parenchymal necrosis?
Back: Normal liver parenchyma gets 75% of its blood supply from the portal vein, whereas hepatic tumors derive 90% of their supply from the hepatic artery.

Front: Define "Hyperthermic Intraperitoneal Chemotherapy" (HIPEC).
Back: The instillation of heated, high-dose chemotherapy solution directly into the peritoneal cavity immediately following complete surgical debulking of peritoneal surface malignancies.

Front: What oncologic emergency occurs when a massive number of tumor cells are rapidly destroyed by systemic therapy, releasing their intracellular contents?
Back: Tumor Lysis Syndrome.

Front: What are the hallmark laboratory abnormalities found in acute Tumor Lysis Syndrome?
Back: Hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hyperuricemia, and hypocalcemia.

Front: What is the primary mechanism of renal failure in untreated Tumor Lysis Syndrome?
Back: Precipitation of uric acid crystals and calcium phosphate crystals inside the renal tubules.

Front: What prophylactic medication is administered to lower uric acid production in patients at high risk for Tumor Lysis Syndrome?
Back: Allopurinol or Rasburicase.

Front: What immediate surgical treatment is indicated for Superior Vena Cava (SVC) Syndrome caused by an un-resectable, compressing mediastinal tumor?
Back: Endovascular SVC stenting combined with emergent radiation therapy or chemotherapy.

Front: What is the classic descriptive sign of a metastatic gastric cancer that drops cells down to seed one or both ovaries?
Back: A Krukenberg tumor.

Front: What sign describes a palpable, firm nodule in the umbilicus representing metastatic intra-abdominal malignancy?
Back: Sister Mary Joseph nodule.

Front: What sign describes a palpable, hard shelf felt on digital rectal exam indicating drop-metastases in the rectouterine or rectovesical pouch?
Back: Blumer's shelf.

Front: What is a "Desmoid tumor"?
Back: A locally aggressive, non-metastasizing fibroblastic tumor arising from musculoaponeurotic structures, frequently associated with Gardner's Syndrome (FAP subtype).

Front: What is the definitive treatment of choice for a symptomatic, localized Desmoid tumor of the abdominal wall if resectable?
Back: Wide local excision with negative margins, though recurrence rates remain high.

Front: What is the primary systemic treatment for an un-resectable or metastatic Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumor (GIST)?
Back: Imatinib (a tyrosine kinase inhibitor targeting the KIT/CD117 receptor).

Front: Why are standard core or incisional biopsies of suspected GISTs avoided if the tumor is safely resectable?
Back: GISTs are highly friable, and biopsying them carries a high risk of tumor rupture, hemorrhage, and peritoneal dissemination.

Front: What is the required surgical margin for a standard Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumor (GIST) resection?
Back: Segmental resection with clear margins; formal lymphadenectomy is unnecessary because GISTs rarely metastasize via lymphatics.

Front: What is the classic imaging finding on a chest CT that suggests a lung nodule is benign rather than malignant?
Back: A diffuse, central, or "popcorn-like" pattern of calcification.

Front: What size threshold for a solitary pulmonary nodule matches a significantly higher probability of malignancy, requiring further workup?
Back: A nodule diameter greater than 8 millimeters or 1 centimeter, especially in a patient with a smoking history.

Front: What is the absolute contraindication to breast-conserving surgery (lumpectomy) in a patient with early-stage breast cancer?
Back: Prior radiation therapy to the same breast or multicentric disease (cancer in multiple quadrants).

Front: What is the surgical management for a patient with biopsy-proven Ductal Carcinoma In Situ (DCIS) found throughout multiple quadrants of the breast?
Back: Total mastectomy (simple mastectomy) without axillary lymph node dissection.

Front: Why is an axillary lymph node dissection or sentinel node biopsy generally skipped in pure DCIS?
Back: DCIS is by definition non-invasive and lacks access to the lymphatic channels in the basement membrane.

Front: Under what condition is a sentinel lymph node biopsy performed during a mastectomy for DCIS?
Back: When a massive area of DCIS requires a total mastectomy, because if invasive cancer is found on final pathology, a sentinel loop can no longer be mapped later.

Front: What defines a "Modified Radical Mastectomy"?
Back: Removal of the entire breast tissue, skin-axillary tail complex, and a formal Level I/II axillary lymph node dissection, while sparing the pectoralis major muscle.

Front: What defines a "Radical Mastectomy" (Halsted Mastectomy), and is it commonly performed today?
Back: Removal of the breast, axillary nodes, and pectoralis major/minor muscles; it is virtually never performed today due to equivalent survival with less morbid options.

Front: What is the classic clinical presentation of Inflammatory Breast Cancer?
Back: Erythema, warmth, and skin edema mimicking mastitis, presenting as "peau d'orange" (orange peel skin) due to tumor emboli blocking dermal lymphatics.

Front: What is the initial step in the management of highly suspected or biopsy-proven Inflammatory Breast Cancer?
Back: Neoadjuvant systemic chemotherapy followed by modified radical mastectomy and adjuvant radiation (never upfront surgery).

Front: What is Paget's disease of the breast?
Back: A malignant intraepithelial adenocarcinoma condition presenting as a raw, scaling, or ulcerated lesion of the nipple-areola complex.

Front: Paget's disease of the breast is almost universally an indicator of what underlying pathology?
Back: An underlying ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) or invasive ductal carcinoma deeper within the breast tissue.

Front: A patient presents with a newly diagnosed right-sided cecal adenocarcinoma. What standard surgical resection is indicated?
Back: Right hemicolectomy, including the ligation of the ileocolic and right colic arteries at their origins.

Front: A patient presents with an adenocarcinoma located in the splenic flexure. What standard surgical resection is indicated?
Back: Left hemicolectomy, including the ligation of the left colic artery and branches of the inferior mesenteric artery.

Front: What anatomical structure determines the boundary between a colon cancer and a rectal cancer?
Back: The peritoneal reflection, typically located roughly 12 to 15 centimeters from the anal verge.

Front: What is the standard management for a patient presenting with an acute, completely obstructing descending colon cancer without perforation?
Back: Urgent surgical resection (e.g., left colectomy or subtotal colectomy) or endoscopic deployment of a self-expanding metallic stent as a bridge to surgery.

Front: What is the most significant risk associated with trying to primary-anastamose an unprepared, acutely obstructed colon?
Back: High rate of anastomotic leak and subsequent fecal peritonitis.

Front: What surgical procedure involves resecting the diseased bowel, creating a proximal end-colostomy, and sewing closed the distal rectal pouch?
Back: A Hartmann's Procedure.

Front: Under what circumstances is a Hartmann's Procedure favored over a primary anastomosis in colorectal surgery?
Back: In the setting of gross purulent/fecal peritonitis, hemodynamic instability, or severe malnutrition where an anastomosis will not heal safely.

Front: What is the standard systemic adjuvant chemotherapy regimen utilized for Stage III (node-positive) colon cancer?
Back: FOLFOX (Fluorouracil, Leucovorin, and Oxaliplatin).

Front: What is the most common clinical subtype of cutaneous melanoma?
Back: Superficial spreading melanoma.

Front: Which melanoma subtype is classically found on the palms, soles, or under the nail beds, and is more prevalent in darker-skinned populations?
Back: Acral lentiginous melanoma.

Front: Which melanoma subtype grows rapidly vertically rather than horizontally, presenting as a dark, dome-shaped nodule with an early invasive phase?
Back: Nodular melanoma.

Front: What is the clinical implication of finding microscopic satellitosis (small tumor nests near the primary mass) in a melanoma specimen?
Back: It significantly upgrades the tumor staging and indicates a much higher risk of regional and systemic recurrence.

Front: If a sentinel lymph node biopsy is positive for melanoma micrometastasis, what is the next step according to modern guidelines?
Back: Therapeutic nodal observation with ultrasound and systemic immunotherapy, rather than an immediate completion lymph node dissection.

Front: What is the classic surgical procedure for a resectable adenocarcinoma located in the head of the pancreas?
Back: A Pancreaticoduodenectomy, universally known as the Whipple procedure.

Front: What specific structures are entirely removed during a standard classic Whipple procedure?
Back: The head of the pancreas, duodenum, distal stomach (antrum), gallbladder, and common bile duct.

Front: What is the most common and dangerous life-threatening complication specific to the postoperative course of a Whipple procedure?
Back: Pancreatic anastomotic leak resulting in a pancreatic fistula, which can digest nearby major vessels and cause catastrophic hemorrhage.

Front: What defines a resectable vs. unresectable pancreatic adenocarcinoma on preoperative CT imaging?
Back: Encasement or occlusion of major vascular structures, specifically the superior mesenteric artery (SMA), celiac axis, or portal vein/SMV confluence.

Front: What is the standard surgical approach for an adenocarcinoma located in the distal third of the stomach?
Back: Subtotal gastrectomy with a Roux-en-Y or Billroth II reconstruction and a formal D2 lymph node dissection.

Front: What is the standard surgical approach for a cancer located in the proximal third or cardia of the stomach?
Back: Total gastrectomy with an esophagojejunostomy reconstruction.

Front: What is the most common histological type of esophageal cancer worldwide vs. in the United States?
Back: Squamous cell carcinoma worldwide; Adenocarcinoma in the United States.

Front: What chronic gastrointestinal condition is the primary predisposing driver for esophageal adenocarcinoma?
Back: Chronic gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) leading to Barrett's esophagus (intestinal metaplasia).

Front: What standard surgical procedure is performed for a resectable mid-to-lower esophageal cancer?
Back: Esophagectomy (e.g., Ivor Lewis esophagectomy involving a laparotomy and right thoracotomy).

Front: What underlying liver pathology is present in the vast majority of patients who develop primary Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC) in the West?
Back: Cirrhosis (driven by Hepatitis C, Alcohol, or Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatohepatitis/MASH).

Front: What clinical criteria are used to determine if a cirrhotic patient with hepatocellular carcinoma is a candidate for liver transplantation?
Back: The Milan Criteria: one nodule less than or equal to 5 cm, or up to 3 nodules each less than or equal to 3 cm, with no vascular invasion.

Front: What is a Klatskin tumor?
Back: A cholangiocarcinoma (bile duct cancer) located precisely at the confluence of the right and left hepatic ducts.

Front: How does a patient with a pancreatic head tumor or a Klatskin tumor characteristically present clinically?
Back: Painless, progressive jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, and significant weight loss.

Front: What is Courvoisier's sign?
Back: A palpable, non-tender, distended gallbladder in a jaundiced patient, strongly suggesting malignant obstruction of the common bile duct rather than gallstones.

Front: What is the appropriate surgical management for an incidental gallbladder cancer found on pathology that is confined only to the lamina propria (T1a)?
Back: Simple cholecystectomy (the initial surgery was therapeutic and sufficient).

Front: What is the appropriate surgical management for an incidental gallbladder cancer that invades the muscular layer (T1b or T2)?
Back: Re-operation for a radical cholecystectomy, which includes a wedge resection of liver beds (Segments IVb and V) and regional lymphadenectomy.

Front: A cancer patient presents with severe back pain, lower extremity weakness, and hyperreflexia. What is the immediate next step in management?
Back: Emergent administration of high-dose intravenous corticosteroids (e.g., Dexamethasone) to reduce edema, followed by an urgent spinal MRI.

Front: What are the two primary therapeutic options for definitive management of malignant spinal cord compression?
Back: Emergent surgical decompression (laminectomy) or urgent external beam radiation therapy.

Front: A patient with advanced pelvic malignancy presents with a completely un-resectable mechanical small bowel obstruction. What surgical palliative option can provide symptom relief?
Back: Placement of a venting gastrostomy tube (G-tube) to decompress the stomach and stop intractable vomiting.

Front: What is the metabolic danger of performing a massive surgical debulking or starting chemotherapy on a patient with a functional carcinoid tumor?
Back: Precipitation of a Carcinoid Crisis, causing life-threatening bronchoconstriction, severe hypotension or hypertension, and cardiac arrhythmias.

Front: What specific medication must be given prophylactically and intraoperatively to prevent or treat a Carcinoid Crisis?
Back: Octreotide (a somatostatin analog).

Front: What genetic test must be performed on all patients newly diagnosed with Medullary Thyroid Carcinoma?
Back: Germline RET proto-oncogene mutation testing to rule out hereditary MEN 2 syndromes.

Front: If a patient with thyroid cancer is found to have a concurrent Pheochromocytoma, why must the pheochromocytoma be resected first?
Back: Induction of anesthesia or surgical stress on an untreated pheochromocytoma can trigger a fatal intraoperative hypertensive crisis.

Front: What is the most common site of extra-adrenal pheochromocytoma (paraganglioma)?
Back: The Organ of Zuckerkandl (located near the bifurcation of the aorta).

Front: What is the classic preoperative medical preparation protocol for a patient scheduled to undergo resection of a pheochromocytoma?
Back: Irreversible alpha-blockade (e.g., Phenoxybenzamine) started 10-14 days prior, followed by beta-blockade only after adequate alpha-blockade is established.

Front: Why is it an absolute error to initiate beta-blockage before alpha-blockage in a patient with a pheochromocytoma?
Back: It eliminates beta-2 mediated vasodilation, leaving alpha-1 vasoconstriction unopposed, which triggers a catastrophic hypertensive crisis.

Front: What is the standard surgical treatment for a localized, primary Hyperparathyroidism caused by a parathyroid carcinoma?
Back: En bloc resection of the enlarged parathyroid tumor along with the ipsilateral thyroid lobe and any adherent tissues.

Front: What is the classic presentation of an extremity soft tissue sarcoma?
Back: A painless, progressively enlarging, deep-seated mass that is often larger than 5 cm by the time of presentation.

Front: What is the initial imaging modality of choice for a suspected extremity soft tissue sarcoma?
Back: Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) of the affected limb.

Front: What is the imaging modality of choice for staging and evaluating a suspected retroperitoneal sarcoma?
Back: Computed Tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen and pelvis.

Front: Why is radiation therapy typically administered as part of limb-salvage surgery for high-grade sarcomas?
Back: It significantly decreases local recurrence rates while maintaining equivalent long-term survival compared to radical amputation.

Front: From which specific pacemaker cells of the gastrointestinal tract do Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumors (GIST) originate?
Back: The interstitial cells of Cajal.

Front: What is the most common anatomical location for a Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumor (GIST) within the GI tract?
Back: The stomach (followed by the small intestine).

Front: Where is the most common primary site for a gastrointestinal carcinoid (neuroendocrine) tumor?
Back: The terminal ileum and the appendix.

Front: Why do primary gastrointestinal carcinoid tumors confined to the bowel wall never cause systemic Carcinoid Syndrome?
Back: The tumor's secretory products (like serotonin) are drained by the portal vein and completely metabolized by the liver via first-pass metabolism.

Front: What anatomical change must occur for a gastrointestinal carcinoid tumor to cause systemic Carcinoid Syndrome?
Back: The tumor must metastasize to the liver (or bypass the portal circulation), allowing serotonin to directly enter the systemic hepatic veins.

Front: What are the classic clinical symptoms of systemic Carcinoid Syndrome?
Back: Episodic cutaneous flushing, secretory diarrhea, bronchoconstriction (wheezing), and right-sided cardiac valvular fibrosis.

Front: Why does carcinoid heart disease preferentially affect the right side of the heart (tricuspid and pulmonary valves) rather than the left?
Back: Pulmonary monoamine oxidase (MAO) metabolizes and inactivates serotonin before the blood reaches the left atrium.

Front: What is the key diagnostic laboratory test used to screen for and confirm a suspected carcinoid tumor?
Back: 24-hour urinary measurement of 5-Hydroxyindoleacetic Acid (5-HIAA), a breakdown product of serotonin.

Front: What is the surgical management for an incidental carcinoid tumor found at the tip of the appendix that is less than 1 to 2 centimeters in size?
Back: A standard simple appendectomy is fully curative.

Front: Under what condition does an incidental appendiceal carcinoid tumor require a formal right hemicolectomy instead of a simple appendectomy?
Back: If the tumor is greater than 2 centimeters, involves the base of the appendix, or shows evidence of mesoappendiceal invasion.

Front: What is the most common type of skin cancer globally?
Back: Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC).

Front: Describe the classic visual presentation of a nodular Basal Cell Carcinoma.
Back: A pearly, translucent papule with central ulceration, rolled borders, and prominent telangiectasias ("rodent ulcer").

Front: What specialized surgical technique offers the highest cure rate and maximum tissue conservation for facial Basal Cell Carcinomas?
Back: Mohs Micrographic Surgery.

Front: How does Mohs Micrographic Surgery differ fundamentally from standard wide local excision?
Back: The surgeon removes tissue layers and maps them microscopically in sequential stages intraoperatively until 100% of the margins are completely clear.

Front: Which precursor skin lesion frequently progresses to invasive Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)?
Back: Actinic Keratosis.

Front: What is a Marjolin’s ulcer?
Back: An aggressive, highly invasive Squamous Cell Carcinoma that develops within a chronic, non-healing wound, burn scar, or long-standing osteomyelitis sinus tract.

Front: What is the standard macroscopic surgical margin required for a low-risk, small Basal Cell Carcinoma?
Back: A 4 millimeter margin of normal skin.

Front: What is the standard macroscopic surgical margin required for a standard cutaneous Squamous Cell Carcinoma?
Back: A 4 to 6 millimeter margin of normal skin.

Front: What does a "sentinel loop" on an abdominal X-ray traditionally signify in surgical pathology?
Back: A localized, dilated segment of bowel reflecting focal ileus due to nearby severe acute inflammation (e.g., acute pancreatitis or acute appendicitis).

Front: What is the name of the aggressive, malignant neuroendocrine tumor of the skin that mimics a benign cyst or lymphoma and requires wide margins and a sentinel node biopsy?
Back: Merkel Cell Carcinoma.

Front: What is the "triple test" used to evaluate a newly discovered palpable breast mass?
Back: Clinical breast examination, diagnostic imaging (mammography/ultrasound), and tissue biopsy (core needle biopsy).

Front: If a breast core biopsy returns as an "Atypical Ductal Hyperplasia" (ADH), what is the next mandatory surgical step?
Back: Excisional biopsy of the area, because up to 15-20% of ADH lesions are upgraded to DCIS or invasive cancer on final surgical pathology.

Front: What defines a "T4" breast cancer regardless of the actual measured physical size of the primary tumor?
Back: Any tumor with direct extension into the chest wall, skin ulceration, or skin satellite nodules.

Front: Why is a diagnostic biopsy of a suspected testicular mass performed via a radical inguinal orchiectomy rather than a transscrotal approach?
Back: A transscrotal biopsy disrupts the native lymphatic drainage of the testicle, causing tumor cells to seed the scrotal skin and alter regional metastatic patterns.

Front: Where do the lymphatics of the testicle naturally drain?
Back: Directly to the retroperitoneal para-aortic lymph nodes.

Front: Where do the lymphatics of the scrotum naturally drain?
Back: To the superficial inguinal lymph nodes.

Section:  General Surgery

Topic: Vascular surgery — PAD, AAA, DVT/PE

Front: Most common anatomical site for atherosclerotic occlusion causing intermittent claudication?
Back: Superficial femoral artery within the adductor (Hunter’s) canal.

Front: Classic definition of intermittent claudication?
Back: Reproducible lower extremity cramping pain induced by exercise and completely relieved by 2 to 5 minutes of rest.

Front: Pathophysiological mechanism of rest pain in Peripheral Arterial Disease?
Back: Critical baseline tissue ischemia causing ischemic neuritis, typically exacerbated when lying flat due to loss of gravity-assisted perfusion.

Front: Formula used to calculate the Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI)?
Back: Higher systolic pressure of the dorsalis pedis or posterior tibial artery divided by the higher systolic pressure of either the left or right brachial artery.

Front: Interpretation of an Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI) between 0.4 and 0.9?
Back: Mild-to-moderate Peripheral Arterial Disease, typically correlating with intermittent claudication.

Front: Interpretation of an Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI) less than 0.4?
Back: Severe Peripheral Arterial Disease, highly correlated with ischemic rest pain and tissue necrosis.

Front: Cause of a falsely elevated Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI) greater than 1.40?
Back: Non-compressible, heavily calcified arteries commonly seen in patients with long-standing diabetes mellitus or end-stage renal disease (Mönckeberg's arteriosclerosis).

Front: Diagnostic alternative to Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI) when lower extremity vessels are non-compressible?
Back: Toe-Brachial Index (TBI), as digital arteries are rarely affected by medial calcific sclerosis.

Front: Cutoff values for Toe-Brachial Index (TBI) indicating significant lower extremity ischemia?
Back: TBI less than 0.70.

Front: Triad of symptoms characterizing Leriche Syndrome (Aortoiliac Occlusive Disease)?
Back: Bilateral buttock/thigh claudication, erectile dysfunction (impotence), and absent or severely diminished femoral pulses.

Front: First-line, most effective non-operative therapy for stabilizing and improving symptoms of intermittent claudication?
Back: Structured, supervised exercise therapy (minimum 30–45 minutes, 3 times per week for at least 3 months).

Front: First-line antiplatelet regimen recommended for secondary cardiovascular risk reduction in all symptomatic PAD patients?
Back: Low-dose aspirin (75–100 mg daily) or clopidogrel (75 mg daily) monotherapy.

Front: Pharmacological agent added specifically to improve walking distance in patients with refractory intermittent claudication?
Back: Cilostazol.

Front: Mechanism of action and absolute contraindication for Cilostazol?
Back: Phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor that increases cAMP (causing vasodilation and platelet inhibition); absolutely contraindicated in heart failure of any severity.

Front: Definition of Chronic Limb-Threatening Ischemia (CLTI)?
Back: PAD clinical spectrum featuring ischemic rest pain, non-healing arterial ulcers, or gangrene present for greater than 2 weeks, confirmed by objective hemodynamic data.

Front: Diagnostic hemodynamic thresholds that define Chronic Limb-Threatening Ischemia (CLTI)?
Back: Ankle pressure less than 50 mmHg OR toe pressure less than 30 mmHg.

Front: Key physical exam findings differentiating an arterial ischemic ulcer from a venous stasis ulcer?
Back: Arterial ulcers are painful, punched-out, dry, and located at distal pressure points (toes); venous ulcers are minimally painful, irregular, wet, and located on the medial malleolus.

Front: Meaning of the clinical sign "dependent rubor" followed by "pallor on elevation"?
Back: Severe ischemia where dermal vessels are chronically, maximally dilated by autoregulation to trap blood flow; limbs turn bright red when dependent and pale when raised.

Front: Gold standard imaging modality required for anatomical mapping prior to planning an endovascular or surgical peripheral arterial revascularization?
Back: Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) or Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA).

Front: Indications for choosing endovascular intervention (PTA/Stenting) over open surgical bypass in PAD?
Back: Focal, short-segment stenoses or occlusions (TASC A and B lesions) in patients with significant operative comorbidities.

Front: Standard conduit of choice for an open infrainguinal peripheral arterial bypass graft (e.g., femoropopliteal)?
Back: Autologous ipsilateral great saphenous vein (GSV), which yields superior long-term patency rates compared to synthetic prosthetic conduits (PTFE).

Front: The "6 Ps" characterizing the clinical presentation of Acute Limb Ischemia (ALI)?
Back: Pain, Pallor, Pulselessness, Paresthesia, Paralysis, and Poikilothermia (perishing cold).

Front: Most common cause of cardiogenic arterial embolization leading to Acute Limb Ischemia?
Back: Atrial fibrillation with thrombus formation in the left atrial appendage.

Front: Most common peripheral arterial aneurysm site associated with microembolization ("blue toe syndrome") and acute thrombosis?
Back: Popliteal artery aneurysm.

Front: Immediate initial medical management step upon clinical diagnosis of Acute Limb Ischemia?
Back: Intravenous bolus followed by a continuous infusion of unfractionated heparin to prevent propagation of the thrombus.

Front: Diagnostic finding that separates Rutherford Class IIa (Incipient Threat) from Class IIb (Immediate Threat) Acute Limb Ischemia?
Back: Class IIa has sensory loss limited to the toes with intact muscle strength; Class IIb has progressive sensory loss extending beyond the toes and mild-to-moderate motor loss (muscle weakness).

Front: Definition of Rutherford Class III Acute Limb Ischemia and its definitive management?
Back: Irreversible ischemic tissue damage characterized by profound, fixed sensory and motor loss, anesthetic skin, and unaudible Doppler signals; requires emergent amputation.

Front: Standard operative intervention for an acute embolic occlusion of a peripheral artery?
Back: Emergent surgical thromboembolectomy utilizing a balloon-tipped Fogarty catheter.

Front: Pathophysiology and main laboratory sign of Reperfusion Injury following revascularization for prolonged ischemia?
Back: Ischemic muscle cells release potassium, creatine kinase, and myoglobin upon restored blood flow, leading to metabolic acidosis, hyperkalemia, and myoglobinuria.

Front: Serious orthopedic/vascular complication characterized by calf muscle swelling, severe pain on passive stretch, and compartment pressures greater than 30 mmHg following limb reperfusion?
Back: Compartment Syndrome, requiring immediate emergency four-compartment decompressive fasciotomy of the lower leg.


Front: Anatomical definition of an Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA)?

Back: Permanent localized dilation of the abdominal aorta matching or exceeding 1.5 times its normal reference diameter, or a definitive diameter of 3.0 cm or greater.

Front: Most common anatomical location for the development of an Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm?

Back: Infrarenal abdominal aorta (below the origin of the renal arteries).

Front: Primary histopathological feature observed in the aortic wall during the pathogenesis of an AAA?

Back: Chronic transmural inflammation, degradation of elastin and collagen by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and depletion of vascular smooth muscle cells.

Front: Single most significant modifiable environmental risk factor for the development and accelerated expansion of an AAA?

Back: Cigarette smoking.

Front: Population group for which a one-time screening abdominal ultrasound for AAA is universally recommended by SVS and USPSTF?

Back: Any male between the ages of 65 and 75 who has ever smoked cigarettes.

Front: Relationship between a patient's biological sex and the risk of AAA rupture?

Back: Female patients have a significantly higher risk of AAA rupture at smaller diameters compared to male patients.

Front: Recommended imaging surveillance interval for a small AAA measuring 3.0 to 3.9 cm?

Back: Every 3 years via transabdominal duplex ultrasound.

Front: Recommended imaging surveillance interval for an AAA measuring 4.0 to 4.9 cm?

Back: Every 12 months (annually) via transabdominal duplex ultrasound.

Front: Standard absolute diameter threshold indicating elective surgical repair for an asymptomatic AAA in a male patient?

Back: 5.5 cm or greater.

Front: Standard absolute diameter threshold indicating elective surgical repair for an asymptomatic AAA in a female patient?

Back: 5.0 cm to 5.4 cm or greater.

Front: Rate of expansion threshold that warrants elective AAA repair regardless of absolute size?

Back: Rapid growth defined as an expansion of 0.5 cm or more within 6 months, or 1.0 cm or more within 1 year.

Front: Classic clinical triad associated with a ruptured Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm?

Back: Acute onset of severe abdominal or back pain, profound hypotension/shock, and a palpable, pulsatile abdominal mass.

Front: Most common anatomical site into which an AAA ruptures, which may self-tamponade temporarily?

Back: The retroperitoneal space.

Front: Determinant of immediate next management step for a patient presenting with suspected AAA rupture who is hemodynamically unstable?

Back: Immediate transfer to the operating room for emergent open repair or Endovascular Aneurysm Repair (EVAR) without delaying for diagnostic CT imaging.

Front: Determinant of immediate next management step for a patient presenting with suspected AAA rupture who is hemodynamically stable?

Back: Emergent contrast-enhanced Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) of the abdomen and pelvis to evaluate anatomy.

Front: Definition of "Permissive Hypotension" in the perioperative resuscitation of a ruptured AAA?

Back: Deliberately maintaining a low-normal systemic blood pressure (systolic 70–90 mmHg) to minimize blood loss and avoid blowing out a fragile retroperitoneal clot before proximal aortic control is secured.

Front: Anatomical prerequisites required to perform standard Endovascular Aneurysm Repair (EVAR)?

Back: An adequate non-aneurysmal infrarenal aortic neck (length ≥10–15 mm) to anchor the graft, and adequate, non-tortuous iliofemoral access vessels.

Front: Definition of a Type I Endoleak following EVAR?

Back: Persistent blood flow into the aneurysm sac due to an ineffective seal at either the proximal or distal graft attachment sites (requires mandatory intervention).

Front: Definition and typical management of a Type II Endoleak following EVAR?

Back: Retrograde blood flow into the aneurysm sac from patent collateral aortic branches, most commonly the lumbar arteries or the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA); managed via observation if the sac size remains stable.

Front: Definition of a Type III Endoleak following EVAR?

Back: Persistent blood flow into the aneurysm sac resulting from a mechanical structural failure of the endograft, such as a fabric tear, component separation, or disconnection (requires mandatory intervention).

Front: Primary long-term imaging surveillance requirement following an EVAR procedure?

Back: Lifelong periodic imaging (typically contrast-enhanced CT or duplex ultrasound at 1 month, 12 months, and annually thereafter) to monitor for endoleaks and sac expansion.

Front: Serious late gastrointestinal complication unique to open AAA repair presenting with massive, herald upper/lower GI bleeding?

Back: Aortoenteric fistula, typically occurring between the proximal aortic suture line and the third/fourth portion of the duodenum.

Front: Underlying structural disease process that classically causes painful thoracic aortic dissections or aneurysms in young adults?

Back: Connective tissue disorders causing cystic medial necrosis, such as Marfan Syndrome (FBN1 mutation) or Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome type IV (COL3A1 mutation).

Front: Stanford classification schema differentiating Type A from Type B aortic dissections?

Back: Type A involves the ascending aorta (proximal to the brachiocephalic artery); Type B is confined to the descending aorta distal to the left subclavian artery origin.

Front: DeBakey classification categories for aortic dissections?

Back: Type I involves the ascending and descending aorta; Type II is isolated to the ascending aorta; Type III is isolated to the descending aorta.

Front: Classic characterization of pain reported by a patient experiencing an acute aortic dissection?

Back: Sudden onset of severe, excruciating, "tearing" or "ripping" pain radiating directly to the back or between the scapulae.

Front: Physical exam finding reflecting asymmetrical arterial branch involvement in a thoracic aortic dissection?

Back: Inter-arm blood pressure discrepancy (systolic difference >20 mmHg) or a pulse deficit in one of the upper or lower extremities.

Front: Direct cause of sudden death or cardiogenic shock in a patient with a Stanford Type A aortic dissection?

Back: Retrograde propagation causing acute cardiac tamponade (hemopericardium), acute severe aortic regurgitation, or acute myocardial infarction due to coronary ostial involvement.

Front: Immediate medical management goal for all acute aortic dissections before any surgical intervention?

Back: Anti-impulsive therapy using intravenous short-acting beta-blockers (e.g., esmolol or labetalol) to aggressively lower heart rate (target <60 bpm) and systolic blood pressure (target 100–120 mmHg) to reduce shear stress ($dP/dt$).

Front: Definitive management standard for all Stanford Type A aortic dissections?

Back: Emergent open surgical replacement of the ascending aorta with a synthetic dacron graft to prevent catastrophic rupture or tamponade.

Front: Primary management paradigm for an uncomplicated Stanford Type B aortic dissection?

Back: Medical management emphasizing continuous strict blood pressure control and lifestyle modifications.

Front: Specific clinical indicators that convert a Stanford Type B dissection from "uncomplicated" to "complicated," requiring intervention?

Back: Malperfusion syndrome (renal, mesenteric, or limb ischemia), uncontrollable refractory chest/back pain, rapid aneurysmal expansion, or impending aortic rupture.

Front: Definitive procedural intervention of choice for a complicated Stanford Type B aortic dissection?

Back: Thoracic Endovascular Aortic Repair (TEVAR).

Front: Elements comprising Virchow's Triad of venous thromboembolism pathogenesis?

Back: Endothelial injury, venous stasis, and a hypercoagulable state.

Front: Most common anatomical origin site for lower extremity deep vein thrombi that subsequently embolize to the pulmonary vasculature?

Back: Proximal deep veins of the lower limb, specifically the popliteal, femoral, and iliac veins.

Front: Clinical significance of isolated calf-vein DVT (distal DVT) compared to proximal DVT?

Back: Distal DVTs have a much lower risk of pulmonary embolization and are often managed with surveillance unless symptomatic or extending proximally.

Front: Classic, yet non-specific, lower extremity physical exam sign characterized by calf pain elicited upon forced dorsiflexion of the foot?

Back: Homans' sign.

Front: Definition and clinical urgency of Phlegmasia Alba Dolens ("milk leg")?

Back: Massive iliofemoral DVT causing near-total deep venous occlusion; marked by a pale, swollen, painful limb with intact collateral venous runoff and intact pulses (requires urgent evaluation).

Front: Definition and pathophysiological hazard of Phlegmasia Cerulea Dolens?

Back: Fulminant, total occlusion of both deep and collateral venous anatomy of a limb; marked by a cyanotic, intensely ischemic, severely painful leg with loss of distal pulses due to high compartment pressures (high risk of venous gangrene and amputation).

Front: Utility and negative predictive value of a quantitative D-Dimer assay in venous thromboembolism evaluation?

Back: Highly sensitive but non-specific degradation product of cross-linked fibrin; a negative test rules out DVT/PE in low-probability patients due to its high negative predictive value.

Front: First-line, gold-standard non-invasive diagnostic imaging test for a suspected lower extremity DVT?

Back: Venous Duplex Ultrasound (Compression Ultrasonography), where the primary diagnostic criterion is the inability to completely compress the venous lumen.

Front: Gold standard imaging modality for diagnosing an acute Pulmonary Embolism?

Back: Computed Tomography Pulmonary Angiography (CTPA).

Front: Diagnostic alternative for identifying a Pulmonary Embolism in a patient with severe contrast allergy or advanced chronic kidney disease?

Back: Ventilation-Perfusion (V/Q) Lung Scintigraphy.

Front: Most common, non-specific electrocardiogram (ECG) finding seen in a patient with an acute Pulmonary Embolism?

Back: Sinus tachycardia.

Front: Classic ECG pattern indicating acute right ventricular strain or cor pulmonale due to a massive PE?

Back: S1Q3T3 pattern (prominent S wave in lead I, Q wave in lead III, and inverted T wave in lead III).

Front: Classic chest X-ray findings associated with a pulmonary infarction secondary to a PE?

Back: Hampton’s Hump (a wedge-shaped, pleural-based opacification) and Westermark’s Sign (regional oligemia/decreased vascularity distal to the occluded vessel).

Front: Immediate initial medical treatment for a confirmed or highly suspected acute, non-massive DVT/PE in a stable patient?

Back: Immediate systemic anticoagulation with therapeutic Low-Molecular-Weight Heparin (LMWH), Fondaparinux, or a Direct Oral Anticoagulant (DOAC like rivaroxaban or apixaban).

Front: Mechanism of action of Unfractionated Heparin (UFH)?

Back: Binds to and accelerates the activity of Antithrombin III, which primarily inactivates Thrombin (Factor IIa) and Factor Xa.

Front: Laboratory parameter monitored to guide the dosing of continuous intravenous Unfractionated Heparin?

Back: Activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) or anti-Factor Xa assay levels.

Front: Primary specific reversal agent for Unfractionated Heparin?

Back: Protamine sulfate (1 mg reverses approximately 100 units of heparin).

Front: Mechanism of action of Low-Molecular-Weight Heparin (LMWH, e.g., enoxaparin)?

Back: Binds to Antithrombin III to selectively inhibit Factor Xa with minimal effect on Thrombin (Factor IIa).

Front: Clinical scenarios where Unfractionated Heparin is preferred over Low-Molecular-Weight Heparin?

Back: Patients with severe renal impairment (CrCl <30 mL/min), hemodynamically unstable PE, or an impending requirement for immediate surgical intervention due to UFH's short half-life and easy reversibility.

Front: Serious immune-mediated drug reaction characterized by a paradoxical drop in platelets and a prothrombotic state 5 to 10 days after starting heparin?

Back: Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT), caused by IgG antibodies directed against the Heparin-Platelet Factor 4 (PF4) complex.

Front: Immediate management actions required upon clinical suspicion of Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT)?

Back: Stop all forms of heparin immediately and initiate alternative anticoagulation with a direct thrombin inhibitor (e.g., argatroban or bivalirudin); do not transfuse platelets.

Front: Mechanism of action of Warfarin?

Back: Inhibits the Vitamin K Epoxide Reductase Complex 1 (VKORC1), blocking the gamma-carboxylation and activation of Vitamin K-dependent clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X, along with proteins C and S.

Front: Reason why rapid induction of Warfarin without a concurrent heparin "bridge" is strictly contraindicated in acute thrombosis?

Back: Warfarin depletes Protein C and S (short half-lives) faster than it depletes factors II and X, inducing a transient hypercoagulable state that can lead to skin necrosis.

Front: Standard target International Normalized Ratio (INR) range for a patient on Warfarin therapy for DVT or PE?

Back: Target INR range of 2.0 to 3.0.

Front: Specific reversal agents for Warfarin therapy depending on the urgency of bleeding?

Back: Intravenous Vitamin K for non-emergent correction; 4-Factor Prothrombin Complex Concentrate (PCC) or Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP) for emergency reversal during major hemorrhage.

Front: Target proteins inhibited by Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs) like Rivaroxaban and Apixaban?

Back: Direct inhibition of free and clot-bound Factor Xa.

Front: Standard recommended duration of anticoagulation therapy for a first-episode DVT/PE secondary to a transient, reversible surgical risk factor?

Back: 3 months.

Front: Core definition of a "Massive" Pulmonary Embolism?

Back: Acute PE presenting with sustained systemic hypotension (systolic BP <90 mmHg for >15 minutes) or requiring inotropic support, independent of right ventricular dysfunction.

Front: Core definition of a "Submassive" Pulmonary Embolism?

Back: Acute PE presenting with normal systemic blood pressure but with objective evidence of right ventricular strain (seen on echocardiogram/CT) or elevated cardiac biomarkers (Troponin/BNP).

Front: First-line definitive therapeutic intervention for a patient with a hemodynamically unstable, massive Pulmonary Embolism who has no contraindications?

Back: Systemic thrombolytic therapy (e.g., recombinant tissue plasminogen activator / alteplase).

Front: Management strategy for a patient with a massive Pulmonary Embolism who has absolute contraindications to systemic thrombolysis?

Back: Emergent surgical pulmonary embolectomy or catheter-directed mechanical thrombectomy.

Front: Precise clinical indication for the placement of an Inferior Vena Cava (IVC) filter?

Back: Confirmed acute proximal DVT or PE in a patient with an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation therapy, or recurrent VTE despite documented therapeutic anticoagulation.

Front: Long-term clinical sequela of a DVT characterized by chronic lower extremity edema, hyperpigmentation, stasis dermatitis, and venous ulcers due to valvular destruction?

Back: Post-Thrombotic Syndrome (PTS).

Front: Optimal surgical management for a patient presenting with an infected, mycotic abdominal aortic aneurysm?

Back: Wide local debridement of the infected aorta, extra-anatomical vascular reconstruction bypass (e.g., axillobifemoral bypass) through clean tissue planes, and long-term targeted intravenous antibiotics.

Front: Definition of an endovascular "Snorkel" or "Chimney" technique used during complex EVAR?

Back: Placement of parallel covered stents into visceral branches (renal or mesenteric arteries) running alongside the main aortic endograft body to preserve branch perfusion while treating juxtarenal or suprarenal aneurysms.

Front: Primary physiological driver of intermediate-term failure (restenosis) within 1 to 24 months inside an endovascularly placed arterial stent?

Back: Neointimal hyperplasia (smooth muscle cell proliferation and extracellular matrix deposition).

Front: Standard screening utility for Popliteal Artery Aneurysms upon initial diagnosis?

Back: Bilateral duplex ultrasound, as up to 50% of popliteal aneurysms are bilateral; also requires evaluation for a synchronous AAA (found in ~30–40% of cases).

Front: Primary indication for surgical or endovascular intervention on an asymptomatic Popliteal Artery Aneurysm?

Back: Thrombus accumulation inside the aneurysm or an absolute local diameter threshold exceeding 2.0 cm, due to high risk of acute ischemic limb loss.

Front: Classic sign on continuous wave Doppler identifying an incomplete palmar arch before harvesting the radial artery?

Back: A positive Allen’s test (or objective duplex Allen's test).

Front: Name and mechanism of the vascular phenomenon causing digital ischemia upon exposure to cold or emotional stress?

Back: Raynaud's Phenomenon; characterized by exaggerated digital vasospasm of precapillary muscular arteries.

Front: The classic three-phase color changes observed in Raynaud’s Phenomenon?

Back: White (pallor due to intense ischemia), Blue (cyanosis due to tissue hypoxia), and Red (rubor due to reactive hyperemia upon reperfusion).

Front: First-line, preventative pharmacological therapy of choice for severe, symptomatic Raynaud's Phenomenon?

Back: Dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (e.g., nifedipine or amlodipine) to promote peripheral smooth muscle vasodilation.

Front: Definition of Buerger’s Disease (Thromboangiitis Obliterans)?

Back: Non-atherosclerotic, segmentally occlusive, highly inflammatory vasculitis affecting small- and medium-sized arteries and veins of the distal extremities, strongly associated with heavy tobacco use in young adults.

Front: Classic angiographic description of collateral vessels bypass-grafting occlusions in Buerger's Disease?

Back: "Corkscrew collaterals" around areas of distal segmental arterial occlusion.

Front: Only effective definitive therapeutic intervention to arrest the progression of Thromboangiitis Obliterans (Buerger's Disease)?

Back: Absolute, complete cessation of all tobacco and nicotine products; surgical revascularization is typically impossible due to the lack of a distal target.

Front: Defining anatomical landmark separating Upper GI from Lower GI hemorrhage sources?

Back: The Ligament of Treitz (suspensory muscle of the duodenum).

Front: Diagnostic imaging modality of choice for suspected acute mesenteric ischemia in an emergency setting?

Back: Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) of the abdomen and pelvis.

Front: Most common source mechanism causing acute mesenteric ischemia?

Back: Acute superior mesenteric artery (SMA) embolism, typically originating from the left atrium in a patient with atrial fibrillation.

Front: Classic clinical hallmark presentation of acute mesenteric ischemia?

Back: Severe, agonizing abdominal pain that is completely out of proportion to the minimal physical exam findings (abdomen remains soft and non-tender initially).

Front: Definitive emergency surgical management required for an acute, embolic superior mesenteric artery occlusion with signs of peritonitis?

Back: Emergent laparotomy for surgical SMA embolectomy, assessment of bowel viability, and resection of frankly necrotic intestinal segments.

Front: Presentation of Chronic Mesenteric Ischemia ("intestinal angina")?

Back: Recurrent postprandial abdominal pain (occurring 30–60 minutes after eating) causing a profound fear of food ("sitophobia") and significant progressive weight loss.

Front: Underlying anatomy involved in Thoracic Outlet Syndrome (TOS)?

Back: Compression of the brachial plexus (neurogenic), subclavian vein (venous), or subclavian artery (arterial) within the scalene triangle or costoclavicular space.

Front: Name of the positional clinical maneuver used to evaluate for arterial thoracic outlet syndrome?

Back: Adson’s Test (loss of radial pulse when the patient rotates their head toward the ipsilateral side and extends the neck during deep inspiration).

Front: Definition of Paget-Schroetter Syndrome?

Back: Effort-induced thrombosis of the subclavian-axillary vein caused by repetitive compressive microtrauma inside the thoracic outlet, often seen in athletes performing overhead motions.

Front: Direct immediate management sequence for a patient with an expanding, pulsatile neck hematoma following a penetrating zone II neck injury?

Back: Immediate endotracheal intubation to secure the airway, followed by urgent transfer to the operating room for surgical exploration without delay for imaging.

Front: ATLS criteria defining hard signs of vascular extremity injury requiring immediate surgical exploration?

Back: Pulsatile hemorrhage, expanding or pulsatile hematoma, an audible bruit or palpable thrill, and clear signs of distal ischemia (absent pulses, cold pale limb).

Front: Management pathway for an extremity trauma patient exhibiting soft signs of vascular injury (e.g., history of moderate bleeding, proximity to a major vessel)?

Back: Measure an Injured Extremity Index (IEI) or Arterial Pressure Index (API) by comparing ankle/wrist systolic pressures between the injured and uninjured limb.

Front: Cutoff values for an Injured Extremity Index (IEI) that mandate further diagnostic imaging evaluation?

Back: An IEI less than 0.90 mandates evaluation via CTA or conventional arteriography to rule out occult vascular injury.

Front: Surgical sequence prioritization when managing an open mangled extremity featuring both an unstable bone fracture and a major arterial transection?

Back: Temporary placement of an intravascular shunt to immediately restore distal tissue perfusion, followed by rigid orthopedic fracture stabilization, followed by definitive vascular graft reconstruction.

Front: A 68-year-old male smoker has a 5.8 cm AAA identified on ultrasound. He is asymptomatic. Next step?

Back: Obtain a contrast-enhanced Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) of the abdomen and pelvis to evaluate anatomical eligibility for elective repair (EVAR vs. Open).

Front: A 72-year-old female presents with sudden back pain. Her known AAA measures 4.8 cm. Her BP is 85/50 mmHg. Next step?

Back: Immediate transfer to the operating suite for emergency aortic repair; do not wait for a CT scan.

Front: A 52-year-old male with atrial fibrillation presents with sudden, severe right leg pain and coldness. The right dorsalis pedis pulse is absent. Next step?

Back: Administer an immediate intravenous bolus of unfractionated heparin and prepare for emergent surgical thromboembolectomy.

Front: A patient 4 hours post-fem-pop bypass develops severe calf pain, numbness between the first and second toes, and pain on passive foot movement. Pulses are intact. Next step?

Back: Emergency measurement of compartment pressures or immediate emergency four-compartment lower leg fasciotomy for Compartment Syndrome.

Front: A 62-year-old male presents with severe calf pain when walking 1 block. His ABI is 0.65. He has no rest pain or ulcers. Next step?

Back: Initiate a structured, supervised exercise program and optimize cardiovascular risk factors (high-intensity statin, aspirin, smoking cessation).

Front: A 35-year-old female smoker presents with painful, blue, ulcerated fingertips. Arteriogram shows segmental occlusions with corkscrew collaterals. Next step?

Back: Counsel the patient on absolute, immediate cessation of all tobacco and nicotine products.

Front: A 74-year-old male with a history of an open AAA repair 2 years ago presents with an initial self-limiting episode of hematemesis followed by massive hematochezia and shock. Next step?

Back: Emergent surgical exploration for an aortoenteric fistula.

Front: A 45-year-old female undergoes a right total knee arthroplasty. On postoperative day 3, she develops right calf swelling and tenderness. Next step?

Back: Perform a right lower extremity venous duplex ultrasound to evaluate for deep vein thrombosis.

Front: A stable postoperative patient has a confirmed acute proximal deep vein thrombosis. He has no history of bleeding or renal disease. Next step?

Back: Initiate therapeutic anticoagulation with a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) or low-molecular-weight heparin.

Front: A patient on continuous intravenous unfractionated heparin for a PE sees her platelet count drop from 250,000 to 90,000 on day 6 of therapy. Next step?

Back: Discontinue all heparin immediately, send an ELISA test for anti-PF4 antibodies, and initiate alternative anticoagulation with Argatroban.

Front: A 60-year-old female with a history of breast cancer presents with sudden onset shortness of breath and pleuritic chest pain. Her BP is 120/80 mmHg. Next step?

Back: Order a Computed Tomography Pulmonary Angiography (CTPA) to evaluate for pulmonary embolism.

Front: A 55-year-old male presents with sudden shortness of breath, a heart rate of 125 bpm, and a blood pressure of 78/45 mmHg. CTPA confirms a large saddle pulmonary embolism. Next step?

Back: Administer systemic thrombolytic therapy (unless absolute contraindications exist).

Front: An 80-year-old male with an acute, symptomatic proximal DVT has an active, bleeding peptic ulcer. Next step?

Back: Percutaneous placement of a retrievable Inferior Vena Cava (IVC) filter.

Front: A 65-year-old male with severe COPD is diagnosed with a symptomatic proximal DVT. He is safely started on therapeutic anticoagulation. Next step?

Back: Plan for a minimum duration of 3 months of therapeutic anticoagulation.

Front: A 24-year-old male driver is brought to the ER after a high-speed motor vehicle collision. Chest X-ray shows a widened mediastinum and loss of the aortic knob outline. Next step?

Back: Obtain an emergent contrast-enhanced CT Angiography of the chest to rule out blunt traumatic aortic injury (BTAI).

Front: A 28-year-old female presents with acute swelling, severe pain, and a deep purplish-cyanotic hue of her entire left lower extremity. Femoral and popliteal pulses are difficult to palpate. Next step?

Back: Intravenous heparin bolus and emergent vascular surgical consultation for Phlegmasia Cerulea Dolens management.

Front: Most reliable physical exam maneuver to differentiate a popliteal artery aneurysm from a simple popliteal (Baker's) cyst?

Back: Evaluation for a prominent, expansile, pulsatile mass in the popliteal fossa (Baker's cysts are fluctuant and do not exhibit intrinsic pulsatility).

Front: Standard procedural choice for an asymptomatic carotid artery stenosis greater than 70% in a fit, 65-year-old patient?

Back: Carotid Endarterectomy (CEA).

Front: Sudden, catastrophic complication during Carotid Endarterectomy characterized by postoperative severe headache, focal neurological deficits, or seizures due to restored high-pressure flow to a chronically ischemic brain?

Back: Cerebral Hyperperfusion Syndrome.

Front: Name of the classic vascular sign where a deep breath combined with arm abduction and head rotation causes radial pulse occlusion?

Back: Adson's maneuver (indicative of neurovascular compression in Thoracic Outlet Syndrome).

Front: Primary therapeutic indication for performing an axillobifemoral extra-anatomical open bypass graft?

Back: Management of a heavily infected infrarenal aortic graft or mycotic aneurysm where an in-situ prosthetic replacement is strictly contraindicated.

Front: Most common physiological mechanism causing intermediate-term failure (restenosis) within 3 to 24 months after endovascular peripheral arterial balloon angioplasty or stenting?

Back: Neointimal hyperplasia (smooth muscle cell migration and proliferation into the tunica intima).

Front: Specific class of medications that must be permanently discontinued before initiating therapeutic Warfarin to prevent warfarin-induced skin necrosis?

Back: None; instead, a fast-acting parenteral anticoagulant (like Heparin or LMWH) must overlap ("bridge") Warfarin for at least 5 days and until the INR is therapeutic.

Front: Target laboratory assay utilized to accurately monitor the therapeutic efficacy of Low-Molecular-Weight Heparin (LMWH) in pregnant or morbidly obese patients?

Back: Anti-Factor Xa assay (standard aPTT is insensitive to LMWH activity).

Front: Specific target protein reversibly or irreversibly bound by the novel oral anticoagulants Dabigatran vs. Apixaban?

Back: Dabigatran directly inhibits Thrombin (Factor IIa); Apixaban directly inhibits Factor Xa.

Front: Reversal agent of choice designed to rapidly counteract life-threatening hemorrhage secondary to Direct Factor Xa inhibitors (Rivaroxaban/Apixaban)?

Back: Andexanet alfa.

Front: Specific monoclonal antibody fragment engineered to immediately reverse the anticoagulant effect of Dabigatran?

Back: Idarucizumab.

Front: Definitive gold-standard objective test used to confirm a clinical suspicion of Chronic Mesenteric Ischemia?

Back: Mesenteric duplex ultrasound or CT Angiography (CTA) demonstrating significant stenosis in at least two of the three major visceral vessels (Celiac, SMA, IMA).

Front: Classic radiological sign visible on a plain abdominal X-ray or non-contrast CT indicating advanced, irreversible transmural bowel infarction?

Back: Pneumatosis intestinalis (gas within the intestinal wall) or portal venous gas.

Front: Pathophysiological mechanism driving severe hypotension and metabolic derangements immediately following the release of an aortic cross-clamp during open AAA repair?

Back: "Declamping shock," caused by sudden systemic vasodilation from the washout of anaerobic metabolic byproducts (lactic acid) accumulated in the ischemic lower extremities.

Front: Most common cranial nerve injured during a standard open Carotid Endarterectomy resulting in temporary or permanent tongue deviation toward the side of the injury?

Back: Hypoglossal nerve (CN XII).

Front: Primary long-term functional risk associated with a permanent, unretrieved Inferior Vena Cava (IVC) filter?

Back: Increased long-term risk of lower extremity deep vein thrombosis recurrence and severe Post-Thrombotic Syndrome, despite reducing immediate PE risk.

Front: Specific diagnostic value of the "Well's Criteria" in the clinical workup of suspected DVT or PE?

Back: Stratifies patients into "low," "moderate," or "high" pre-test probability to determine whether to order a D-Dimer (low/moderate risk) or proceed directly to definitive imaging (high risk).

Front: The most sensitive and specific diagnostic standard for identifying a suspected blunt traumatic aortic injury (BTAI) following high-impact deceleration chest trauma?

Back: Contrast-enhanced Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) of the chest.

Front: Precise anatomical location where the vast majority of blunt traumatic aortic injuries (BTAI) occur due to tethering forces?

Back: The aortic isthmus, immediately distal to the origin of the left subclavian artery (at the site of the ligamentum arteriosum).

Front: Primary non-operative goal for a stable, low-grade (Grade I) blunt traumatic aortic injury?

Back: Strict blood pressure and heart rate control using intravenous beta-blockers to maintain systolic BP below 120 mmHg and prevent delayed free rupture.

Front: Exact duration of time a patient must be completely off Clopidogrel (Plavix) before undergoing elective open major vascular surgery to reduce bleeding risks?

Back: 5 days.

Front: Underlying anatomical defect that allows a lower extremity DVT to cause an embolic ischemic stroke (paradoxical embolism)?

Back: A patent foramen ovale (PFO) or atrial septal defect (ASD), allowing the clot to bypass the lungs from right to left.

Front: Name of the phenomenon where an open AAA repair causes acute ischemic colitis due to sacrifice of the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA)?

Back: Collateral flow failure from the Arc of Riolan or Marginal Artery of Drummond.

Front: Next diagnostic step if a patient has strong clinical signs of ischemic colitis (bloody diarrhea, leukocytosis) 48 hours post-open AAA repair?

Back: Flexible sigmoidoscopy to directly visualize the integrity of the colonic mucosa.

Front: Most common surgical complication encountered when performing an open femoral artery exposure or endovascular groin access?

Back: Femoral groin hematoma, lymphocele, or localized pseudoaneurysm formation.

Front: Physical exam finding that distinguishes a true femoral artery aneurysm from a post-catheterization femoral pseudoaneurysm?

Back: A true aneurysm is a smoothly expansile mass; a pseudoaneurysm typically features a harsh, systolic bruit and localized tenderness.

Front: Diagnostic criteria on Duplex ultrasound used to confirm a femoral or radial pseudoaneurysm?

Back: The classic "yin-yang" flow pattern within the aneurysmal sac and a turbulent "to-and-fro" waveform in the tract neck.

Front: First-line minimally invasive management for a small (<2 cm), symptomatic femoral pseudoaneurysm?

Back: Ultrasound-guided thrombin injection directly into the pseudoaneurysm sac.

Front: The most sensitive indicator of early, occult hemorrhagic shock in a vascular trauma patient when blood pressure is still normotensive?

Back: Narrowing pulse pressure (systolic and diastolic values converging due to compensatory vasoconstriction).

Front: Specific anatomical landmark used to locate the proximal control site for the superficial femoral artery during open surgery?

Back: The medial border of the sartorius muscle within the upper third of the thigh.

Front: Expected normal drop in Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI) immediately following a standardized treadmill exercise test in a healthy individual?

Back: Zero; in healthy individuals, systolic ankle pressure stays the same or slightly rises with exercise.

Front: Specific physical finding in the foot that indicates critical digital microembolization from a proximal ulcerated plaque?

Back: "Trash foot" (isolated, violently painful, cyanotic toes with completely intact, palpable pedal pulses).

Front: Primary therapeutic indication for a surgical crossover femoro-femoral bypass graft?

Back: Unilateral iliac artery occlusion in a patient who cannot undergo an endovascular fix and is too frail for an open trans-abdominal reconstruction.

Front: Precise timing requirement for checking the aPTT after adjusting a continuous intravenous Unfractionated Heparin infusion rate?

Back: 6 hours after the dose or rate change.

Front: Gold standard treatment for a patient presenting with an uncomplicated, small (<2 cm) asymptomatic visceral artery aneurysm (e.g., splenic artery)?

Back: Conservative observation, except in women of childbearing age or pregnant individuals, where immediate prophylactic repair is mandatory due to a high risk of rupture.
