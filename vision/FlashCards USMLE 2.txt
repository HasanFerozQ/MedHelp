For FlashCards USMLE 2
Section: Surgery 
Topic: General Surgery

Q1251: What is the primary purpose of a surgical drain?

A: To evacuate fluid (blood, serous fluid, bile, pus, or pancreatic secretions), reduce dead space, and allow early detection of postoperative leaks.

Q1252: What is the difference between an open drain and a closed-suction drain?

A: Open drains allow fluid to drain externally and carry a higher infection risk; closed-suction drains (e.g., Jackson-Pratt) reduce contamination by using a sealed system.

Q1253: Which drain is most commonly used after many abdominal operations?

A: Jackson-Pratt (JP) drain.

Q1254: Which drain provides stronger negative pressure than a Jackson-Pratt drain?

A: Hemovac drain.

Q1255: Which postoperative drain finding suggests a bile leak?

A: Persistent bilious drainage.

Q1256: Which postoperative drain finding suggests a pancreatic leak?

A: High-amylase drainage fluid.

Q1257: Should surgical drains be left in place longer than necessary?

A: No. Remove them as soon as clinically appropriate to reduce infection risk.

Nasogastric Tubes

Q1258: What are the major indications for nasogastric (NG) tube placement?

A: Gastric decompression, bowel obstruction, postoperative ileus, gastric outlet obstruction, and prevention of aspiration in selected patients.

Q1259: Is routine postoperative NG tube placement recommended after uncomplicated abdominal surgery?

A: No. Routine use delays recovery and is generally avoided.

Q1260: What should always be confirmed after NG tube placement?

A: Correct gastric position before use, typically with radiographic confirmation when indicated.

Ostomies

Q1261: What is an ileostomy?

A: A surgically created opening of the ileum to the abdominal wall.

Q1262: What is a colostomy?

A: A surgically created opening of the colon to the abdominal wall.

Q1263: Which ostomy usually produces liquid, enzyme-rich output?

A: Ileostomy.

Q1264: Which ostomy has the greatest risk of dehydration and electrolyte loss?

A: Ileostomy.

Q1265: Which electrolyte abnormality commonly develops with high-output ileostomy?

A: Hyponatremia (often with volume depletion and other electrolyte losses).

Q1266: Which ostomy generally produces more formed stool?

A: Descending or sigmoid colostomy.

Q1267: Which ostomy output is expected to be continuously liquid in the early postoperative period?

A: Ileostomy output.

Enterocutaneous Fistulas

Q1268: What is an enterocutaneous fistula?

A: An abnormal connection between the gastrointestinal tract and the skin.

Q1269: What is the most common cause of enterocutaneous fistulas?

A: Previous abdominal surgery.

Q1270: What are the priorities in managing an enterocutaneous fistula?

A: Control sepsis, optimize nutrition, replace fluids/electrolytes, protect surrounding skin, and define fistula anatomy.

Q1271: Which nutritional approach is preferred if the gastrointestinal tract remains usable?

A: Enteral nutrition.

Q1272: Which factors reduce the likelihood of spontaneous fistula closure? (FRIEND mnemonic)

A: Foreign body, Radiation, Infection/Inflammation, Epithelialization, Neoplasm, Distal obstruction.

Anastomotic Leak

Q1273: What is an anastomotic leak?

A: Breakdown of a surgical bowel anastomosis resulting in leakage of intestinal contents.

Q1274: When do anastomotic leaks most commonly present?

A: Usually within the first postoperative week (often postoperative days 3–7).

Q1275: What is the earliest clinical clue to an anastomotic leak?

A: Persistent tachycardia with unexplained clinical deterioration.

Q1276: What other symptoms suggest an anastomotic leak?

A: Fever, abdominal pain, ileus, peritonitis, leukocytosis, and sepsis.

Q1277: What imaging study is commonly used to evaluate a suspected anastomotic leak?

A: CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast and, when appropriate, oral or rectal contrast.

Q1278: What is the treatment for an anastomotic leak?

A: Broad-spectrum antibiotics, source control (percutaneous drainage or surgery), fluid resuscitation, and supportive care.

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS)

Q1279: What is the goal of ERAS protocols?

A: Reduce surgical stress, shorten recovery, and decrease postoperative complications.

Q1280: Name key components of ERAS protocols.

A: Patient education, multimodal analgesia, minimal fasting, early feeding, early ambulation, avoidance of unnecessary tubes/drains, and VTE prophylaxis.

Q1281: Why is early postoperative ambulation encouraged?

A: It reduces venous thromboembolism, pulmonary complications, and ileus while improving functional recovery.

Q1282: Why is multimodal analgesia preferred over opioid-only pain control?

A: It decreases opioid requirements and related adverse effects while improving recovery.

Surgical Site Infection (SSI)

Q1283: When are prophylactic antibiotics most effective?

A: When administered within 60 minutes before skin incision (timing varies for some antibiotics).

Q1284: Should prophylactic antibiotics routinely continue for several days after clean surgery?

A: No. Prolonged prophylaxis generally provides no additional benefit and increases risks.

Q1285: What is the first step in treating a superficial surgical site infection with purulent drainage?

A: Open the incision to allow drainage and manage according to the clinical situation.

Postoperative Fever

Q1286: What is the most common cause of fever within the first 48 hours after surgery?

A: The normal inflammatory response to surgery (after excluding serious complications).

Q1287: What should always be considered in postoperative fever with hypotension and abdominal pain?

A: Anastomotic leak or intra-abdominal sepsis.

Ileus

Q1288: What is postoperative ileus?

A: Temporary impairment of bowel motility following surgery.

Q1289: What symptoms suggest postoperative ileus?

A: Abdominal distension, nausea, vomiting, inability to tolerate oral intake, delayed flatus, and absent or hypoactive bowel sounds.

Q1290: What is the initial management of postoperative ileus?

A: Supportive care: IV fluids, electrolyte correction, minimize opioids, early ambulation, and NG decompression if needed.

Perioperative Anticoagulation

Q1291: Which postoperative complication is VTE prophylaxis designed to prevent?

A: Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE).

Q1292: Which patients often require both mechanical and pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis?

A: High-risk surgical patients without contraindications to anticoagulation.

Q1293: What is the preferred pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis in many hospitalized surgical patients?

A: Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), unless contraindicated.

Postoperative Urinary Retention

Q1294: What are common risk factors for postoperative urinary retention?

A: Neuraxial anesthesia, opioids, older age, male sex, prolonged surgery, and benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH).

Q1295: How is postoperative urinary retention confirmed?

A: Bladder ultrasound (bladder scan).

Q1296: What is the initial treatment for significant postoperative urinary retention?

A: Bladder catheterization (intermittent or indwelling, depending on the clinical situation).

High-Yield Review

Q1297: Which postoperative finding should always raise concern for an anastomotic leak?

A: Persistent unexplained tachycardia, especially with fever or abdominal pain.

Q1298: Which ostomy has the greatest risk of dehydration?

A: Ileostomy.

Q1299: Which postoperative measure most effectively reduces pulmonary and thromboembolic complications?

A: Early ambulation.

Q1300: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for postoperative care?

A: Persistent tachycardia after gastrointestinal surgery should prompt evaluation for an anastomotic leak. ERAS emphasizes early feeding, multimodal analgesia, and early mobilization. Ileostomies require careful fluid and electrolyte monitoring, and enterocutaneous fistulas are managed by controlling sepsis, optimizing nutrition, and achieving source control before definitive repair.

Q1301: A patient has sudden severe abdominal pain with involuntary guarding and rebound tenderness. What is the next best step?

A: Begin resuscitation (IV fluids, analgesia, antibiotics if indicated), obtain urgent surgical consultation, and evaluate for perforation or other surgical emergencies.

Q1302: A chest X-ray shows free air beneath the diaphragm in a patient with severe abdominal pain. What is the diagnosis?

A: Perforated hollow viscus until proven otherwise.

Q1303: What is the next best step after identifying pneumoperitoneum in a patient with generalized peritonitis?

A: Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics, resuscitation, and urgent operative evaluation.

Bowel Obstruction

Q1304: What is the most common cause of small bowel obstruction in developed countries?

A: Postoperative adhesions.

Q1305: A patient with prior abdominal surgery presents with vomiting, abdominal distension, and high-pitched bowel sounds. What is the most likely diagnosis?

A: Adhesive small bowel obstruction.

Q1306: Which CT finding suggests strangulated bowel requiring urgent surgery?

A: Bowel wall ischemia (e.g., poor enhancement), pneumatosis intestinalis, portal venous gas, closed-loop obstruction, or free intraperitoneal air.

Q1307: Which symptoms suggest bowel ischemia rather than uncomplicated obstruction?

A: Fever, leukocytosis, tachycardia, metabolic acidosis, localized peritonitis, or severe continuous pain.

Appendicitis

Q1308: A young adult has periumbilical pain migrating to the right lower quadrant with fever and leukocytosis. What is the diagnosis?

A: Acute appendicitis.

Q1309: What is the preferred imaging study for suspected appendicitis in nonpregnant adults?

A: CT abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast (when appropriate).

Q1310: What is the preferred imaging study for suspected appendicitis in children and pregnant patients?

A: Ultrasound first (MRI if ultrasound is nondiagnostic in pregnancy).

Cholecystitis

Q1311: A patient has fever, right upper quadrant pain, leukocytosis, and a positive Murphy sign. What is the diagnosis?

A: Acute cholecystitis.

Q1312: What is the preferred initial imaging study for suspected acute cholecystitis?

A: Right upper quadrant ultrasound.

Q1313: Which imaging study is most sensitive if the ultrasound is equivocal but suspicion remains high?

A: Hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid (HIDA) scan.

Cholangitis

Q1314: What is Charcot triad?

A: Fever, jaundice, and right upper quadrant pain.

Q1315: What is Reynold pentad?

A: Charcot triad plus hypotension and altered mental status.

Q1316: What is the definitive treatment for ascending cholangitis?

A: Urgent biliary decompression (usually ERCP) after resuscitation and IV antibiotics.

Pancreatitis

Q1317: Which laboratory test is most useful for diagnosing acute pancreatitis?

A: Serum lipase.

Q1318: Which imaging study should not be routinely obtained early in uncomplicated acute pancreatitis?

A: Contrast-enhanced CT (unless diagnosis is uncertain or complications are suspected).

Q1319: What is the cornerstone of initial treatment for acute pancreatitis?

A: Aggressive IV fluids, analgesia, and early enteral nutrition when tolerated.

Mesenteric Ischemia

Q1320: Which symptom is classically associated with acute mesenteric ischemia?

A: Severe abdominal pain out of proportion to the physical examination.

Q1321: What is the preferred diagnostic test in a hemodynamically stable patient with suspected acute mesenteric ischemia?

A: CT angiography.

Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm

Q1322: An older patient develops sudden abdominal pain, hypotension, and a pulsatile abdominal mass. What should be suspected?

A: Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA).

Q1323: What is the next best step in an unstable patient with suspected ruptured AAA?

A: Immediate vascular surgery consultation and operative management without delaying for CT imaging.

Breast

Q1324: What is the next best step for a palpable breast mass in a woman ≥30 years old?

A: Diagnostic mammography with targeted breast ultrasound, followed by biopsy if indicated.

Q1325: What is the preferred biopsy for a suspicious breast lesion?

A: Core needle biopsy.

Thyroid

Q1327: If TSH is suppressed, what is the next best diagnostic test?

Q1328: Which thyroid nodules generally require fine-needle aspiration (FNA)?

Hernias

Q1329: Which hernia is most prone to strangulation?

A: Femoral hernia.

Q1330: Which clinical finding mandates emergency surgery in a patient with a hernia?

A: Signs of strangulation (severe pain, erythema, peritonitis, bowel obstruction, or systemic toxicity).

Testicular Emergencies

Q1331: What is the next best step for suspected testicular torsion?

A: Immediate surgical exploration without delaying for imaging if clinical suspicion is high.

Q1332: What is the classic time window associated with the highest likelihood of testicular salvage?

A: Within approximately 6 hours of symptom onset.

Trauma

Q1333: What is the first priority in trauma management?

Q1334: Which trauma patients should undergo FAST examination?

A: Hemodynamically unstable patients with suspected torso trauma.

Q1335: Which imaging study is preferred for stable patients with blunt abdominal trauma?

Burns

Q1336: Which formula estimates initial fluid requirements for major burns?

A: Parkland formula:
4 mL × body weight (kg) × %TBSA burned

Q1337: How is the Parkland formula administered?

A: Half of the calculated volume in the first 8 hours from the time of burn injury and the remaining half over the next 16 hours.

Surgical Principles

Q1348: Which postoperative vital sign abnormality should never be ignored after gastrointestinal surgery?

A: Persistent unexplained tachycardia.

A: Source control in addition to appropriate antimicrobial therapy.

Q1350: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK comprehensive surgery principle?

A: Know the "next best step." Stabilize first (ABCs), recognize surgical emergencies early (peritonitis, compartment syndrome, tension pneumothorax, ruptured AAA, testicular torsion, obstructed infected kidney, cholangitis), choose the correct first imaging study, and obtain definitive source control without unnecessary delays.

Surgical Principles

Q1396: What is the single most important principle in managing surgical infections?

A: Source control.

A: Early ambulation with pulmonary hygiene (including incentive spirometry when appropriate).

Q1398: What is the most common preventable postoperative complication in hospitalized surgical patients?

A: Venous thromboembolism (DVT/PE).

Q1399: What is the single most common mistake tested on USMLE Step 2 CK surgery questions?

Q1400: What is the single highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK surgical principle?

A: Recognize life-threatening surgical emergencies immediately, stabilize the patient first (ABCs), choose the correct initial imaging only when appropriate, and proceed rapidly to definitive source control or operative management when indicated.

Q2101: What are the four classes of surgical wounds?

A:

Clean
Clean-contaminated
Contaminated
Dirty/infected

Q2102: What defines a clean surgical wound?

A: An uninfected operative wound with no entry into respiratory, GI, genital, or urinary tracts.

Examples:

Hernia repair
Thyroid surgery

Q2103: What defines a clean-contaminated wound?

A: Controlled entry into respiratory, GI, genital, or urinary tract without unusual contamination.

Examples:

Elective bowel surgery

Q2104: What defines a contaminated wound?

A: Open traumatic wounds, gross spillage from GI tract, or major breaks in sterile technique.

Q2105: What defines a dirty/infected wound?

A: Existing infection, pus, perforated viscera, or old traumatic wounds.

Wound Healing

Q2106: What are the three phases of wound healing?

A:

Inflammatory phase
Proliferative phase
Remodeling phase

Q2107: What occurs during the inflammatory phase of wound healing?

A: Hemostasis, platelet activation, and inflammatory cell recruitment.

Q2108: What cells dominate the early inflammatory phase?

A: Neutrophils followed by macrophages.

Q2109: What is the main function of macrophages in wound healing?

A: Remove debris and release growth factors that stimulate repair.

Q2110: What occurs during the proliferative phase?

A:

Fibroblast proliferation
Collagen deposition
Angiogenesis
Granulation tissue formation

Q2111: What collagen type is initially deposited during wound healing?

A: Type III collagen.

Q2112: What collagen type predominates later in wound remodeling?

A: Type I collagen.

Q2113: When does wound tensile strength reach approximately 80% of normal?

A: Around 1 year.

Q2114: What is the maximum tensile strength a healed wound achieves?

A: Approximately 80% of original tissue strength.

Pulmonary Embolism

Q2149: What is the classic presentation of postoperative pulmonary embolism?

Q2150: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for postoperative complications?

A: Recognize complications by timing and clinical pattern. Wound dehiscence occurs around days 5–8 and presents with serosanguineous drainage. Evisceration requires immediate protection of exposed organs and surgical evaluation. Anastomotic leaks cause fever, tachycardia, and sepsis in the first postoperative week. Prevent DVT using early ambulation and appropriate prophylaxis, while treating suspected PE promptly with anticoagulation when safe.

Topic: Pre-operative and post-operative care

Q1: What are the five components of the preoperative assessment?

A: History, physical examination, medication review, assessment of comorbidities (especially cardiac and pulmonary), and determination of operative risk (e.g., ASA classification).

Q2: Which cardiac condition is the strongest predictor of perioperative major adverse cardiac events?

A: Active cardiac disease (unstable angina, recent MI, decompensated heart failure, severe arrhythmia, or severe valvular disease).

Q3: Which medication should generally be continued perioperatively?

A: Chronic β-blockers should be continued to reduce the risk of perioperative cardiac events.

Q4: Which antihypertensive medications are commonly held on the morning of surgery?

A: ACE inhibitors and ARBs, due to the risk of intraoperative hypotension.

Q5: What is the preferred laboratory test to evaluate renal function before surgery?

A: Serum creatinine (with estimated GFR) and basic metabolic panel.

Q6: What is the ASA Physical Status Classification used for?

A: To estimate perioperative risk based on the patient's overall health status.

Q7: What does ASA Class I represent?

A: A healthy patient without systemic disease.

Q8: What does ASA Class V represent?

A: A moribund patient not expected to survive without surgery.

Q9: What is the preferred imaging test before elective surgery in an otherwise healthy patient?

A: None. Routine imaging is not indicated unless clinically warranted.

Q10: Which patients require preoperative ECG?

A: Patients with known cardiovascular disease, significant risk factors, or those undergoing intermediate- or high-risk surgery.

Q11: What is the Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI) used for?

A: To estimate the risk of major cardiac complications after noncardiac surgery.

Q12: Name the six predictors in the RCRI.

A: High-risk surgery, ischemic heart disease, heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, insulin-treated diabetes, and creatinine >2.0 mg/dL.

Q13: Which surgery is considered high cardiac risk?

A: Major vascular surgery.

Q14: What functional capacity generally indicates acceptable operative risk?

A: ≥4 METs.

Q15: Give an example of a 4-MET activity.

A: Climbing one flight of stairs or walking uphill.

Q16: Which patients should receive perioperative stress-dose corticosteroids?

A: Patients on chronic systemic corticosteroid therapy with suspected adrenal suppression.

Q17: When should smoking cessation ideally occur before surgery?

A: At least 4–8 weeks before surgery.

Q18: What is the most effective intervention to reduce postoperative pulmonary complications?

A: Smoking cessation.

Q19: Which patients benefit most from incentive spirometry?

A: Patients undergoing abdominal or thoracic surgery.

Q20: What is the most common postoperative pulmonary complication?

A: Atelectasis.

Q21: What is the most common cause of fever within the first 24 hours after surgery?

A: Normal inflammatory response; atelectasis is commonly tested but is not the primary cause of early postoperative fever.

Q22: What is the first step in evaluating postoperative fever?

A: Assess timing, vital signs, surgical site, and associated symptoms.

Q23: What are the classic "5 Ws" of postoperative fever?

A: Wind, Water, Wound, Walking, Wonder drugs.

Q24: Which postoperative day is wound infection most likely to appear?

A: Postoperative days 5–7.

Q25: Which postoperative complication usually occurs after postoperative day 7?

A: Deep surgical site infection or intra-abdominal abscess.

Q26: What organism is the most common cause of surgical site infection?

A: Staphylococcus aureus.

Q27: What is the most important measure to prevent surgical site infection?

A: Appropriate prophylactic antibiotics administered within 60 minutes before incision.

Q28: When should prophylactic antibiotics generally be discontinued?

A: Within 24 hours after surgery.

Q29: Which antibiotic is most commonly used for clean surgical procedures?

A: Cefazolin.

Q30: Which prophylactic antibiotic is preferred for colorectal surgery?

A: Cefazolin plus metronidazole (or another regimen covering gram-negative and anaerobic organisms).

Q31: What defines a clean surgical wound?

A: No inflammation and no entry into the respiratory, gastrointestinal, genitourinary, or biliary tract.

Q32: What defines a clean-contaminated wound?

A: Controlled entry into the respiratory, gastrointestinal, biliary, or genitourinary tract without significant contamination.

Q33: What defines a contaminated wound?

A: Major break in sterile technique, gross spillage from the GI tract, or fresh traumatic wound.

Q34: What defines a dirty wound?

A: Existing infection, perforated viscus, abscess, or old traumatic wound with devitalized tissue.

Q35: What is primary intention wound healing?

A: Direct closure of wound edges with minimal tissue loss.

Q36: What is secondary intention wound healing?

A: Healing by granulation tissue without surgical closure.

Q37: What is tertiary intention (delayed primary closure)?

A: Leaving a contaminated wound open initially and closing it after infection risk decreases.

Q38: Which phase of wound healing begins immediately after injury?

A: Hemostasis.

Q39: Which cells dominate the inflammatory phase of wound healing after the first 24–48 hours?

A: Macrophages.

Q40: Which cells are primarily responsible for collagen production?

A: Fibroblasts.

Q41: During which phase is type III collagen replaced by type I collagen?

A: Remodeling (maturation) phase.

Q42: Which vitamin deficiency impairs collagen synthesis and wound healing?

A: Vitamin C deficiency.

Q43: Which mineral is essential for wound healing?

A: Zinc.

Q44: What is the most important nutritional factor for wound healing?

A: Adequate protein intake.

Q45: Which chronic disease most impairs wound healing?

A: Diabetes mellitus.

Q46: How does diabetes impair wound healing?

A: Through impaired leukocyte function, reduced perfusion, and decreased collagen synthesis.

Q47: Which medications delay wound healing?

A: Corticosteroids and cytotoxic chemotherapy.

Q48: What is wound dehiscence?

A: Partial or complete separation of a surgical incision after closure.

Q49: On which postoperative days does wound dehiscence most commonly occur?

A: Postoperative days 5–8.

Q50: What is the immediate management of wound dehiscence with evisceration?

A: Cover exposed bowel with sterile saline-moistened dressings, begin IV fluids and broad-spectrum antibiotics as indicated, and take the patient urgently to the operating room for surgical repair.

Topic: Trauma & ATLS 

Q51: What are the four major classes of shock?

A: Hypovolemic, cardiogenic, obstructive, and distributive (septic, anaphylactic, neurogenic).

Q52: What is the most common cause of shock in trauma patients?

A: Hemorrhagic (hypovolemic) shock until proven otherwise.

Q53: What is the earliest physiologic response to hypovolemia?

A: Tachycardia due to sympathetic activation.

Q54: Which vital sign change is a late finding in hypovolemic shock?

A: Hypotension.

Q55: Which laboratory marker is most useful for assessing tissue hypoperfusion?

A: Serum lactate.

Q56: What does an elevated lactate indicate?

A: Tissue hypoxia with anaerobic metabolism.

Q57: What is the initial fluid of choice for hypovolemic shock?

A: Isotonic crystalloid (Lactated Ringer's or Normal Saline).

Q58: Which crystalloid is generally preferred in trauma resuscitation?

A: Lactated Ringer's solution.

Q59: When should blood products be started instead of continued crystalloid?

A: Ongoing significant hemorrhage or failure to respond to initial crystalloid resuscitation.

Q60: What blood product ratio is recommended during massive transfusion?

A: Approximately 1:1:1 (packed RBCs : fresh frozen plasma : platelets).

Q61: Define massive transfusion.

A: Transfusion of ≥10 units of packed RBCs within 24 hours or replacement of one blood volume.

Q62: What complication results from citrate toxicity during massive transfusion?

A: Hypocalcemia.

Q63: Which electrolyte abnormality commonly develops with stored blood transfusion?

A: Hyperkalemia.

Q64: What acid-base disturbance is common early in hemorrhagic shock?

A: High anion gap metabolic acidosis due to lactic acidosis.

Q65: What is the definitive treatment for hemorrhagic shock?

A: Control the source of bleeding.

Septic Shock

Q66: What is the most common cause of distributive shock?

A: Sepsis.

Q67: What are the hemodynamic features of early septic shock?

A: High cardiac output with low systemic vascular resistance (warm shock).

Q68: What is the first step in managing septic shock?

A: Rapid administration of IV crystalloids and broad-spectrum antibiotics.

Q69: How much crystalloid should initially be given in septic shock?

A: Approximately 30 mL/kg.

Q70: Which vasopressor is first-line if hypotension persists after fluids?

A: Norepinephrine.

Q71: What is the target mean arterial pressure (MAP) in septic shock?

A: ≥65 mmHg.

Q72: Besides antibiotics, what is the most important treatment principle in sepsis?

A: Source control (e.g., drain abscess, remove infected catheter).

Cardiogenic Shock

Q73: What is the most common cause of cardiogenic shock?

A: Acute myocardial infarction.

Q74: How do pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) and cardiac output change in cardiogenic shock?

A: Increased PCWP with decreased cardiac output.

Q75: Why should large-volume fluid boluses generally be avoided in cardiogenic shock?

A: They worsen pulmonary edema.

Obstructive Shock

Q76: Name three common causes of obstructive shock.

A: Cardiac tamponade, tension pneumothorax, and massive pulmonary embolism.

Q77: What are Beck's triad findings in cardiac tamponade?

A: Hypotension, jugular venous distention, and muffled heart sounds.

Q78: What is the definitive treatment for cardiac tamponade?

A: Pericardiocentesis.

Q79: What is the immediate treatment for tension pneumothorax?

A: Immediate needle decompression followed by chest tube placement.

Q80: Which side should undergo needle decompression for a tension pneumothorax?

A: The affected side.

Fluids & Electrolytes

Q81: Which IV fluid most closely resembles plasma electrolyte composition?

A: Lactated Ringer's solution.

Q82: Which IV fluid can worsen hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis?

A: Normal saline.

Q83: Which electrolyte abnormality most commonly develops after prolonged nasogastric suction?

A: Hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis.

Q84: Which electrolyte abnormality commonly occurs after prolonged vomiting?

A: Hypokalemia.

Q85: Why does hypokalemia often persist despite potassium replacement?

A: Coexisting hypomagnesemia impairs potassium repletion.

Q86: Which electrolyte should be corrected before potassium if both are low?

A: Magnesium.

Q87: Which ECG change is classically seen in hyperkalemia?

A: Peaked T waves.

Q88: What is the first medication given for life-threatening hyperkalemia with ECG changes?

A: IV calcium gluconate (or calcium chloride in selected settings) to stabilize the cardiac membrane.

Q89: Which medications shift potassium into cells?

A: Insulin with dextrose, β₂-agonists (e.g., albuterol), and sodium bicarbonate (if severe metabolic acidosis).

Q90: Which therapy removes potassium from the body most rapidly in severe or refractory hyperkalemia?

A: Hemodialysis.

Acid–Base Disorders

Q91: What acid-base disorder is most commonly seen in septic shock?

A: High anion gap metabolic acidosis.

Q92: What acid-base disorder results from prolonged vomiting?

A: Metabolic alkalosis.

Q93: What acid-base disorder is associated with severe diarrhea?

A: Non-anion gap (hyperchloremic) metabolic acidosis.

Q94: Which formula estimates expected respiratory compensation in metabolic acidosis?

A: Winter's formula: Expected PaCO₂ = 1.5(HCO₃⁻) + 8 ± 2.

Q95: How is the anion gap calculated?

A: AG = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻).

Blood Transfusion

Q96: What is the usual hemoglobin threshold for transfusion in stable hospitalized adults?

A: <7 g/dL, unless there are special clinical indications (e.g., active ischemia, ongoing hemorrhage).

Q97: Which blood product is used to reverse warfarin-associated life-threatening bleeding?

A: Four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) plus IV vitamin K.

Q98: Which blood product is indicated for isolated fibrinogen deficiency?

A: Cryoprecipitate.

Q99: What is the most common acute complication of blood transfusion?

A: Febrile nonhemolytic transfusion reaction.

Q100: What is the most dangerous acute transfusion reaction?

A: Acute hemolytic transfusion reaction due to ABO incompatibility. Immediate management includes stopping the transfusion, maintaining IV access with normal saline, providing supportive care, and notifying the blood bank.

Q101: What are the priorities of the ATLS primary survey?

A: A-B-C-D-E: Airway with cervical spine protection, Breathing, Circulation with hemorrhage control, Disability (neurologic assessment), Exposure with prevention of hypothermia.

Q102: What is the first priority in trauma management?

A: Secure the airway while maintaining cervical spine immobilization.

Q103: What does the "A" in the ATLS primary survey include?

A: Airway assessment, cervical spine stabilization, suctioning, airway adjuncts, and definitive airway if indicated.

Q104: Which trauma patients require cervical spine immobilization?

A: Any patient with significant blunt trauma, altered mental status, neurologic deficits, neck pain/tenderness, or distracting injuries until the cervical spine is cleared.

Q105: What finding suggests a patent airway?

A: The patient can speak clearly in full sentences.

Q106: What are indications for immediate endotracheal intubation in trauma?

A: Airway obstruction, inability to protect the airway (GCS ≤8), respiratory failure, severe facial trauma, or impending airway compromise.

Q107: What is the preferred airway technique in trauma patients with suspected cervical spine injury?

A: Rapid sequence intubation with manual in-line cervical stabilization.

Q108: When is a surgical airway indicated?

A: Failed intubation with inability to oxygenate or severe upper airway obstruction preventing oral or nasal intubation.

Q109: What is the preferred emergency surgical airway in adults?

A: Cricothyrotomy.

Q110: What is the most common preventable cause of death in trauma?

A: Airway obstruction.

Breathing

Q111: What is assessed during the "B" portion of the primary survey?

A: Respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, chest wall movement, breath sounds, tracheal position, and signs of thoracic injury.

Q112: What are the six immediately life-threatening chest injuries identified during the primary survey?

A: Airway obstruction, tension pneumothorax, open pneumothorax, massive hemothorax, flail chest, and cardiac tamponade.

Q113: What clinical findings suggest tension pneumothorax?

A: Severe respiratory distress, hypotension, unilateral absent breath sounds, hyperresonance, tracheal deviation (late), and jugular venous distention.

Q114: Should a chest x-ray be obtained before treating a suspected tension pneumothorax?

A: No. Treat immediately based on clinical diagnosis.

Q115: What is the definitive treatment for tension pneumothorax?

A: Tube thoracostomy after immediate needle or finger decompression.

Q116: What is an open pneumothorax?

A: A chest wall defect that allows free communication between the pleural space and the atmosphere ("sucking chest wound").

Q117: How should an open pneumothorax initially be managed?

A: Apply a three-sided occlusive dressing and place a chest tube away from the wound.

Q118: What defines a massive hemothorax?

A: >1,500 mL of blood immediately after chest tube placement or continued drainage >200 mL/hour for 2–4 hours.

Q119: What is the initial treatment for massive hemothorax?

A: Large-bore chest tube, aggressive resuscitation, and urgent thoracic surgical consultation.

Q120: What injury causes paradoxical chest wall movement?

A: Flail chest.

Q121: What is the most common associated injury with flail chest?

A: Pulmonary contusion.

Q122: What is the primary treatment for pulmonary contusion?

A: Supportive care with adequate oxygenation, pain control, and cautious fluid administration.

Circulation

Q123: What is the leading preventable cause of death after trauma?

A: Uncontrolled hemorrhage.

Q124: What is the first intervention for severe external bleeding?

A: Direct pressure.

Q125: When should a tourniquet be used?

A: Life-threatening extremity hemorrhage not controlled with direct pressure.

Q126: Which IV access is recommended in unstable trauma patients?

A: Two large-bore peripheral IV lines (14–16 gauge) or intraosseous access if IV access cannot be rapidly obtained.

Q127: Which crystalloid is preferred during initial trauma resuscitation?

A: Lactated Ringer's solution.

Q128: When should blood products replace crystalloid during trauma resuscitation?

A: Persistent hypotension or suspected major hemorrhage.

Q129: What blood pressure strategy is recommended in uncontrolled hemorrhage without traumatic brain injury?

A: Permissive hypotension (target SBP ≈80–90 mmHg) until bleeding is controlled.

Q130: Why is permissive hypotension avoided in traumatic brain injury?

A: Because adequate cerebral perfusion pressure must be maintained.

Disability

Q131: What does the "D" in the primary survey assess?

A: Neurologic status using GCS, pupil examination, and limb movement.

Q132: What are the components of the Glasgow Coma Scale?

A: Eye opening, verbal response, and motor response.

Q133: What GCS score indicates severe traumatic brain injury?

A: ≤8.

Q134: What pupil finding suggests uncal herniation?

A: Unilateral fixed, dilated pupil.

Exposure

Q135: Why is complete exposure necessary in trauma patients?

A: To identify occult injuries while preventing missed diagnoses.

Q136: What important complication must be prevented after exposing the patient?

A: Hypothermia.

FAST Examination

Q137: What does FAST stand for?

A: Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma.

Q138: What is the purpose of the FAST examination?

A: Rapid detection of free intraperitoneal or pericardial fluid.

Q139: Which four areas are evaluated during a FAST examination?

A: Right upper quadrant (Morison pouch), left upper quadrant, pelvis, and pericardium.

Q140: In an unstable trauma patient with a positive FAST examination, what is the next step?

A: Immediate exploratory laparotomy.

Q141: What is the next step if the FAST examination is negative but the patient remains hemodynamically unstable?

A: Continue evaluation for other bleeding sources (thorax, pelvis, retroperitoneum, long bones) and repeat FAST or proceed with further indicated interventions.

Secondary Survey

Q142: When is the secondary survey performed?

A: After completion of the primary survey and stabilization of life-threatening conditions.

Q143: What is included in the secondary survey?

A: Complete head-to-toe examination, detailed history (AMPLE), and selective imaging/laboratory studies.

Q144: What does the AMPLE history include?

A: Allergies, Medications, Past medical history/Pregnancy, Last meal, Events surrounding injury.

Imaging

Q145: Which imaging study is preferred for hemodynamically stable blunt abdominal trauma?

A: Contrast-enhanced CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis.

Q146: Which imaging study is preferred for unstable blunt abdominal trauma?

A: FAST examination.

Q147: Is diagnostic peritoneal lavage (DPL) commonly used today?

A: No. It has largely been replaced by FAST but may be useful when FAST is unavailable or equivocal.

Trauma Decision-Making

Q148: Which factor is more important than CT findings when deciding trauma management?

A: Hemodynamic stability.

Q149: What is the definitive treatment for hemorrhagic shock in trauma?

A: Surgical or procedural control of bleeding.

Q150: What is the single most important principle in trauma care?

A: Treat immediately life-threatening injuries as they are identified—do not delay life-saving interventions for diagnostic testing.
Blunt Abdominal Trauma

Liver Trauma

Bladder Trauma

Urethral Trauma

Q179: What study should be obtained before Foley catheter placement if urethral injury is suspected?

A: Blind urethral catheterization.

Pelvic Trauma

Q181: Why are pelvic fractures potentially life-threatening?

Q183: What is the definitive treatment for ongoing pelvic hemorrhage despite resuscitation?

Q184: Which pelvic fracture pattern has the highest risk of major hemorrhage?

Thoracic Trauma

Q186: What is the initial management of most hemothoraces?

Q187: Which injury causes persistent large air leak after chest tube placement?

A: Major tracheobronchial injury.

Q188: What symptom is classic for esophageal rupture after trauma?

Cardiac Trauma

Q190: What is the most common blunt cardiac injury?

Q191: Which tests are used to evaluate suspected blunt cardiac injury?

A: ECG and cardiac troponin; echocardiography if clinically indicated.

Q192: What is the most common ECG finding in myocardial contusion?

A: Sinus tachycardia or nonspecific ST-T wave abnormalities.

Damage Control Surgery

Q193: What is the goal of damage-control surgery?

A: Rapid control of hemorrhage and contamination, followed by physiologic stabilization before definitive repair.

Q194: Which "lethal triad" indicates the need for damage-control surgery?

A: Hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy.

Q195: Why is hypothermia dangerous in trauma patients?

A: It worsens coagulopathy and increases mortality.

Trauma Pearls

Q196: What is the most important determinant of trauma management?

A: Hemodynamic stability.

Q197: Which trauma patients require immediate surgery without CT imaging?

A: Hemodynamically unstable patients with evidence of surgically correctable hemorrhage or diffuse peritonitis.

Q198: What is the leading cause of preventable trauma death after hospital arrival?

A: Uncontrolled hemorrhage.

Q199: What is the cornerstone of successful trauma resuscitation?

A: Early hemorrhage control, balanced blood product resuscitation, and correction of coagulopathy.

Q200: What is the single highest-yield ATLS principle for the USMLE Step 2 CK?

A: Always prioritize life-threatening injuries using the ABCDE approach. Hemodynamic instability takes precedence over diagnostic imaging, and definitive hemorrhage control is the key to survival.

Topic: Burns & Wound Management

Q201: How are burns classified?

A: By depth: superficial (first-degree), superficial partial-thickness, deep partial-thickness, and full-thickness (third-degree). Fourth-degree burns extend into muscle, tendon, or bone.

Q202: Which skin layers are involved in a superficial (first-degree) burn?

A: Epidermis only.

Q203: What are the clinical features of a superficial burn?

A: Erythema, pain, dry skin, no blisters, and blanching.

Q204: What is the classic example of a superficial burn?

A: Sunburn.

Q205: Which skin layers are involved in a superficial partial-thickness burn?

A: Epidermis and superficial dermis.

Q206: What are the clinical features of a superficial partial-thickness burn?

A: Blisters, moist appearance, severe pain, and brisk capillary refill.

Q207: Which skin layers are involved in a deep partial-thickness burn?

A: Epidermis and deep dermis.

Q208: What are the clinical features of a deep partial-thickness burn?

A: Pale or mottled appearance, decreased sensation, delayed capillary refill, and reduced spontaneous healing.

Q209: Which skin layers are involved in a full-thickness burn?

A: Entire epidermis and dermis are destroyed.

Q210: What are the clinical features of a full-thickness burn?

A: White, brown, or charred skin; leathery texture; absent sensation within the burn; and absent capillary refill.

Burn Assessment

Q211: Which method estimates burn size in adults?

A: Rule of Nines.

Q212: According to the Rule of Nines, what percentage of TBSA does each arm represent in adults?

A: 9% (4.5% anterior + 4.5% posterior).

Q213: According to the Rule of Nines, what percentage of TBSA does each leg represent?

A: 18% (9% anterior + 9% posterior).

Q214: What percentage of TBSA does the anterior trunk represent?

A: 18%.

Q215: What percentage of TBSA does the posterior trunk represent?

A: 18%.

Q216: What percentage of TBSA does the head represent in adults?

A: 9%.

Q217: What percentage of TBSA does the perineum represent?

A: 1%.

Q218: Which burn assessment tool is more accurate in children?

A: Lund–Browder chart.

Q219: Which burns are included when calculating TBSA for fluid resuscitation?

A: Partial-thickness and full-thickness burns only.

Q220: Are first-degree burns included in TBSA calculations?

A: No.

Initial Burn Management

Q221: What is the first priority in major burn management?

A: Airway assessment and stabilization (ABCs).

Q222: Which burn patients should receive high-flow oxygen immediately?

A: Any patient with suspected inhalation injury or smoke exposure.

Q223: Which findings suggest inhalation injury?

A: Facial burns, singed nasal hairs, soot in the mouth or sputum, hoarseness, stridor, carbonaceous sputum, or burns sustained in an enclosed space.

Q224: When should early endotracheal intubation be strongly considered in burn patients?

A: Progressive facial/airway edema, stridor, respiratory distress, or significant inhalation injury.

Q225: Why is early intubation preferred in severe inhalation injury?

A: Airway edema can rapidly progress, making later intubation difficult or impossible.

Fluid Resuscitation

Q226: Which formula is used for initial fluid resuscitation in major burns?

A: Parkland formula.

Q227: What is the Parkland formula?

A: 4 mL × body weight (kg) × %TBSA burned using Lactated Ringer's solution.

Q228: Over what time period is the Parkland formula applied?

A: The first 24 hours after the burn.

Q229: How should the calculated Parkland fluid be administered?

A: 50% in the first 8 hours from the time of injury, and the remaining 50% over the next 16 hours.

Q230: Which IV fluid is preferred for burn resuscitation?

A: Lactated Ringer's solution.

Q231: What is the best endpoint of adequate burn resuscitation in adults?

A: Urine output of ≥0.5 mL/kg/hour.

Q232: What is the urine output goal in children with major burns?

A: Approximately 1 mL/kg/hour.

Q233: Why should fluid administration be adjusted rather than relying solely on the Parkland calculation?

A: The formula provides an initial estimate; fluids should be titrated based on clinical response, especially urine output and perfusion.

Burn Center Referral

Q234: Which adult burn patients generally require burn center referral?

A: Partial-thickness burns >10% TBSA, burns involving the face, hands, feet, genitalia, perineum, major joints, full-thickness burns, inhalation injury, electrical burns, chemical burns, or significant comorbidities.

Q235: Which burn location is particularly important because of cosmetic and functional outcomes?

A: Face and hands.

Burn Wound Care

Q236: What is the first step in local burn wound management?

A: Cool the burn with cool (not ice) running water if done shortly after injury, then gently clean the wound.

Q237: Why should ice not be applied directly to burns?

A: It may worsen tissue injury through vasoconstriction and cold-induced damage.

Q238: What should be done with intact burn blisters?

A: Management depends on size and location; large, tense, or function-limiting blisters are often debrided, whereas small intact blisters may be left intact.

Q239: Which topical antimicrobial is commonly used for partial-thickness burns?

A: Bacitracin is commonly used for superficial partial-thickness burns; topical therapy varies by burn depth and location.

Q240: Which topical burn agent is avoided on the face due to cosmetic concerns and delayed healing?

A: Silver sulfadiazine is generally avoided on the face and is no longer preferred for many superficial burns because it may delay wound healing.

Burn Complications

Q241: What is the most common cause of death during the first 24 hours after a major burn?

A: Burn shock (hypovolemic shock from plasma loss).

Q242: What is the major cause of death after the first several days in severe burns?

A: Sepsis and multiorgan dysfunction.

Q243: Why are burn patients at high risk for infection?

A: Loss of the skin barrier and burn-induced immune dysfunction.

Q244: When are prophylactic systemic antibiotics indicated for uncomplicated burns?

A: They are not routinely indicated; antibiotics are reserved for documented infection or specific indications.

Escharotomy & Fasciotomy

Q245: What is an escharotomy?

A: Surgical incision through constricting burn eschar to relieve pressure and restore circulation or ventilation.

Q246: When is escharotomy indicated?

A: Circumferential full-thickness burns causing vascular compromise or impaired chest wall expansion.

Q247: How does escharotomy differ from fasciotomy?

A: Escharotomy releases the burned skin (eschar); fasciotomy releases the deep fascia to treat compartment syndrome.

Electrical & Chemical Burns

Q248: What cardiac evaluation is recommended after a significant electrical injury?

A: ECG for all significant electrical injuries, with cardiac monitoring if there are ECG abnormalities, loss of consciousness, high-voltage exposure, or concerning symptoms.

Q249: What is the major muscle complication of high-voltage electrical burns?

A: Rhabdomyolysis, which can lead to acute kidney injury.

Q250: What is the first step in managing a chemical burn?

A: Remove contaminated clothing and immediately irrigate the affected area with copious amounts of water (except for a few specific chemicals that require special management).

Topic: Surgical Infections, Soft Tissue Infections & Wound Care

Q251: What is the mechanism of carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning?

A: CO binds hemoglobin with an affinity approximately 200–250 times greater than oxygen, reducing oxygen delivery and impairing tissue oxygenation.

Q252: Why can pulse oximetry appear normal in carbon monoxide poisoning?

A: Standard pulse oximeters cannot distinguish oxyhemoglobin from carboxyhemoglobin.

Q253: What is the best diagnostic test for carbon monoxide poisoning?

A: Arterial or venous blood gas with co-oximetry to measure carboxyhemoglobin.

Q254: What is the initial treatment for carbon monoxide poisoning?

A: 100% oxygen via non-rebreather mask.

Q255: When should hyperbaric oxygen therapy be considered in carbon monoxide poisoning?

A: Severe neurologic symptoms, myocardial ischemia, significant metabolic acidosis, pregnancy with significant exposure, or markedly elevated carboxyhemoglobin levels (clinical judgment and local protocols apply).

Q256: Which poison is commonly associated with smoke inhalation from enclosed-space fires?

A: Cyanide.

Q257: What is the antidote for suspected cyanide poisoning?

A: Hydroxocobalamin is first-line in most settings.

Q258: What laboratory finding suggests cyanide poisoning?

A: Severe lactic acidosis with markedly elevated serum lactate.

Compartment Syndrome

Q259: What is compartment syndrome?

A: Increased pressure within a closed fascial compartment causing reduced tissue perfusion and ischemia.

Q260: What is the earliest and most sensitive symptom of compartment syndrome?

A: Severe pain out of proportion to the injury, especially pain with passive stretch.

Q261: What are the classic "6 Ps" of compartment syndrome?

A: Pain, paresthesia, pallor, paralysis, pulselessness, and poikilothermia (pulselessness is a late finding).

Q262: Which finding is usually present before pulselessness develops?

A: Pain with passive stretch.

Q263: What is the definitive treatment for compartment syndrome?

A: Emergent fasciotomy.

Q264: Should treatment be delayed while awaiting imaging if compartment syndrome is strongly suspected?

A: No. It is a surgical emergency.

Necrotizing Soft Tissue Infection

Q265: What is necrotizing fasciitis?

A: A rapidly progressive infection of the fascia with secondary necrosis of subcutaneous tissue.

Q266: What is the hallmark clinical feature of necrotizing fasciitis?

A: Severe pain out of proportion to physical examination findings.

Q267: What skin findings may develop as necrotizing fasciitis progresses?

A: Bullae, ecchymosis, skin discoloration, crepitus, anesthesia, and tissue necrosis.

Q268: Which imaging finding may support the diagnosis of necrotizing fasciitis?

A: Gas within the soft tissues (although imaging should not delay surgery).

Q269: What is the definitive management of necrotizing fasciitis?

A: Immediate surgical exploration and aggressive debridement plus broad-spectrum IV antibiotics.

Q270: Which antibiotic regimen provides broad empiric coverage for necrotizing fasciitis?

A: Broad-spectrum therapy (e.g., vancomycin + piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem) plus clindamycin to inhibit toxin production when streptococcal infection is suspected.

Q271: Why is clindamycin useful in necrotizing fasciitis?

A: It suppresses bacterial toxin production, particularly in streptococcal infections.

Fournier Gangrene

Q272: What is Fournier gangrene?

A: Necrotizing fasciitis involving the perineum, genitalia, or perianal region.

Q273: Which patients are at highest risk for Fournier gangrene?

A: Patients with diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, alcoholism, obesity, or chronic kidney disease.

Q274: What is the treatment of Fournier gangrene?

A: Immediate aggressive surgical debridement, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics, and hemodynamic support.

Cellulitis & Abscess

Q275: What is cellulitis?

A: A diffuse bacterial infection of the skin and subcutaneous tissue without a localized pus collection.

Q276: Which organisms most commonly cause cellulitis?

A: Streptococcus pyogenes and Staphylococcus aureus.

Q277: What is the hallmark of an abscess?

A: A localized, fluctuant collection of pus.

Q278: What is the definitive treatment for a simple cutaneous abscess?

A: Incision and drainage (I&D).

Q279: Are antibiotics always required after I&D of an uncomplicated abscess?

A: No. Antibiotics are reserved for selected patients (e.g., extensive cellulitis, systemic illness, immunocompromise, or high-risk features).

Q280: Which pathogen should be considered in recurrent skin abscesses?

A: Community-acquired methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (CA-MRSA).

Bite Wounds

Q281: Which animal bite has the highest infection rate?

A: Cat bites.

Q282: Which organism is classically associated with dog and cat bites?

A: Pasteurella multocida.

Q283: What is the preferred prophylactic antibiotic for most dog and cat bites?

A: Amoxicillin-clavulanate.

Q284: Which human bite injury is particularly high risk for infection?

A: Closed-fist ("fight bite") injuries over the metacarpophalangeal joints.

Q285: Which organism is classically associated with human bites?

A: Eikenella corrodens.

Q286: Why should fight-bite injuries receive careful surgical evaluation?

A: They may involve tendons, joints, or deep spaces of the hand and have a high risk of serious infection.

Tetanus

Q287: Which organism causes tetanus?

A: Clostridium tetani.

Q288: What is the mechanism of tetanus toxin?

A: It blocks release of inhibitory neurotransmitters (GABA and glycine), causing muscle rigidity and spasms.

Q289: Which wound characteristics increase tetanus risk?

A: Dirty, contaminated, puncture, crush, devitalized, or necrotic wounds.

Q290: When is tetanus immune globulin (TIG) indicated?

A: In patients with high-risk wounds who are inadequately immunized or have an unknown vaccination history, in addition to tetanus vaccination when indicated.

Surgical Site Infection (SSI)

Q291: What are the cardinal signs of a surgical site infection?

A: Increasing pain, erythema, warmth, swelling, purulent drainage, and fever.

Q292: What is the first step in managing a superficial incisional SSI?

A: Open the incision if needed to allow drainage, assess the wound, and provide appropriate local care; antibiotics are used when clinically indicated.

Q293: What is the most common pathogen causing postoperative wound infections?

A: Staphylococcus aureus.

Surgical Drains

Q294: Why are surgical drains used?

A: To evacuate fluid, blood, bile, or pus and reduce fluid accumulation.

Q295: What is the difference between an open and a closed surgical drain?

A: Open drains empty onto dressings; closed drains collect fluid in a sealed system and generally have a lower infection risk.

Q296: Name two commonly used closed suction drains.

A: Jackson-Pratt (JP) drain and Hemovac drain.

Q297: What does bilious output from a postoperative drain suggest?

A: A possible bile leak or enteric injury, depending on the surgical procedure.

Q298: What does feculent drain output suggest?

A: An enteric leak or bowel injury.

Wound Healing Pearls

Q299: Which patients are at increased risk of postoperative wound infection?

A: Patients with diabetes, obesity, smoking history, malnutrition, immunosuppression, prolonged operations, or contaminated wounds.

Q300: What is the single most effective measure to reduce surgical site infections?

A: Meticulous surgical technique with appropriate perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis, strict sterile practice, and good perioperative glycemic control when indicated.

Topic: Hernias

Q301: What is a hernia?

A: Protrusion of an organ or tissue through a weakness or defect in the surrounding fascia or muscle.

Q302: What is the most common abdominal wall hernia?

A: Inguinal hernia.

Q303: Which sex is more commonly affected by inguinal hernias?

A: Males.

Q304: What is the most common type of groin hernia?

A: Indirect inguinal hernia.

Q305: Which groin hernia has the highest risk of strangulation?

A: Femoral hernia.

Inguinal Anatomy

Q306: What forms the deep (internal) inguinal ring?

A: An opening in the transversalis fascia located lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels.

Q307: What forms the superficial (external) inguinal ring?

A: An opening in the external oblique aponeurosis.

Q308: Which ligament forms the floor of the inguinal canal?

A: Inguinal ligament.

Q309: Which structure forms the posterior wall of the inguinal canal?

A: Transversalis fascia (reinforced medially by the conjoint tendon).

Q310: What structures pass through the inguinal canal in males?

A: Spermatic cord and the ilioinguinal nerve.

Indirect Inguinal Hernia

Q311: Where does an indirect inguinal hernia pass in relation to the inferior epigastric vessels?

A: Lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels.

Q312: Through which ring does an indirect inguinal hernia enter?

A: Deep inguinal ring.

Q313: What is the embryologic cause of an indirect inguinal hernia?

A: Failure of the processus vaginalis to close.

Q314: Can an indirect inguinal hernia extend into the scrotum?

A: Yes.

Q315: Which age group most commonly develops indirect inguinal hernias?

A: Infants, children, and young adults, although they can occur at any age.

Direct Inguinal Hernia

Q316: Where does a direct inguinal hernia occur in relation to the inferior epigastric vessels?

A: Medial to the inferior epigastric vessels.

Q317: Through which anatomical area does a direct inguinal hernia protrude?

A: Hesselbach triangle.

Q318: What are the boundaries of Hesselbach triangle?

A: Inferior epigastric vessels (lateral), lateral border of the rectus abdominis (medial), and inguinal ligament (inferior).

Q319: Does a direct inguinal hernia usually pass through the deep inguinal ring?

A: No.

Q320: Does a direct inguinal hernia commonly enter the scrotum?

A: No, although very large hernias occasionally may.

Femoral Hernia

Q321: Where is a femoral hernia located relative to the inguinal ligament?

A: Inferior to the inguinal ligament.

Q322: Which patients most commonly develop femoral hernias?

A: Women, especially older women and those with multiple pregnancies.

Q323: Why are femoral hernias surgically important?

A: They have a high risk of incarceration and strangulation.

Q324: What is the recommended treatment for a femoral hernia?

A: Elective surgical repair, even if asymptomatic in most patients, because of the high risk of complications.

Clinical Presentation

Q325: What is the most common symptom of an uncomplicated inguinal hernia?

A: Groin bulge that enlarges with standing, lifting, or coughing and decreases when supine.

Q326: What physical examination maneuver helps identify a groin hernia?

A: Ask the patient to cough or perform a Valsalva maneuver while palpating the groin.

Q327: What is a reducible hernia?

A: A hernia whose contents can be returned to the abdominal cavity manually or spontaneously.

Q328: What is an incarcerated hernia?

A: A nonreducible hernia without evidence of compromised blood supply.

Q329: What is a strangulated hernia?

A: An incarcerated hernia with compromised blood flow, resulting in ischemia.

Q330: What symptoms suggest strangulation?

A: Severe pain, erythema, fever, tachycardia, leukocytosis, and signs of bowel obstruction or peritonitis.

Hernia Complications

Q331: What is the most dangerous complication of any hernia?

A: Strangulation.

Q332: What complication results from prolonged strangulation?

A: Bowel ischemia, necrosis, perforation, and sepsis.

Q333: What are symptoms of bowel obstruction from a hernia?

A: Abdominal pain, vomiting, abdominal distention, and inability to pass stool or flatus.

Q334: What is the treatment for a strangulated hernia?

A: Emergency surgical exploration and repair.

Diagnosis

Q335: How is an inguinal hernia usually diagnosed?

A: Clinical history and physical examination.

Q336: Which imaging modality is preferred if the diagnosis is uncertain?

A: Groin ultrasound; CT or MRI may be used in selected cases.

Q337: Which imaging modality is most useful for occult or recurrent groin hernias?

A: MRI has the highest sensitivity in difficult cases.

Surgical Management

Q338: What is the definitive treatment for symptomatic inguinal hernias?

A: Surgical repair.

Q339: Which repair technique is the standard for most adult inguinal hernias?

A: Tension-free mesh repair.

Q340: When is tissue repair preferred over mesh repair?

A: When mesh is contraindicated, such as in contaminated or infected surgical fields.

Q341: What are the two major surgical approaches for inguinal hernia repair?

A: Open repair and laparoscopic repair.

Q342: Which patients particularly benefit from laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair?

A: Patients with bilateral or recurrent inguinal hernias and selected patients desiring faster recovery.

Q343: What is the major advantage of laparoscopic repair?

A: Less postoperative pain and earlier return to normal activities in appropriately selected patients.

Special Hernias

Q344: What is a Richter hernia?

A: Herniation of only part of the bowel wall (antimesenteric border), which may strangulate without causing complete bowel obstruction.

Q345: Why is a Richter hernia clinically important?

A: Patients may develop bowel ischemia without classic signs of intestinal obstruction.

Q346: What is a sliding hernia?

A: A hernia in which part of the hernia sac wall is formed by a retroperitoneal organ, commonly the colon or bladder.

Q347: What is an incisional hernia?

A: Herniation through a previous surgical incision due to fascial weakness or failure of healing.

Q348: Which factors increase the risk of incisional hernia?

A: Wound infection, obesity, smoking, malnutrition, diabetes, chronic cough, ascites, and poor surgical healing.

Q349: What is an umbilical hernia?

A: Herniation through the umbilical ring.

Q350: When should an adult umbilical hernia be repaired?

A: Symptomatic hernias, enlarging hernias, incarcerated or strangulated hernias, and many elective cases because spontaneous closure is uncommon in adults.

Q351: What is the most common cause of dysphagia in adults?

A: Mechanical obstruction (e.g., peptic stricture, esophageal cancer) or a motility disorder (e.g., achalasia); the differential depends on whether dysphagia is for solids only or solids and liquids.

Q352: What pattern of dysphagia suggests a mechanical obstruction?

A: Progressive dysphagia for solids first, later progressing to liquids.

Q353: What pattern of dysphagia suggests a motility disorder?

A: Dysphagia to both solids and liquids from the onset.

Q354: What is the initial diagnostic test for esophageal dysphagia with alarm features (e.g., weight loss, anemia, progressive symptoms)?

A: Upper endoscopy (EGD).

Q355: What is the gold standard diagnostic test for achalasia?

A: Esophageal manometry.

Q356: What are the classic manometric findings in achalasia?

A: Incomplete relaxation of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) and absent normal esophageal peristalsis.

Q357: What is the classic barium swallow finding in achalasia?

A: Bird's-beak appearance.

Q358: What is the definitive treatment for achalasia in a good surgical candidate?

A: Laparoscopic Heller myotomy (often combined with a partial fundoplication) or peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM), depending on patient factors and expertise.

Q359: Which temporary treatment may be used in poor surgical candidates with achalasia?

A: Botulinum toxin injection into the LES or pneumatic dilation in selected patients.

Q360: What malignancy is associated with long-standing achalasia?

A: Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma.

Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD)

Q361: What is the most common cause of GERD?

A: Incompetent lower esophageal sphincter.

Q362: What is the initial treatment for uncomplicated GERD?

A: Lifestyle modification and proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy.

Q363: Which patients with GERD should undergo upper endoscopy?

A: Patients with alarm symptoms (dysphagia, GI bleeding, anemia, weight loss, recurrent vomiting) or persistent symptoms despite appropriate therapy.

Q364: What is Barrett esophagus?

A: Replacement of normal squamous epithelium by specialized intestinal-type columnar epithelium due to chronic GERD.

Q365: Why is Barrett esophagus clinically important?

A: It increases the risk of esophageal adenocarcinoma.

Q366: Which type of esophageal cancer is associated with Barrett esophagus?

A: Adenocarcinoma.

Q367: Which operation is the classic surgical treatment for refractory GERD?

A: Nissen fundoplication.

Hiatal Hernias

Q368: What are the two major types of hiatal hernia?

A: Sliding hiatal hernia and paraesophageal (rolling) hiatal hernia.

Q369: Which type of hiatal hernia is most common?

A: Sliding hiatal hernia.

Q370: Which hiatal hernia is most strongly associated with GERD?

A: Sliding hiatal hernia.

Q371: Which hiatal hernia has the greatest risk of volvulus and strangulation?

A: Paraesophageal (rolling) hiatal hernia.

Q372: What is the treatment for symptomatic paraesophageal hernia?

A: Surgical repair.

Esophageal Perforation

Q373: What is Boerhaave syndrome?

A: Spontaneous transmural esophageal rupture, usually after forceful vomiting.

Q374: What is the classic presentation of Boerhaave syndrome?

A: Severe chest pain after vomiting, subcutaneous emphysema, and systemic toxicity (Mackler triad: vomiting, chest pain, subcutaneous emphysema).

Q375: What is the preferred initial imaging study for suspected esophageal perforation?

A: Water-soluble contrast esophagram (often followed by CT if needed).

Q376: What is the treatment for esophageal perforation?

A: Urgent broad-spectrum antibiotics, NPO status, IV fluids, and prompt surgical or endoscopic management depending on the location, size, timing, and patient stability.

Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)

Q377: What are the two most common causes of peptic ulcer disease?

A: Helicobacter pylori infection and NSAID use.

Q378: Which organism causes most duodenal ulcers?

A: Helicobacter pylori.

Q379: What is the preferred diagnostic test for H. pylori in patients without alarm features?

A: Urea breath test or stool antigen test (off PPIs for an appropriate interval before testing).

Q380: What is the first-line treatment for H. pylori infection?

A: Guideline-recommended eradication therapy (commonly bismuth quadruple therapy in areas with high macrolide resistance, with confirmation of eradication after treatment).

Perforated Peptic Ulcer

Q381: What is the classic presentation of a perforated peptic ulcer?

A: Sudden severe epigastric pain with a rigid, board-like abdomen and signs of peritonitis.

Q382: Which imaging finding suggests perforated peptic ulcer?

A: Free air under the diaphragm (pneumoperitoneum).

Q383: What is the definitive treatment for perforated peptic ulcer?

A: Urgent surgical repair (often with an omental/Graham patch), IV fluids, antibiotics, acid suppression, and treatment of H. pylori if present.

Upper Gastrointestinal Bleeding

Q384: What is the most common cause of upper GI bleeding?

A: Peptic ulcer disease.

Q385: What is the first step in managing acute upper GI bleeding?

A: Assess airway if needed, stabilize with IV access and fluid/blood resuscitation, then perform risk assessment.

Q386: What is the diagnostic and therapeutic procedure of choice for upper GI bleeding?

A: Upper endoscopy (EGD).

Q387: Which endoscopic therapies are commonly used for bleeding peptic ulcers?

A: Combination therapy such as epinephrine injection plus thermal coagulation and/or endoscopic clips.

Q388: When is surgery considered for peptic ulcer bleeding?

A: Persistent or recurrent bleeding despite appropriate endoscopic and interventional radiology therapy, or when those options are unavailable or unsuitable.

Gastric Outlet Obstruction

Q389: What are common causes of gastric outlet obstruction?

A: Chronic peptic ulcer disease (less common today) and malignancy (e.g., gastric or pancreatic cancer).

Q390: What are the classic symptoms of gastric outlet obstruction?

A: Early satiety, postprandial vomiting of undigested food, abdominal distention, and weight loss.

Q391: What electrolyte abnormality is classically associated with prolonged gastric outlet obstruction?

A: Hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis.

Q392: What is the initial management of gastric outlet obstruction?

A: NPO, nasogastric decompression, IV fluids, electrolyte correction, and evaluation of the underlying cause.

Gastric Cancer

Q393: What is the most common histologic type of gastric cancer?

A: Adenocarcinoma.

Q394: Which bacterium is an important risk factor for gastric adenocarcinoma?

A: Helicobacter pylori.

Q395: What is the preferred initial diagnostic test for suspected gastric cancer?

A: Upper endoscopy with biopsy.

Q396: Which imaging modality is commonly used for staging gastric cancer?

A: Contrast-enhanced CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis (often with endoscopic ultrasound for local staging when appropriate).

Bariatric Surgery

Q397: Which bariatric procedure is considered the most commonly performed worldwide?

A: Sleeve gastrectomy.

Q398: Which bariatric procedure provides substantial weight loss and metabolic benefit but carries a risk of nutritional deficiencies?

A: Roux-en-Y gastric bypass.

Q399: What is a common long-term nutritional deficiency after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass?

A: Iron deficiency, vitamin B₁₂ deficiency, folate deficiency, calcium deficiency, and vitamin D deficiency.

Q400: What is the most important principle in managing upper gastrointestinal surgical disease on the USMLE Step 2 CK?

A: Stabilize the patient first, recognize surgical emergencies (perforation, obstruction, ischemia, uncontrolled bleeding), and choose the next best diagnostic or therapeutic step based on hemodynamic stability.

Q401: What is the most common cause of small bowel obstruction (SBO) in developed countries?

A: Postoperative intra-abdominal adhesions.

Q402: What are the next most common causes of SBO after adhesions?

A: Hernias and malignancy.

Q405: What bowel sound finding is more typical of late SBO with bowel ischemia or perforation?

A: Hypoactive or absent bowel sounds.

Q406: What is the preferred initial imaging study for suspected SBO?

A: CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast (when not contraindicated).

Q407: What are classic imaging findings of SBO on CT?

A: Dilated proximal small bowel, collapsed distal bowel, and a transition point.

Q408: What small bowel diameter suggests obstruction?

A: Greater than 3 cm.

Q409: What is the initial management of uncomplicated SBO?

A: NPO, IV fluids, electrolyte correction, and nasogastric tube decompression if significant vomiting or distention is present.

Q410: Which patients with SBO require urgent surgery?

A: Patients with bowel ischemia, perforation, peritonitis, strangulation, closed-loop obstruction, or failure of nonoperative management.

Q411: Which CT findings suggest bowel ischemia?

A: Reduced bowel wall enhancement, pneumatosis intestinalis, portal venous gas, mesenteric edema, or free intraperitoneal air (with perforation).

Q412: What laboratory finding raises concern for bowel ischemia?

A: Elevated serum lactate.

Ileus

Q413: What is ileus?

A: Functional paralysis of the intestine without a mechanical obstruction.

Q414: What is the most common cause of postoperative ileus?

A: Recent abdominal surgery.

Q415: Which medications commonly contribute to ileus?

A: Opioids and anticholinergic medications.

Q416: How does ileus differ from SBO on imaging?

A: Ileus shows diffuse dilation of both the small bowel and colon without a discrete transition point.

Q417: What is the treatment of postoperative ileus?

A: Supportive care with early ambulation, minimizing opioids, correcting electrolyte abnormalities, IV fluids, and NG decompression if needed.

Volvulus

Q418: What is volvulus?

A: Twisting of the bowel around its mesentery, causing obstruction and possible ischemia.

Q419: Which is the most common site of volvulus in adults?

A: Sigmoid colon.

Q420: Which patients are at highest risk for sigmoid volvulus?

A: Elderly, institutionalized patients and those with chronic constipation or neuropsychiatric disease.

Q421: What is the classic abdominal x-ray finding in sigmoid volvulus?

A: Coffee-bean sign.

Q422: What is the initial treatment for uncomplicated sigmoid volvulus?

A: Endoscopic detorsion (flexible sigmoidoscopy) with rectal tube placement.

Q423: When is surgery indicated for sigmoid volvulus?

A: Peritonitis, bowel ischemia, perforation, or failed endoscopic decompression.

Q424: Which volvulus commonly affects younger patients?

A: Cecal volvulus.

Q425: What is the treatment of cecal volvulus?

A: Surgical resection or fixation; endoscopic decompression is generally ineffective.

Intussusception

Q426: What is intussusception?

A: Telescoping of one segment of bowel into another.

Q427: What is the classic triad of pediatric intussusception?

A: Intermittent colicky abdominal pain, currant jelly stools, and a palpable sausage-shaped abdominal mass.

Q428: What is the most common cause of intussusception in children?

A: Idiopathic, often associated with hypertrophied Peyer patches after a viral illness.

Q429: What is the preferred diagnostic study in children with suspected intussusception?

A: Abdominal ultrasound showing the "target" or "doughnut" sign.

Q430: What is the preferred initial treatment for stable pediatric intussusception?

A: Air (or contrast) enema reduction.

Q431: When is surgery indicated for intussusception?

A: Perforation, peritonitis, shock, failed enema reduction, or suspicion of a pathologic lead point.

Meckel Diverticulum

Q432: What is Meckel diverticulum?

A: A true congenital diverticulum resulting from persistence of the vitelline (omphalomesenteric) duct.

Q433: What is the "Rule of 2s" for Meckel diverticulum?

A: Occurs in ~2% of the population, ~2 inches long, within ~2 feet of the ileocecal valve, often presents before age 2, and may contain two types of ectopic tissue (gastric and pancreatic).

Q434: What is the most common presentation of Meckel diverticulum in children?

A: Painless lower GI bleeding.

Q435: Which nuclear medicine study diagnoses Meckel diverticulum?

A: Technetium-99m pertechnetate scan (Meckel scan).

Q436: What is the definitive treatment for symptomatic Meckel diverticulum?

A: Surgical resection.

Acute Appendicitis

Q437: What is the most common cause of acute appendicitis?

A: Obstruction of the appendiceal lumen (commonly by a fecalith or lymphoid hyperplasia).

Q438: What is the classic progression of pain in appendicitis?

A: Periumbilical pain migrating to the right lower quadrant.

Q439: Why does appendicitis initially cause periumbilical pain?

A: Visceral afferent pain from the midgut is referred to the T10 dermatome.

Q440: What causes the pain to localize to the right lower quadrant?

A: Inflammation of the parietal peritoneum.

Q441: What is McBurney point?

A: A point located approximately one-third of the distance from the anterior superior iliac spine to the umbilicus, corresponding to the typical location of maximal tenderness.

Q442: What is Rovsing sign?

A: Right lower quadrant pain elicited by palpation of the left lower quadrant.

Q443: What is the psoas sign?

A: Right lower quadrant pain with passive extension of the right hip, suggesting irritation of the psoas muscle.

Q444: What is the obturator sign?

A: Right lower quadrant pain with internal rotation of the flexed right hip, suggesting a pelvic appendix.

Q445: What is the preferred imaging study for suspected appendicitis in nonpregnant adults?

A: CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast.

Q446: What is the preferred initial imaging study for children and pregnant patients with suspected appendicitis?

A: Ultrasound (MRI if ultrasound is nondiagnostic in pregnancy).

Q447: What is the definitive treatment for uncomplicated appendicitis?

A: Laparoscopic appendectomy is standard; selected uncomplicated cases may be managed initially with antibiotics in carefully chosen patients.

Q448: What antibiotics should be administered before appendectomy?

A: Broad-spectrum antibiotics covering gram-negative organisms and anaerobes.

Q449: What complication should be suspected if fever persists several days after appendectomy?

A: Intra-abdominal abscess.

Q450: What is the single highest-yield USMLE principle for appendicitis?

A: Migratory periumbilical-to-right lower quadrant pain with localized tenderness strongly suggests appendicitis; prompt diagnosis and treatment reduce the risk of perforation.

Q451: What is diverticulosis?

A: The presence of acquired mucosal and submucosal outpouchings (false diverticula) through the colonic muscular wall, most commonly in the sigmoid colon.

Q452: What is the most common risk factor for diverticulosis?

A: Increasing age.

Q453: What is the most common presentation of diverticulosis?

A: Asymptomatic; it is often discovered incidentally during colonoscopy or imaging.

Q454: What is the most common cause of significant lower gastrointestinal bleeding in adults?

A: Diverticular bleeding.

Q455: How does diverticular bleeding typically present?

A: Sudden, painless hematochezia.

Q456: What is diverticulitis?

A: Inflammation and microperforation of a colonic diverticulum.

Q457: What is the classic presentation of acute diverticulitis?

A: Left lower quadrant abdominal pain, fever, and leukocytosis.

Q458: Which patients are more likely to have right-sided diverticulitis?

A: Patients of Asian ancestry, although left-sided disease remains most common overall.

Q459: What is the preferred imaging study for suspected diverticulitis?

A: CT abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast.

Q460: What CT findings suggest acute diverticulitis?

A: Colonic wall thickening, pericolic fat stranding, and inflamed diverticula; abscess or perforation may also be seen in complicated disease.

Q461: Should colonoscopy be performed during acute diverticulitis?

A: No. It is avoided during the acute episode because of perforation risk and is typically performed after recovery if indicated.

Q462: How is uncomplicated diverticulitis treated?

A: Selected patients may be managed as outpatients with supportive care; antibiotics are used selectively based on severity, comorbidities, and current guidelines.

Q463: Which patients with diverticulitis require hospitalization?

A: Patients with sepsis, peritonitis, inability to tolerate oral intake, significant comorbidities, immunosuppression, or complicated disease.

Q464: What is the treatment for diverticular abscess >3 cm?

A: Percutaneous drainage plus antibiotics when feasible.

Q465: What is the treatment for perforated diverticulitis with generalized peritonitis?

A: Emergency surgical intervention.

Lower Gastrointestinal Bleeding

Q466: What is the first priority in acute lower GI bleeding?

A: Assess hemodynamic stability and begin resuscitation with IV access, fluids, and blood products as needed.

Q467: What is the preferred initial diagnostic test for hemodynamically stable patients with significant lower GI bleeding?

A: Colonoscopy after appropriate bowel preparation.

Q468: What is the preferred evaluation for ongoing brisk lower GI bleeding in an unstable patient?

A: CT angiography is commonly the initial imaging study if the patient can undergo scanning; catheter angiography allows diagnosis and embolization when appropriate.

Q469: What intervention can be performed during catheter angiography for active GI bleeding?

A: Transcatheter arterial embolization.

Q470: Which upper GI condition can present with hematochezia?

A: Massive upper GI bleeding.

Colorectal Cancer

Q471: What is the most common histologic type of colorectal cancer?

A: Adenocarcinoma.

Q472: What are the most important risk factors for colorectal cancer?

A: Increasing age, adenomatous polyps, hereditary syndromes (e.g., Lynch syndrome, FAP), inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, smoking, and diets high in processed/red meat.

Q473: What symptoms suggest left-sided colon cancer?

A: Change in bowel habits, pencil-thin stools, constipation, and obstructive symptoms.

Q474: What symptoms suggest right-sided colon cancer?

A: Occult bleeding, iron deficiency anemia, fatigue, and weight loss.

Q475: Which laboratory abnormality commonly occurs with right-sided colon cancer?

A: Iron deficiency anemia.

Q476: What is the preferred diagnostic test for suspected colorectal cancer?

A: Colonoscopy with biopsy.

Q477: Which imaging study is used for staging colorectal cancer?

A: Contrast-enhanced CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis.

Q478: Which tumor marker is commonly used for postoperative surveillance rather than screening?

A: Carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA).

Q479: What is the definitive treatment for localized colon cancer?

A: Surgical resection with regional lymph node evaluation.

Q480: Which syndrome should be suspected in a young patient with colorectal cancer and endometrial cancer in the family?

A: Lynch syndrome (hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer).

Mesenteric Ischemia

Q481: What is acute mesenteric ischemia?

A: Sudden interruption of intestinal blood flow causing bowel ischemia.

Q482: What is the classic presentation of acute mesenteric ischemia?

A: Severe abdominal pain out of proportion to physical examination findings.

Q483: Which patients are at highest risk for embolic mesenteric ischemia?

A: Patients with atrial fibrillation or other cardiac embolic sources.

Q484: Which laboratory abnormality commonly develops in mesenteric ischemia?

A: Elevated serum lactate due to tissue hypoperfusion.

Q485: What is the preferred diagnostic test in a hemodynamically stable patient with suspected acute mesenteric ischemia?

A: CT angiography.

Q486: What is the initial management of acute mesenteric ischemia?

A: Aggressive IV fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, bowel rest, anticoagulation when appropriate (e.g., arterial thrombosis or embolism without contraindication), and urgent vascular/surgical consultation.

Q487: Which patients require emergency laparotomy for mesenteric ischemia?

A: Patients with peritonitis, bowel perforation, bowel necrosis, or clinical deterioration.

Colonic Emergencies

Q488: What is toxic megacolon?

A: Acute, severe colonic dilation with systemic toxicity, usually complicating inflammatory bowel disease or infectious colitis (especially Clostridioides difficile).

Q489: Which imaging finding is characteristic of toxic megacolon?

A: Marked colonic dilation (commonly transverse colon >6 cm) on abdominal imaging.

Q490: What is the initial treatment of toxic megacolon?

A: Bowel rest, IV fluids, correction of electrolytes, treatment of the underlying cause, close monitoring, and urgent surgical consultation.

Q491: When is surgery indicated for toxic megacolon?

A: Perforation, uncontrolled bleeding, worsening sepsis, or failure of medical therapy.

Q492: What is acute colonic pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie syndrome)?

A: Massive colonic dilation without a mechanical obstruction.

Q493: Which hospitalized patients are at greatest risk for Ogilvie syndrome?

A: Elderly, severely ill, postoperative, trauma, neurologic, or immobilized patients.

Q494: What is the initial treatment of uncomplicated Ogilvie syndrome?

A: Supportive care, correction of reversible causes, bowel rest, and discontinuation of contributing medications.

Q495: Which medication is used when conservative therapy fails in Ogilvie syndrome without contraindications?

A: Neostigmine.

Q496: When is colonoscopic decompression considered in Ogilvie syndrome?

A: If neostigmine is contraindicated or unsuccessful and there is no perforation.

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (Surgical Pearls)

Q497: Which inflammatory bowel disease is cured by total proctocolectomy?

A: Ulcerative colitis.

Q498: Is surgery curative for Crohn disease?

A: No. Surgery treats complications but disease recurrence is common.

Q499: What is the most common indication for surgery in Crohn disease?

A: Complications such as obstruction from fibrostenotic disease, fistulas, abscesses, perforation, or medically refractory disease.

Q500: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for colorectal surgical emergencies?

A: Differentiate mechanical obstruction, ischemia, perforation, and inflammatory disease early. Hemodynamic instability, peritonitis, bowel ischemia, or perforation generally require urgent surgical evaluation and intervention.

For FlashCards USMLE 2
Section: Surgery 
Topic: Hepatobiliary Surgery (Gallbladder, Bile Duct & Liver)

Q501: What are the major types of gallstones?

A: Cholesterol stones (most common in developed countries), black pigment stones (associated with chronic hemolysis/cirrhosis), and brown pigment stones (associated with biliary infection).

Q502: What are the classic risk factors for cholesterol gallstones?

A: Female sex, increasing age, obesity, pregnancy, rapid weight loss, diabetes, and estrogen exposure.

Q503: What is biliary colic?

A: Episodic right upper quadrant (RUQ) or epigastric pain caused by transient cystic duct obstruction, often after a fatty meal, without gallbladder inflammation.

Q504: How does biliary colic differ from acute cholecystitis?

A: Biliary colic is transient, typically lasts <6 hours, and lacks fever or significant inflammatory markers; acute cholecystitis involves persistent pain, inflammation, and often fever/leukocytosis.

Q505: What is the preferred initial imaging study for suspected gallstones?

A: Right upper quadrant ultrasound.

Q506: What ultrasound findings indicate cholelithiasis?

A: Echogenic stones with posterior acoustic shadowing and mobility.

Q507: Should asymptomatic gallstones routinely be treated with cholecystectomy?

A: No. Most asymptomatic gallstones are managed expectantly, with selected exceptions (e.g., certain high-risk conditions).

Acute Cholecystitis

Q508: What causes acute calculous cholecystitis?

A: Persistent obstruction of the cystic duct by a gallstone leading to gallbladder inflammation.

Q509: What is the classic presentation of acute cholecystitis?

A: Persistent RUQ pain, fever, nausea/vomiting, leukocytosis, and Murphy sign.

Q510: What is Murphy sign?

A: Inspiratory arrest due to pain when palpating the RUQ beneath the costal margin.

Q511: What is the preferred initial imaging study for acute cholecystitis?

A: Right upper quadrant ultrasound.

Q512: Which ultrasound findings support acute cholecystitis?

A: Gallstones, gallbladder wall thickening (>3 mm), pericholecystic fluid, and a sonographic Murphy sign.

Q513: Which imaging study is most sensitive when ultrasound is equivocal?

A: Hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid (HIDA) scan.

Q514: What HIDA scan finding confirms acute cholecystitis?

A: Nonvisualization of the gallbladder due to cystic duct obstruction.

Q515: What is the initial management of acute cholecystitis?

A: NPO, IV fluids, analgesia, and antibiotics when indicated (especially moderate/severe disease), followed by surgical evaluation.

Q516: What is the definitive treatment for acute calculous cholecystitis?

A: Early laparoscopic cholecystectomy during the index hospitalization for most suitable patients.

Q517: Which patients may require percutaneous cholecystostomy instead of immediate surgery?

A: Critically ill or poor surgical candidates with acute cholecystitis.

Acalculous Cholecystitis

Q518: What is acalculous cholecystitis?

A: Gallbladder inflammation without gallstones.

Q519: Which patients are at greatest risk for acalculous cholecystitis?

A: Critically ill patients, those with sepsis, trauma, burns, prolonged fasting, or total parenteral nutrition.

Q520: Why is acalculous cholecystitis associated with higher mortality?

A: Diagnosis is often delayed, increasing the risk of gangrene and perforation.

Choledocholithiasis

Q521: What is choledocholithiasis?

A: Presence of gallstones in the common bile duct (CBD).

Q522: What laboratory abnormalities suggest choledocholithiasis?

A: Elevated bilirubin and cholestatic liver enzymes (especially alkaline phosphatase and γ-glutamyl transferase), often with elevated AST/ALT.

Q523: Which imaging finding suggests common bile duct obstruction?

A: Dilated common bile duct on ultrasound.

Q524: Which imaging study is commonly used to evaluate suspected choledocholithiasis in stable patients?

A: Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) or endoscopic ultrasound (EUS), depending on the probability of CBD stones.

Q525: What is the preferred therapeutic procedure for confirmed choledocholithiasis?

A: Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with stone extraction, followed by cholecystectomy when appropriate.

Acute Cholangitis

Q526: What causes ascending cholangitis?

A: Biliary obstruction with superimposed bacterial infection.

Q527: What is Charcot triad?

A: Fever, jaundice, and right upper quadrant pain.

Q528: What is Reynolds pentad?

A: Charcot triad plus hypotension and altered mental status.

Q529: What is the initial management of acute cholangitis?

A: IV fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and urgent biliary decompression when indicated.

Q530: What is the preferred method of biliary decompression in most patients with acute cholangitis?

A: ERCP.

Gallstone Pancreatitis

Q531: What causes gallstone pancreatitis?

A: Transient obstruction of the ampulla of Vater by a gallstone.

Q532: Which laboratory finding supports gallstone pancreatitis?

A: Elevated serum lipase (or amylase) with elevated ALT suggesting a biliary source.

Q533: When is urgent ERCP indicated in gallstone pancreatitis?

A: If there is concomitant acute cholangitis or persistent biliary obstruction.

Q534: When should cholecystectomy be performed after mild gallstone pancreatitis?

A: During the same hospitalization after the pancreatitis has clinically improved.

Gallbladder Polyps & Cancer

Q535: Which gallbladder polyp size is generally considered an indication for cholecystectomy due to increased malignancy risk?

A: Polyps ≥10 mm, or smaller polyps with high-risk features.

Q536: What is the most common histologic type of gallbladder cancer?

A: Adenocarcinoma.

Q537: What is the strongest risk factor for gallbladder carcinoma?

A: Chronic gallstones (cholelithiasis).

Q538: What imaging modality is typically used first to evaluate suspected gallbladder carcinoma?

A: Right upper quadrant ultrasound, followed by cross-sectional imaging for staging.

Liver Lesions

Q539: What is the most common benign liver tumor?

A: Cavernous hemangioma.

Q540: Which benign liver tumor is associated with oral contraceptive use and carries a risk of hemorrhage?

A: Hepatocellular adenoma.

Q541: Which benign liver lesion characteristically contains a central stellate scar?

A: Focal nodular hyperplasia (FNH).

Liver Abscess

Q542: What is the classic triad of pyogenic liver abscess?

A: Fever, right upper quadrant pain, and leukocytosis (jaundice is less common).

Q543: What is the preferred imaging study for suspected liver abscess?

A: Contrast-enhanced CT scan (ultrasound is also useful).

Q544: What is the treatment for most pyogenic liver abscesses?

A: Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics plus percutaneous drainage for drainable collections.

Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC)

Q545: Which patients are at greatest risk for hepatocellular carcinoma?

A: Patients with cirrhosis (from hepatitis B, hepatitis C, alcohol-associated liver disease, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, etc.).

Q546: Which serum tumor marker is commonly associated with HCC?

A: Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), although it is not sufficiently sensitive or specific for diagnosis alone.

Q547: What imaging feature is characteristic of HCC on multiphasic CT or MRI?

A: Arterial phase hyperenhancement with portal venous or delayed washout.

Portal Hypertension

Q548: What is the most common cause of portal hypertension?

A: Cirrhosis.

Q549: What are the major complications of portal hypertension?

A: Esophageal/gastric varices, ascites, splenomegaly, hepatic encephalopathy, and hypersplenism.

Q550: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle in hepatobiliary surgery?

A: Use RUQ ultrasound as the first test for suspected gallbladder disease, recognize cholangitis as a medical emergency requiring antibiotics and biliary drainage, and distinguish biliary colic from acute cholecystitis by the presence of persistent pain and inflammation.

Q551: What are the two most common causes of acute pancreatitis?

A: Gallstones and alcohol use.

Q552: What are other important causes of acute pancreatitis?

A: Hypertriglyceridemia, hypercalcemia, ERCP, medications (e.g., azathioprine, valproate, thiazides in rare cases), trauma, infections, and pancreatic tumors.

Q553: What is the classic presentation of acute pancreatitis?

A: Sudden severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, associated with nausea and vomiting.

Q554: Which serum enzyme is most specific for acute pancreatitis?

A: Serum lipase.

Q555: What diagnostic criteria are used for acute pancreatitis?

A: Diagnosis requires 2 of 3:

Characteristic abdominal pain
Lipase or amylase ≥3× the upper limit of normal
Imaging findings consistent with pancreatitis.

Q556: Is CT required to diagnose acute pancreatitis?

A: No. CT is reserved for diagnostic uncertainty, severe disease, or suspected complications.

Q557: What imaging study should be obtained in most patients with acute pancreatitis to evaluate the cause?

A: Right upper quadrant ultrasound to assess for gallstones.

Q558: What is the cornerstone of initial treatment for acute pancreatitis?

A: Aggressive IV fluid resuscitation, adequate analgesia, and early enteral nutrition when tolerated.

Q559: Which IV fluid is generally preferred for initial resuscitation in acute pancreatitis?

A: Lactated Ringer's solution.

Q560: Are prophylactic antibiotics recommended in uncomplicated acute pancreatitis?

A: No.

Severity Assessment

Q561: Which scoring system is commonly used to predict the severity of acute pancreatitis?

A: BISAP score (also APACHE II and revised Atlanta classification are used).

Q562: What is the most common cause of death during the first week of severe acute pancreatitis?

A: Systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) leading to multiorgan failure.

Q563: What is the most common cause of death after the first week of severe pancreatitis?

A: Infected pancreatic necrosis and its complications.

Q564: What laboratory abnormality is associated with severe pancreatitis?

A: Elevated hematocrit (hemoconcentration), elevated BUN, and elevated CRP may indicate more severe disease.

Pancreatic Necrosis

Q565: When should pancreatic necrosis be suspected?

A: Persistent fever, leukocytosis, organ failure, or clinical deterioration despite supportive treatment.

Q566: Which imaging study best evaluates pancreatic necrosis?

A: Contrast-enhanced CT scan.

Q567: When should CT generally be performed to evaluate suspected necrosis?

A: Usually after 72 hours from symptom onset if the diagnosis is uncertain or complications are suspected.

Q568: When are antibiotics indicated in pancreatic necrosis?

A: Only for infected pancreatic necrosis or another documented infection—not for sterile necrosis.

Q569: What is the preferred treatment for infected pancreatic necrosis?

A: A step-up approach: antibiotics plus image-guided drainage, with minimally invasive or surgical necrosectomy if needed.

Pancreatic Pseudocyst

Q570: What is a pancreatic pseudocyst?

A: A mature, encapsulated collection of pancreatic fluid surrounded by a fibrous wall without an epithelial lining, typically developing ≥4 weeks after pancreatitis.

Q571: Which imaging modality is most useful for diagnosing a pancreatic pseudocyst?

A: Contrast-enhanced CT scan.

Q572: Which pancreatic pseudocysts require intervention?

A: Symptomatic, infected, enlarging, bleeding, obstructing, or persistent pseudocysts with clinically significant complications.

Q573: What is the preferred treatment for symptomatic pancreatic pseudocysts?

A: Endoscopic drainage when anatomically appropriate; percutaneous or surgical drainage in selected cases.

Chronic Pancreatitis

Q574: What is the most common cause of chronic pancreatitis in adults?

A: Chronic alcohol use (although other causes such as genetic disorders and obstructive disease also occur).

Q575: What are the classic symptoms of chronic pancreatitis?

A: Chronic epigastric pain, exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (steatorrhea), weight loss, and diabetes mellitus.

Q576: Which imaging finding is characteristic of chronic pancreatitis?

A: Pancreatic calcifications.

Q577: Why do patients with chronic pancreatitis develop steatorrhea?

A: Loss of pancreatic exocrine enzyme production causes fat malabsorption.

Q578: How is pancreatic exocrine insufficiency treated?

A: Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy.

Pancreatic Cancer

Q579: What is the most common type of pancreatic cancer?

A: Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma.

Q580: What is the strongest modifiable risk factor for pancreatic adenocarcinoma?

A: Cigarette smoking.

Q581: What is the classic presentation of pancreatic head cancer?

A: Painless obstructive jaundice with weight loss.

Q582: What is Courvoisier sign?

A: A palpable, nontender enlarged gallbladder in a jaundiced patient, suggesting malignant biliary obstruction.

Q583: What is the preferred initial imaging study for suspected pancreatic cancer?

A: Pancreatic protocol contrast-enhanced CT scan.

Q584: Which tumor marker is commonly elevated in pancreatic adenocarcinoma?

A: CA 19-9 (used mainly for prognosis and monitoring, not screening).

Q585: What operation is performed for resectable pancreatic head cancer?

A: Pancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple procedure).

Q586: Which structures are typically removed during a Whipple procedure?

A: Pancreatic head, duodenum, distal common bile duct, gallbladder, and usually the gastric antrum or pylorus (depending on the technique).

Pancreatic Neuroendocrine Tumors

Q587: Which pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor causes recurrent fasting hypoglycemia?

A: Insulinoma.

Q588: Which pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor causes Zollinger-Ellison syndrome?

A: Gastrinoma.

Q589: What laboratory finding supports the diagnosis of gastrinoma?

A: Markedly elevated fasting serum gastrin with gastric acid hypersecretion (after appropriate evaluation).

Splenic Disorders

Q590: What is the most common indication for splenectomy after trauma?

A: Hemodynamic instability due to splenic injury.

Q591: Which patients with splenic trauma can usually be managed nonoperatively?

A: Hemodynamically stable patients without peritonitis.

Q592: What is the most common complication after splenectomy?

A: Increased lifelong susceptibility to overwhelming infection with encapsulated bacteria.

Q593: Which organisms are classically associated with overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI)?

A: Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae type b, and Neisseria meningitidis.

Q594: Which vaccines should be administered before elective splenectomy when possible?

A: Pneumococcal, meningococcal, and Hib vaccines (ideally at least 2 weeks before surgery).

Q595: If an emergency splenectomy is performed, when should these vaccines generally be given?

A: As soon as the patient has recovered adequately, typically about 2 weeks after surgery, according to current guidelines.

Q596: Which blood cell abnormality commonly develops after splenectomy?

A: Reactive thrombocytosis.

Q597: Which peripheral blood smear finding is characteristic after splenectomy?

A: Howell-Jolly bodies.

Q598: Why do Howell-Jolly bodies appear after splenectomy?

A: The spleen normally removes nuclear remnants from erythrocytes; without splenic function, these persist.

Q599: Which patients should be educated to seek urgent medical care for fever after splenectomy?

A: All asplenic patients, because fever may indicate overwhelming post-splenectomy infection.

Q600: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle regarding pancreatic and splenic disease?

A: Treat acute pancreatitis with supportive care first, reserve antibiotics for infected necrosis (not sterile disease), recognize painless jaundice as pancreatic cancer until proven otherwise, and always protect asplenic patients against encapsulated organisms with appropriate vaccination.

Topic: Breast Surgery

Q601: Which lymph nodes receive most lymphatic drainage from the breast?

A: Axillary lymph nodes (especially the anterior/pectoral group).

Q602: Which quadrant of the breast is the most common site of breast cancer?

A: Upper outer quadrant.

Q603: Which ligament provides structural support to the breast?

A: Cooper (suspensory) ligaments.

Q604: Why does advanced breast cancer cause skin dimpling?

A: Tumor invasion and shortening of Cooper ligaments.

Breast Mass Evaluation

Q605: What are the three components of the "triple assessment" of a breast mass?

A: Clinical breast examination, breast imaging, and tissue diagnosis (biopsy when indicated).

Q606: What is the preferred initial imaging study for a palpable breast mass in women <30 years?

A: Breast ultrasound.

Q607: What is the preferred initial imaging study for a palpable breast mass in women ≥30 years?

A: Diagnostic mammography (usually with targeted ultrasound).

Q608: What is the preferred biopsy method for most suspicious breast lesions?

A: Core needle biopsy.

Q609: Why is core needle biopsy preferred over fine-needle aspiration for suspicious breast masses?

A: It preserves tissue architecture, allowing histologic diagnosis and receptor testing (ER, PR, HER2).

Fibroadenoma

Q610: What is the most common benign breast tumor in young women?

A: Fibroadenoma.

Q611: How does a fibroadenoma typically feel on examination?

A: Firm, rubbery, smooth, mobile, and well-circumscribed.

Q612: Which imaging finding is typical of fibroadenoma on ultrasound?

A: Oval, well-circumscribed, homogeneous hypoechoic mass with parallel orientation.

Q613: How are small, biopsy-proven fibroadenomas usually managed?

A: Observation with periodic clinical or imaging follow-up if asymptomatic.

Fibrocystic Breast Changes

Q614: What is the most common cause of cyclic breast pain in premenopausal women?

A: Fibrocystic breast changes.

Q615: How do fibrocystic breast changes typically present?

A: Bilateral breast pain, nodularity, and tenderness that fluctuate with the menstrual cycle.

Q616: Do fibrocystic breast changes increase the risk of breast cancer?

A: Simple nonproliferative fibrocystic changes do not significantly increase breast cancer risk.

Mastitis & Breast Abscess

Q617: Which organism most commonly causes lactational mastitis?

A: Staphylococcus aureus.

Q618: What is the typical presentation of lactational mastitis?

A: Localized painful erythematous breast with fever in a breastfeeding woman.

Q619: What is the first-line treatment for lactational mastitis?

A: Anti-staphylococcal antibiotics, continued breastfeeding or milk expression, and supportive care.

Q620: When should a breast abscess be suspected?

A: Persistent fluctuance or failure of mastitis to improve with appropriate antibiotics.

Q621: What is the preferred treatment for a breast abscess?

A: Ultrasound-guided needle aspiration or incision and drainage plus antibiotics.

Nipple Discharge

Q622: Which type of nipple discharge is most concerning for malignancy?

A: Spontaneous, unilateral, bloody, or serous discharge from a single duct.

Q623: What is the most common cause of bloody nipple discharge?

A: Intraductal papilloma.

Q624: What is an intraductal papilloma?

A: A benign papillary tumor arising within a lactiferous duct.

Fat Necrosis

Q625: What commonly causes fat necrosis of the breast?

A: Trauma, surgery, radiation, or previous breast procedures.

Q626: Why is fat necrosis clinically important?

A: It may mimic breast cancer on physical examination and imaging.

Mammography

Q627: Which mammographic finding is highly suspicious for malignancy?

A: Fine pleomorphic or linear branching microcalcifications, especially in clustered distributions.

Q628: What does a BI-RADS 1 result indicate?

A: Negative examination.

Q629: What does BI-RADS 2 indicate?

A: Benign finding.

Q630: What does BI-RADS 3 indicate?

A: Probably benign lesion (<2% risk of malignancy); short-interval imaging follow-up is recommended.

Q631: What does BI-RADS 4 indicate?

A: Suspicious abnormality requiring tissue diagnosis (biopsy).

Q632: What does BI-RADS 5 indicate?

A: Highly suggestive of malignancy (>95% likelihood); biopsy is indicated.

Breast Cancer

Q633: What is the most common histologic type of breast cancer?

A: Invasive ductal carcinoma (no special type).

Q634: What is the second most common invasive breast cancer?

A: Invasive lobular carcinoma.

Q635: Which breast cancer is often bilateral and multifocal?

A: Invasive lobular carcinoma.

Q636: Which receptor status predicts response to endocrine therapy?

A: Estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) positivity.

Q637: Which receptor predicts response to trastuzumab?

A: HER2 overexpression or amplification.

Q638: Which subtype generally has the poorest prognosis and lacks targeted endocrine or HER2 therapy?

A: Triple-negative breast cancer (ER−, PR−, HER2−).

Clinical Signs

Q639: What is peau d'orange?

A: Edematous skin with an orange-peel appearance caused by lymphatic obstruction from breast cancer.

Q640: Which breast cancer presents with diffuse erythema, edema, warmth, and peau d'orange?

A: Inflammatory breast cancer.

Q641: Does inflammatory breast cancer usually present as a discrete palpable mass?

A: No. It often presents with diffuse skin changes rather than a distinct mass.

Paget Disease

Q642: What is Paget disease of the breast?

A: Malignant involvement of the nipple-areolar complex, usually associated with underlying ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) or invasive carcinoma.

Q643: How does Paget disease typically present?

A: Chronic unilateral eczematous, scaly, or ulcerative nipple changes that do not resolve with topical therapy.

Surgical Management

Q644: What is the preferred axillary staging procedure for clinically node-negative invasive breast cancer?

A: Sentinel lymph node biopsy.

Q645: When is axillary lymph node dissection generally performed?

A: In selected patients with proven axillary nodal involvement or based on surgical and oncologic indications.

Q646: What is the major complication of axillary lymph node dissection?

A: Lymphedema of the ipsilateral upper extremity.

Genetics

Q647: Which genes are most commonly associated with hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome?

A: BRCA1 and BRCA2.

Q648: Which patients should be referred for genetic risk assessment and possible BRCA testing?

A: Patients with strong family histories, early-onset breast cancer, male breast cancer, ovarian cancer, multiple affected relatives, or other features suggesting hereditary cancer syndromes.

Treatment Principles

Q649: What is breast-conserving therapy?

A: Lumpectomy followed by whole-breast radiation therapy in appropriate candidates.

Q650: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for breast surgery?

A: Every suspicious breast mass requires triple assessment (clinical examination, appropriate imaging, and tissue diagnosis). Core needle biopsy is the standard diagnostic method, and treatment is guided by stage and receptor status (ER, PR, and HER2).

Q656: What is the preferred biopsy method for suspicious thyroid nodules?

A: Ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (FNA).

Thyroid Cancer

Q657: What is the most common type of thyroid cancer?

A: Papillary thyroid carcinoma.

Q658: Which thyroid cancer has the best prognosis?

A: Papillary thyroid carcinoma.

Q659: Which environmental exposure is a major risk factor for papillary thyroid carcinoma?

A: Childhood exposure to ionizing radiation.

Q660: Which histologic feature is characteristic of papillary thyroid carcinoma?

A: Orphan Annie eye nuclei (optically clear nuclei), often with nuclear grooves and psammoma bodies.

Q661: Which thyroid cancer spreads primarily via lymphatics?

A: Papillary thyroid carcinoma.

Q662: Which thyroid cancer spreads primarily via the bloodstream?

A: Follicular thyroid carcinoma.

Q663: Which thyroid cancer arises from parafollicular (C) cells?

A: Medullary thyroid carcinoma.

Q664: Which tumor marker is elevated in medullary thyroid carcinoma?

A: Calcitonin (CEA may also be elevated).

Q665: Which hereditary syndrome is associated with medullary thyroid carcinoma?

A: Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia type 2 (MEN2).

Q666: Which thyroid cancer is the most aggressive?

A: Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma.

Hyperthyroidism

Q667: What is the most common cause of hyperthyroidism?

A: Graves disease.

Q668: Which antibody is responsible for Graves disease?

A: Thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulin (TSI), also called TSH receptor antibody.

Q669: What are the classic clinical findings of Graves disease?

A: Diffuse goiter, hyperthyroidism, ophthalmopathy, and pretibial myxedema.

Q670: What is the first-line medication for symptomatic hyperthyroidism?

A: Beta-blockers for symptomatic control (along with antithyroid therapy when indicated).

Q671: Which antithyroid drugs are commonly used?

A: Methimazole and propylthiouracil (PTU).

Q672: Which antithyroid drug is preferred during the first trimester of pregnancy?

A: Propylthiouracil (PTU).

Q673: Which antithyroid drug is generally preferred outside the first trimester?

A: Methimazole.

Thyroid Storm

Q674: What is thyroid storm?

A: A life-threatening exacerbation of severe thyrotoxicosis causing multisystem dysfunction.

Q675: What are common clinical features of thyroid storm?

A: High fever, severe tachycardia, agitation, delirium, vomiting, diarrhea, and heart failure.

Q676: What is the correct treatment sequence for thyroid storm?

A: Beta-blocker → PTU (or methimazole) → iodine (after antithyroid drug) → glucocorticoids, along with aggressive supportive care.

Q677: Why should iodine be administered only after antithyroid medication?

A: To prevent new thyroid hormone synthesis before blocking hormone release.

Hypothyroidism

Q678: What is the treatment for primary hypothyroidism?

A: Levothyroxine.

Q679: What is myxedema coma?

A: Severe decompensated hypothyroidism causing altered mental status, hypothermia, hypotension, and hypoventilation.

Q680: How is myxedema coma treated?

A: IV levothyroxine, stress-dose glucocorticoids until adrenal insufficiency is excluded, supportive care, and treatment of precipitating factors.

Hyperparathyroidism

Q681: What is the most common cause of primary hyperparathyroidism?

A: Solitary parathyroid adenoma.

Q682: What are the classic manifestations of hyperparathyroidism?

A: "Bones, stones, abdominal groans, psychic overtones."

Q683: What laboratory pattern is typical of primary hyperparathyroidism?

A: ↑PTH, ↑calcium, ↓phosphate.

Q684: What is the definitive treatment for symptomatic primary hyperparathyroidism?

A: Parathyroidectomy.

Hypoparathyroidism

Q685: What is the most common cause of hypoparathyroidism?

A: Accidental injury or removal during thyroid surgery.

Q686: What laboratory abnormalities occur in hypoparathyroidism?

A: ↓PTH, ↓calcium, ↑phosphate.

Q687: What physical examination signs indicate hypocalcemia?

A: Chvostek sign and Trousseau sign.

MEN Syndromes

Q688: What tumors are associated with MEN1?

A: Parathyroid tumors, Pituitary tumors, and Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors ("3 Ps").

Q689: What tumors are associated with MEN2A?

A: Medullary thyroid carcinoma, pheochromocytoma, and primary hyperparathyroidism.

Q690: What tumors are associated with MEN2B?

A: Medullary thyroid carcinoma, pheochromocytoma, mucosal neuromas, marfanoid habitus, and intestinal ganglioneuromatosis.

Pheochromocytoma

Q691: What is pheochromocytoma?

A: A catecholamine-secreting tumor arising from adrenal medullary chromaffin cells.

Q692: What is the classic triad of pheochromocytoma?

A: Episodic headache, diaphoresis, and palpitations.

Q693: What is the preferred biochemical test for pheochromocytoma?

A: Plasma free metanephrines (or 24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines).

Q694: Which imaging study is commonly used to localize pheochromocytoma after biochemical confirmation?

A: CT or MRI of the adrenal glands.

Q695: What medication must always be started before beta-blockers in pheochromocytoma?

A: Alpha-blockade (e.g., phenoxybenzamine or selective α₁-blockers).

Q696: Why must alpha-blockade precede beta-blockade?

A: Beta-blockade alone can cause unopposed alpha-adrenergic stimulation, leading to severe hypertension.

Primary Hyperaldosteronism (Conn Syndrome)

Q697: What laboratory abnormalities suggest primary hyperaldosteronism?

A: Hypertension, hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, low plasma renin, and elevated aldosterone.

Q698: What is the definitive treatment for unilateral aldosterone-producing adenoma?

A: Laparoscopic adrenalectomy.

Adrenal Incidentaloma

Q699: What is the first step after discovering an adrenal incidentaloma?

A: Evaluate for hormonal activity (e.g., cortisol, catecholamines, aldosterone when indicated) and assess imaging features suggesting malignancy.

Q700: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle in endocrine surgery?

A: Always determine whether an endocrine lesion is functioning before surgery. For pheochromocytoma, alpha-blockade must always precede beta-blockade. Evaluate thyroid nodules with TSH followed by ultrasound, and biopsy appropriate nodules with ultrasound-guided FNA.

Topic: Vascular Surgery (Arterial Disease, Venous Disease & Aortic Disorders)

Q701: What is the most common cause of peripheral arterial disease (PAD)?

A: Atherosclerosis.

Q702: What is the strongest modifiable risk factor for PAD?

A: Cigarette smoking.

Q703: What is the classic symptom of PAD?

A: Intermittent claudication (reproducible muscle pain with exertion that resolves with rest).

Q704: Which muscle group is affected in femoropopliteal PAD?

A: Calf muscles.

Q705: Which muscle group is affected in aortoiliac occlusive disease?

A: Buttocks and thighs.

Q706: What is Leriche syndrome?

A: Aortoiliac occlusive disease causing buttock claudication, erectile dysfunction, and diminished femoral pulses.

Q707: What physical examination findings suggest PAD?

A: Diminished or absent peripheral pulses, cool extremity, delayed capillary refill, trophic skin changes, and hair loss.

Q708: What is the best initial diagnostic test for suspected PAD?

A: Ankle-brachial index (ABI).

Q709: How is the ankle-brachial index (ABI) calculated?

A: ABI = Highest ankle systolic pressure ÷ Highest brachial systolic pressure

Q710: What ABI value is considered normal?

A: 1.00–1.40

Q711: What ABI suggests peripheral arterial disease?

A: <0.90

Q712: What ABI suggests critical limb ischemia?

A: Approximately ≤0.40.

Q713: What is the first-line medical treatment for symptomatic PAD?

A: Smoking cessation, supervised exercise therapy, statin therapy, and antiplatelet therapy.

Q714: Which antiplatelet medications are commonly used in PAD?

A: Aspirin or clopidogrel.

Q715: Which medication can improve walking distance in patients with lifestyle-limiting claudication who do not have heart failure?

A: Cilostazol.

Critical Limb Ischemia (Chronic Limb-Threatening Ischemia)

Q716: What defines chronic limb-threatening ischemia?

A: Ischemic rest pain, nonhealing ulcers, or gangrene due to advanced PAD.

Q717: What is the definitive treatment for chronic limb-threatening ischemia when feasible?

A: Revascularization (endovascular or surgical bypass).

Acute Limb Ischemia

Q718: What is acute limb ischemia?

A: Sudden reduction in arterial blood flow threatening limb viability.

Q719: What are the classic "6 Ps" of acute limb ischemia?

A: Pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, and poikilothermia.

Q720: What is the most common cause of acute limb ischemia?

A: Arterial embolism (commonly from atrial fibrillation) or acute thrombosis of preexisting PAD.

Q721: What is the first treatment after recognizing acute limb ischemia (unless contraindicated)?

A: Immediate IV unfractionated heparin.

Q722: What is the definitive treatment for a threatened limb in acute limb ischemia?

A: Urgent revascularization (catheter-directed therapy or surgery, depending on the clinical scenario).

Q723: Which finding indicates irreversible limb ischemia?

A: Profound anesthesia, paralysis with fixed rigor, and nonviable tissue.

Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA)

Q724: What is an abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA)?

A: Permanent dilation of the abdominal aorta to ≥3.0 cm.

Q725: What is the most common location of an AAA?

A: Infrarenal abdominal aorta.

Q726: What are the major risk factors for AAA?

A: Older age, male sex, smoking, atherosclerosis, and family history.

Q727: What is the preferred screening test for AAA?

A: Abdominal ultrasound.

Q728: Which population is routinely recommended for one-time AAA screening in many guidelines?

A: Men aged 65–75 years who have ever smoked.

Q729: What symptom strongly suggests rupture of an AAA?

A: Sudden abdominal or back pain with hypotension.

Q730: What classic triad is associated with ruptured AAA?

A: Hypotension, abdominal/back pain, and a pulsatile abdominal mass (not always present).

Q731: What is the definitive treatment for ruptured AAA?

A: Emergency surgical or endovascular repair.

Q732: At approximately what aneurysm diameter is elective repair generally recommended in men?

A: ≥5.5 cm, rapid expansion, or symptomatic aneurysm.

Thoracic Aortic Disease

Q733: Which connective tissue disorder is strongly associated with thoracic aortic aneurysm?

A: Marfan syndrome.

Q734: What imaging modality is commonly used to evaluate thoracic aortic aneurysm?

A: CT angiography (or MR angiography in selected patients).

Aortic Dissection

Q735: What is the most common risk factor for aortic dissection?

A: Chronic hypertension.

Q736: What is the classic symptom of acute aortic dissection?

A: Sudden severe tearing chest or back pain.

Q737: What imaging study is preferred for diagnosing aortic dissection in a hemodynamically stable patient?

A: CT angiography.

Q738: Which medication should be administered first in acute aortic dissection?

A: IV beta-blocker (e.g., esmolol) to reduce heart rate and shear stress.

Q739: Which Stanford type of aortic dissection requires emergency surgery?

A: Stanford Type A (ascending aorta involved).

Q740: How is uncomplicated Stanford Type B dissection generally managed initially?

A: Aggressive medical therapy with blood pressure and heart rate control; intervention is reserved for complications.

Carotid Artery Disease

Q741: What is the major complication of carotid artery stenosis?

A: Ischemic stroke.

Q742: Which imaging test is used first to evaluate carotid stenosis?

A: Carotid duplex ultrasound.

Q743: Which patients benefit most from carotid endarterectomy?

A: Selected patients with symptomatic high-grade carotid stenosis.

Venous Disease

Q744: What is the most common cause of chronic venous insufficiency?

A: Venous valvular incompetence, often following prior DVT or due to primary valve failure.

Q745: What are typical clinical features of chronic venous insufficiency?

A: Leg edema, varicose veins, hyperpigmentation, lipodermatosclerosis, and venous ulcers.

Q746: Where do venous stasis ulcers most commonly occur?

A: Near the medial malleolus.

Q747: What is the first-line treatment for chronic venous insufficiency?

A: Compression therapy (if there is no significant arterial insufficiency).

Varicose Veins

Q748: What causes varicose veins?

A: Superficial venous valve incompetence leading to venous reflux.

Q749: What is the preferred diagnostic test for symptomatic varicose veins?

A: Duplex venous ultrasound.

Q750: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for vascular surgery?

A: Differentiate acute from chronic vascular disease. Acute limb ischemia requires immediate anticoagulation and urgent revascularization. Suspected ruptured AAA or Stanford Type A aortic dissection requires emergent surgical management. ABI is the initial test for PAD, and compression therapy is first-line for chronic venous insufficiency after excluding significant arterial disease.

Q751: What is the most common cause of deep vein thrombosis (DVT)?

A: Venous stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability (Virchow triad).

Q752: What are the components of Virchow triad?

A: Venous stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability.

Q753: What are the most important risk factors for DVT?

A: Recent surgery, trauma, malignancy, prolonged immobility, pregnancy/postpartum, estrogen therapy, obesity, prior DVT, inherited thrombophilias, and increasing age.

Q754: What are the classic symptoms of DVT?

A: Unilateral leg swelling, pain, warmth, and erythema.

Q755: Is Homan sign reliable for diagnosing DVT?

A: No. It has poor sensitivity and specificity and is not recommended for diagnosis.

Q756: What clinical prediction rule is commonly used to estimate the probability of DVT?

A: Wells score for DVT.

Q757: In a patient with low clinical probability of DVT, what is the appropriate initial test?

A: High-sensitivity D-dimer (if negative, DVT is effectively excluded in most patients).

Q758: What is the preferred diagnostic test for suspected lower-extremity DVT?

A: Compression duplex ultrasonography.

Q759: What is the first-line treatment for proximal DVT in a hemodynamically stable patient?

A: Anticoagulation (e.g., a DOAC in appropriate patients, or LMWH followed by another anticoagulant depending on the clinical scenario).

Q760: When is catheter-directed thrombolysis considered for DVT?

A: Selected patients with extensive iliofemoral DVT, severe symptoms, low bleeding risk, and good functional status.

Pulmonary Embolism (PE)

Q761: What is the most common source of pulmonary emboli?

A: Proximal deep veins of the lower extremities.

Q762: What are the classic symptoms of pulmonary embolism?

A: Sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, tachypnea, and tachycardia.

Q763: What ECG finding is classically associated with pulmonary embolism?

A: Sinus tachycardia is most common; the S₁Q₃T₃ pattern is classic but uncommon.

Q764: What is the preferred imaging study for diagnosing PE in a hemodynamically stable patient?

A: CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA).

Q765: Which imaging test is preferred when CTPA is contraindicated (e.g., severe contrast allergy or selected patients)?

A: Ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) scan, when appropriate.

Q766: What is the initial treatment for hemodynamically stable pulmonary embolism?

A: Anticoagulation.

Q767: Which patients with PE should receive systemic thrombolytic therapy?

A: Patients with high-risk (massive) PE causing hemodynamic instability, if there are no major contraindications.

Q768: Which patients with PE may require surgical embolectomy or catheter-based intervention?

A: Selected patients with massive PE who cannot receive thrombolysis or who fail thrombolytic therapy.

Inferior Vena Cava (IVC) Filters

Q769: What is the primary indication for placement of an IVC filter?

A: Acute VTE with an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation.

Q770: Should retrievable IVC filters be removed?

A: Yes. They should generally be removed once anticoagulation can be safely initiated and the indication has resolved.

Superficial Thrombophlebitis

Q771: What is superficial thrombophlebitis?

A: Thrombosis and inflammation of a superficial vein.

Q772: How is uncomplicated superficial thrombophlebitis treated?

A: NSAIDs, warm compresses, ambulation, and compression therapy when appropriate; anticoagulation is considered for extensive or high-risk disease.

Dialysis Access

Q773: What is the preferred long-term vascular access for hemodialysis?

A: Native arteriovenous (AV) fistula.

Q774: Why are AV fistulas preferred over AV grafts?

A: They have lower infection and thrombosis rates and better long-term patency.

Q775: Which AV fistula is most commonly created first?

A: Radiocephalic (Brescia-Cimino) fistula at the wrist, when feasible.

Q776: What physical examination finding confirms a functioning AV fistula?

A: A palpable thrill and an audible bruit.

Vascular Graft Complications

Q777: What is the most common early complication of a vascular graft?

A: Graft thrombosis.

Q778: What is the most serious complication of a prosthetic vascular graft?

A: Graft infection.

Organ Donation

Q779: What is brain death?

A: Irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem.

Q780: Is brain death legally recognized as death?

A: Yes.

Q781: Can organs be donated after brain death?

A: Yes, with appropriate consent and according to applicable laws and protocols.

Kidney Transplantation

Q782: What is the treatment of choice for eligible patients with end-stage kidney disease?

A: Kidney transplantation.

Q783: Which donor generally provides the best long-term graft survival?

A: Living donor.

Q784: What vascular anastomosis is commonly performed during kidney transplantation?

A: Donor renal vessels are anastomosed to the recipient's external iliac vessels.

Immunosuppressive Therapy

Q785: Which calcineurin inhibitors are commonly used after organ transplantation?

A: Tacrolimus and cyclosporine.

Q786: What is a major adverse effect of calcineurin inhibitors?

A: Nephrotoxicity.

Q787: Which antiproliferative agents are commonly used in transplant immunosuppression?

A: Mycophenolate mofetil and azathioprine.

Q788: Which mTOR inhibitors are used in transplant recipients?

A: Sirolimus and everolimus.

Q789: What is the role of corticosteroids after transplantation?

A: Prevention and treatment of acute rejection as part of immunosuppressive regimens.

Transplant Rejection

Q790: When does hyperacute rejection occur?

A: Minutes to hours after transplantation.

Q791: What causes hyperacute rejection?

A: Preformed recipient antibodies against donor antigens.

Q792: How is hyperacute rejection treated?

A: It is generally not reversible; graft removal is usually required.

Q793: When does acute rejection usually occur?

A: Days to months after transplantation (and can occur later).

Q794: What causes acute rejection?

A: Primarily T-cell-mediated immune responses, with or without antibody-mediated mechanisms.

Q795: How is acute rejection treated?

A: Increased immunosuppression (e.g., high-dose corticosteroids; additional therapies based on rejection type).

Q796: When does chronic rejection occur?

A: Months to years after transplantation.

Q797: Is chronic rejection generally reversible?

A: No. It usually progresses despite treatment.

Post-Transplant Complications

Q798: What is the leading cause of death after successful organ transplantation?

A: Cardiovascular disease overall; infection is a major cause early after transplantation due to immunosuppression.

Q799: Which malignancies occur more frequently after transplantation?

A: Skin cancers, post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (often EBV-associated), and several other malignancies.

Q800: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for vascular and transplant surgery?

A: Use compression ultrasound to diagnose DVT, CT pulmonary angiography for stable PE, anticoagulate unless contraindicated, reserve IVC filters for patients who cannot receive anticoagulation, and recognize hyperacute rejection as antibody-mediated and generally irreversible.

Q801: How is microscopic hematuria defined?

A: ≥3 red blood cells (RBCs) per high-power field (HPF) on microscopic examination of a properly collected urine specimen.

Q802: What is the first step in evaluating hematuria?

A: Confirm hematuria with urinalysis, assess for infection or glomerular disease, and obtain a focused history and physical examination.

Q803: Which painless presentation should be considered bladder cancer until proven otherwise?

A: Painless gross hematuria in an adult.

Q804: What are common nonglomerular causes of hematuria?

A: Urolithiasis, urinary tract infection, bladder cancer, renal cell carcinoma, trauma, benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), and anticoagulation (which does not exclude underlying pathology).

Q805: Which urinalysis findings suggest a glomerular source of hematuria?

A: Dysmorphic RBCs, RBC casts, and significant proteinuria.

Q806: What is the preferred evaluation for visible (gross) hematuria in adults at risk for urinary tract malignancy?

A: Cystoscopy plus upper urinary tract imaging (typically CT urography if appropriate).

Q807: Which imaging study is preferred for evaluation of the upper urinary tract in many patients with hematuria?

A: CT urography.

Q808: What is the gold standard for evaluating the bladder in patients with suspected bladder cancer?

A: Cystoscopy.

Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)

Q809: What is the most common cause of lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) in older men?

A: Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH).

Q810: Which symptoms are characteristic of BPH?

A: Hesitancy, weak stream, intermittency, incomplete emptying, urinary frequency, urgency, nocturia, and postvoid dribbling.

Q811: Which symptom is usually the earliest complaint in BPH?

A: Nocturia.

Q812: Which examination should be performed in all men with suspected BPH?

A: Digital rectal examination (DRE).

Q813: How does the prostate typically feel on DRE in BPH?

A: Smooth, enlarged, rubbery, and symmetric.

Q814: Which DRE finding raises concern for prostate cancer rather than BPH?

A: Hard, irregular, asymmetric nodules or induration.

Q815: Which symptom score is commonly used to assess BPH severity?

A: International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS).

Q816: What is the first-line medication for symptomatic BPH?

A: An α₁-adrenergic blocker (e.g., tamsulosin) for rapid symptom relief.

Q817: Which medications shrink the prostate over time?

A: 5-α-reductase inhibitors (finasteride or dutasteride).

Q818: How do 5-α-reductase inhibitors work?

A: They inhibit conversion of testosterone to dihydrotestosterone (DHT).

Q819: Which medication class improves BPH symptoms most rapidly?

A: α₁-blockers.

Q820: Which patients benefit most from adding a 5-α-reductase inhibitor?

A: Men with enlarged prostates and moderate to severe symptoms.

Acute Urinary Retention

Q821: What is the most common cause of acute urinary retention in older men?

A: Benign prostatic hyperplasia.

Q822: What is the immediate management of acute urinary retention?

A: Prompt bladder decompression with a urethral Foley catheter (or suprapubic catheter if urethral catheterization is contraindicated or unsuccessful).

Q823: Which medications can precipitate urinary retention?

A: Anticholinergics, sympathomimetics, opioids, and some antihistamines.

Q824: What is post-obstructive diuresis?

A: Marked polyuria after relief of urinary obstruction that may require careful monitoring of volume status and electrolytes.

Prostate Cancer

Q825: What is the most common noncutaneous cancer in men?

A: Prostate adenocarcinoma.

Q826: What are the major risk factors for prostate cancer?

A: Increasing age, family history, African ancestry, and certain inherited genetic mutations (e.g., BRCA2).

Q827: Which laboratory test is commonly used with DRE to evaluate for prostate cancer?

A: Prostate-specific antigen (PSA).

Q828: Is an elevated PSA diagnostic of prostate cancer?

A: No. PSA can also be elevated in BPH, prostatitis, urinary retention, and after certain urologic procedures.

Q829: What is the definitive diagnostic test for prostate cancer?

A: Prostate biopsy (usually image-guided after appropriate evaluation).

Q830: Where does prostate cancer most commonly arise?

A: Peripheral zone of the prostate.

Q831: Where does BPH most commonly occur?

A: Transitional zone.

Q832: What is the most common site of distant metastasis from prostate cancer?

A: Bone, particularly the axial skeleton.

Q833: What type of bone lesions are classically seen in metastatic prostate cancer?

A: Osteoblastic (sclerotic) lesions.

Renal Cell Carcinoma (RCC)

Q834: What is the most common type of kidney cancer in adults?

A: Renal cell carcinoma (clear cell subtype is most common).

Q835: What are the major risk factors for RCC?

A: Smoking, obesity, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and certain hereditary syndromes (e.g., von Hippel-Lindau).

Q836: What is the classic triad of RCC?

A: Hematuria, flank pain, and a palpable flank mass.

Q837: Is the classic triad of RCC common at presentation?

A: No. Most patients present before all three findings develop.

Q838: Which paraneoplastic syndromes are associated with RCC?

A: Polycythemia (↑EPO), hypercalcemia, hypertension, hepatic dysfunction (Stauffer syndrome), and others.

Q839: Which imaging study is preferred for evaluating a suspected renal mass?

A: Contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen (renal mass protocol when appropriate).

Q840: What is the definitive treatment for localized RCC?

A: Partial nephrectomy when feasible, or radical nephrectomy depending on tumor characteristics.

Renal Trauma

Q841: Which imaging study is preferred for evaluating renal trauma in a hemodynamically stable patient?

A: Contrast-enhanced CT abdomen and pelvis.

Q842: Which patients with renal trauma generally require surgery?

A: Hemodynamically unstable patients or those with certain severe injuries not amenable to nonoperative management.

Obstructive Uropathy

Q843: What is hydronephrosis?

A: Dilation of the renal collecting system due to urinary tract obstruction.

Q844: Which imaging study is commonly used first to detect hydronephrosis?

A: Renal ultrasound.

Q845: What is the definitive treatment of obstructive uropathy?

A: Relief of the obstruction (e.g., catheterization, ureteral stent, or percutaneous nephrostomy depending on the cause and level of obstruction).

Postrenal Acute Kidney Injury

Q846: What is the most common postrenal cause of acute kidney injury in older men?

A: Benign prostatic hyperplasia causing bladder outlet obstruction.

Q847: What bedside study rapidly detects urinary retention?

A: Bladder ultrasound (bladder scan).

Surgical Pearls

Q848: Which urologic emergency requires immediate bladder decompression?

A: Acute urinary retention.

Q849: Which adult patient with hematuria requires urgent evaluation for malignancy?

A: Any adult with painless gross hematuria, particularly those with smoking history or other urothelial cancer risk factors.

Q850: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for common urologic surgical disease?

A: Painless gross hematuria is bladder cancer until proven otherwise. Treat acute urinary retention with immediate catheterization, evaluate BPH with DRE and symptom assessment, and recognize that RCC often presents with incidental imaging findings rather than the classic triad.

Q851: What is the most common type of kidney stone?

A: Calcium oxalate stone.

Q852: What are the four major types of kidney stones?

A: Calcium oxalate/calcium phosphate, uric acid, struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate), and cystine.

Q853: What is the classic presentation of nephrolithiasis?

A: Sudden, severe colicky flank pain radiating to the groin with hematuria, nausea, and vomiting.

Q854: Which imaging study is the preferred initial test for suspected nephrolithiasis in most nonpregnant adults?

A: Noncontrast CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis (CT KUB).

Q855: Which imaging modality is preferred in pregnancy for suspected nephrolithiasis?

A: Renal and bladder ultrasound.

Q856: Which laboratory test should be obtained in all patients with suspected nephrolithiasis?

A: Urinalysis (with urine culture if infection is suspected).

Q857: Which stone is radiolucent on plain abdominal radiographs?

A: Uric acid stone.

Q858: Which stone is classically associated with urease-producing bacteria?

A: Struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate) stone.

Q859: Which organisms commonly produce struvite stones?

A: Proteus, Klebsiella, and some other urease-producing organisms.

Q860: Which stone forms staghorn calculi?

A: Struvite stone.

Q861: Which inherited disorder is associated with cystine stones?

A: Cystinuria.

Q862: What is the first-line treatment for a stable patient with a small ureteral stone (<10 mm)?

A: Hydration, analgesia (NSAIDs when appropriate), and medical expulsive therapy in selected distal ureteral stones (e.g., tamsulosin).

Q863: Which medication may facilitate passage of distal ureteral stones?

A: Tamsulosin.

Q864: Which patients with nephrolithiasis require urgent urologic decompression?

A: Patients with obstructing stones plus infection (infected obstructed system), sepsis, solitary kidney with obstruction, bilateral obstruction, or worsening renal failure.

Q865: What are the methods of urgent urinary decompression?

A: Ureteral stent placement or percutaneous nephrostomy.

Testicular Torsion

Q866: What causes testicular torsion?

A: Twisting of the spermatic cord, causing acute interruption of testicular blood flow.

Q867: What is the classic presentation of testicular torsion?

A: Sudden severe unilateral scrotal pain with nausea and vomiting.

Q868: Which physical examination finding strongly suggests testicular torsion?

A: High-riding, horizontally oriented testis.

Q869: Which reflex is typically absent in testicular torsion?

A: Cremasteric reflex.

Q870: What is the definitive treatment for testicular torsion?

A: Immediate surgical exploration with detorsion and bilateral orchiopexy.

Q871: Should surgery be delayed for imaging when torsion is strongly suspected?

A: No. Immediate surgery is indicated.

Q872: What imaging study is used if the diagnosis is uncertain and torsion is less likely?

A: Color Doppler scrotal ultrasound.

Q873: Why is rapid treatment of testicular torsion essential?

A: Testicular salvage rates decrease rapidly as ischemia duration increases.

Epididymitis

Q874: What is the most common cause of epididymitis in sexually active men <35 years?

A: Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae.

Q875: What is the most common cause of epididymitis in older men?

A: Enteric gram-negative bacteria (e.g., Escherichia coli), often associated with urinary tract pathology.

Q876: How does epididymitis differ clinically from testicular torsion?

A: Epididymitis usually has gradual onset, preserved cremasteric reflex, epididymal tenderness, and often urinary symptoms.

Q877: What is the treatment for epididymitis?

A: Appropriate antibiotics based on likely pathogens, scrotal elevation, NSAIDs, and supportive care.

Hydrocele & Varicocele

Q878: What is a hydrocele?

A: Collection of serous fluid within the tunica vaginalis surrounding the testicle.

Q879: How does a hydrocele appear on transillumination?

A: It transilluminates.

Q880: What is a varicocele?

A: Dilatation of the pampiniform venous plexus.

Q881: On which side do varicoceles occur most commonly?

A: Left side.

Q882: What physical examination finding is characteristic of a varicocele?

A: "Bag of worms" appearance or feel.

Q883: When should an isolated right-sided varicocele raise concern?

A: It may suggest retroperitoneal pathology (e.g., renal or inferior vena cava obstruction) and warrants further evaluation.

Fournier Gangrene

Q884: What is Fournier gangrene?

A: Necrotizing fasciitis of the perineum and external genitalia.

Q885: Which patients are at greatest risk for Fournier gangrene?

A: Patients with diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, alcohol use disorder, obesity, or chronic kidney disease.

Q886: What is the treatment for Fournier gangrene?

A: Immediate surgical debridement, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics, aggressive resuscitation, and repeated debridement as needed.

Priapism

Q887: What is ischemic (low-flow) priapism?

A: Painful penile erection lasting >4 hours due to impaired venous outflow; it is a urologic emergency.

Q888: What is the initial treatment for ischemic priapism?

A: Aspiration of corporal blood with intracavernosal phenylephrine.

Q889: Which condition is classically associated with ischemic priapism?

A: Sickle cell disease.

Paraphimosis

Q890: What is paraphimosis?

A: Entrapment of a retracted foreskin behind the glans penis, causing venous congestion and possible ischemia.

Q891: What is the treatment for paraphimosis?

A: Immediate manual reduction after edema reduction; dorsal slit or circumcision if reduction fails.

Bladder Cancer

Q892: What is the most common histologic type of bladder cancer in the United States?

A: Urothelial (transitional cell) carcinoma.

Q893: What is the strongest risk factor for bladder cancer?

A: Cigarette smoking.

Q894: What is the most common presentation of bladder cancer?

A: Painless gross hematuria.

Q895: What is the definitive diagnostic procedure for bladder cancer?

A: Cystoscopy with transurethral resection/biopsy (TURBT).

Urethral Injury

Q896: Which pelvic injury is commonly associated with posterior urethral injury?

A: Pelvic fracture.

Q897: What clinical finding suggests urethral injury?

A: Blood at the urethral meatus (often with inability to void and/or a high-riding prostate on DRE in posterior injury, though DRE is not highly sensitive).

Q898: Which imaging study should be performed before urethral catheterization if urethral injury is suspected?

A: Retrograde urethrogram (RUG).

Urologic Trauma

Q899: What is the most common injury to the urinary bladder?

A: Extraperitoneal bladder rupture, usually associated with pelvic fracture.

Q900: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for urologic surgery?

A: Never miss testicular torsion—operate immediately if clinically suspected. Painless gross hematuria suggests bladder cancer until proven otherwise. An obstructed infected kidney requires urgent decompression, and suspected urethral injury requires a retrograde urethrogram before catheterization.

Topic: Thoracic Surgery

Q901: What is a pneumothorax?

A: Air within the pleural space causing partial or complete lung collapse.

Q902: What is a primary spontaneous pneumothorax?

A: A pneumothorax occurring without underlying lung disease, typically due to rupture of apical subpleural blebs in tall, thin young adults.

Q903: What is a secondary spontaneous pneumothorax?

A: A pneumothorax occurring in patients with underlying lung disease (e.g., COPD, cystic fibrosis, interstitial lung disease).

Q904: What is the most common symptom of spontaneous pneumothorax?

A: Sudden onset pleuritic chest pain with dyspnea.

Q905: What physical examination findings suggest pneumothorax?

A: Decreased breath sounds, hyperresonance to percussion, and reduced chest expansion on the affected side.

Q906: What is the initial imaging study for a stable patient with suspected pneumothorax?

A: Upright chest radiograph (chest X-ray).

Q907: What is the most life-threatening type of pneumothorax?

A: Tension pneumothorax.

Q908: What causes tension pneumothorax?

A: A one-way valve mechanism allowing air into but not out of the pleural space, increasing intrathoracic pressure.

Q909: What are the classic clinical features of tension pneumothorax?

A: Severe respiratory distress, hypotension, tachycardia, distended neck veins, unilateral absent breath sounds, and tracheal deviation away from the affected side (a late sign).

Q910: Should imaging be obtained before treating suspected tension pneumothorax?

A: No. It is a clinical diagnosis requiring immediate treatment.

Q911: What is the immediate treatment for tension pneumothorax?

A: Immediate needle decompression (or finger thoracostomy where appropriate) followed by tube thoracostomy.

Q912: Where is needle decompression commonly performed?

A: Second intercostal space, midclavicular line, or fourth/fifth intercostal space at the anterior to mid-axillary line (based on current trauma practice and equipment).

Q913: What is the definitive treatment for tension pneumothorax?

A: Tube thoracostomy (chest tube placement).

Chest Tubes

Q914: What is the preferred location for chest tube insertion?

A: The "safe triangle" (approximately the 4th–5th intercostal space, anterior to the mid-axillary line).

Q915: Why is the superior border of the rib used for chest tube insertion?

A: To avoid the intercostal neurovascular bundle located along the inferior border of the rib.

Q916: What are the major indications for chest tube placement?

A: Pneumothorax, hemothorax, empyema, and selected pleural effusions.

Q917: When can a chest tube usually be removed?

A: After lung re-expansion, resolution of the air leak, and minimal appropriate drainage.

Hemothorax

Q918: What is a hemothorax?

A: Blood within the pleural space.

Q919: What is the most common cause of traumatic hemothorax?

A: Blunt or penetrating chest trauma.

Q920: What is the initial treatment of traumatic hemothorax?

A: Tube thoracostomy.

Q921: Which chest tube output generally indicates the need for urgent thoracic surgical evaluation?

A: Initial drainage >1,500 mL or persistent bleeding >200 mL/hour for 2–4 hours (clinical judgment required).

Massive Hemothorax

Q922: What are the major clinical consequences of massive hemothorax?

A: Hypovolemic shock and respiratory compromise.

Q923: What is the definitive treatment for ongoing massive hemothorax?

A: Surgical exploration (typically thoracotomy) when indicated.

Rib Fractures

Q924: What is the most common complication of rib fractures in older adults?

A: Pneumonia due to inadequate ventilation from pain.

Q925: What is the cornerstone of rib fracture management?

A: Adequate multimodal pain control and pulmonary hygiene (e.g., incentive spirometry).

Q926: Why should rib fracture pain be treated aggressively?

A: To prevent atelectasis and pneumonia by allowing effective deep breathing and coughing.

Flail Chest

Q927: What is flail chest?

A: Fractures of ≥3 consecutive ribs, each fractured in 2 or more places, creating a free-floating chest wall segment.

Q928: What physical examination finding characterizes flail chest?

A: Paradoxical chest wall movement during respiration.

Q929: What injury commonly accompanies flail chest?

A: Pulmonary contusion.

Q930: What is the main treatment for flail chest?

A: Adequate analgesia, respiratory support, pulmonary hygiene, and mechanical ventilation if respiratory failure develops.

Pulmonary Contusion

Q931: What is a pulmonary contusion?

A: Bruising of the lung parenchyma causing alveolar hemorrhage and edema.

Q932: What imaging study best identifies pulmonary contusion?

A: CT chest (though chest X-ray findings may evolve over time).

Q933: What is the primary treatment for pulmonary contusion?

A: Supportive care with oxygen, pain control, and ventilatory support as needed while avoiding unnecessary fluid overload.

Pleural Effusion

Q934: What is a pleural effusion?

A: Accumulation of fluid within the pleural space.

Q935: What are the two major categories of pleural effusion?

A: Transudative and exudative.

Q936: Which criteria are used to differentiate transudative from exudative pleural effusions?

A: Light's criteria.

Q937: What is the most common cause of transudative pleural effusion?

A: Congestive heart failure.

Q938: What are common causes of exudative pleural effusions?

A: Pneumonia, malignancy, pulmonary embolism, tuberculosis, pancreatitis, and inflammatory diseases.

Empyema

Q939: What is an empyema?

A: Collection of pus within the pleural space.

Q940: What is the most common cause of empyema?

A: Complicated bacterial pneumonia.

Q941: What is the treatment for empyema?

A: Antibiotics plus pleural drainage (tube thoracostomy; surgery may be required if loculated or persistent).

Thoracic Trauma

Q942: Which imaging study is typically obtained first in a stable trauma patient with chest injury?

A: Portable chest X-ray (with CT used for further evaluation when indicated).

Q943: Which injury should be suspected after high-speed deceleration trauma with a widened mediastinum on chest X-ray?

A: Traumatic aortic injury.

Q944: What is the preferred imaging study for suspected blunt thoracic aortic injury in a stable patient?

A: CT angiography of the chest.

Q945: What is Beck triad?

A: Hypotension, jugular venous distension, and muffled heart sounds.

Q946: Which life-threatening condition is associated with Beck triad?

A: Cardiac tamponade.

Q947: What is the initial bedside imaging study for suspected cardiac tamponade in trauma?

A: Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST) or focused cardiac ultrasound.

Q948: What is the emergency treatment for traumatic cardiac tamponade?

A: Immediate operative management in unstable trauma patients (pericardiocentesis may be a temporizing measure in selected settings).

Q949: Which thoracic injuries are immediately life-threatening and must be recognized during the ATLS primary survey?

A: Tension pneumothorax, open pneumothorax, massive hemothorax, cardiac tamponade, flail chest with pulmonary contusion, and major airway injury.

Q950: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for thoracic trauma?

A: Never delay treatment of tension pneumothorax for imaging. Insert chest tubes through the safe triangle, treat rib fractures with aggressive pain control to prevent pulmonary complications, and recognize massive hemothorax and cardiac tamponade as surgical emergencies.

Q951: What is an esophageal perforation?

A: A full-thickness tear of the esophageal wall causing leakage of esophageal contents into the mediastinum.

Q952: What is the most common cause of esophageal perforation?

A: Iatrogenic injury during endoscopy.

Q953: What is Boerhaave syndrome?

A: Spontaneous transmural esophageal rupture caused by forceful vomiting.

Q954: What is the classic presentation of Boerhaave syndrome?

A: Severe chest pain after repeated vomiting with subcutaneous emphysema and systemic toxicity.

Q955: What is Mackler triad?

A: Vomiting, chest pain, and subcutaneous emphysema.

Q956: What imaging study is commonly used first in suspected esophageal perforation?

A: CT chest with oral water-soluble contrast (or water-soluble contrast esophagram depending on the clinical situation).

Q957: Why should barium generally be avoided initially if esophageal perforation is strongly suspected?

A: Leakage of barium into the mediastinum can provoke significant inflammatory reactions.

Q958: What is the initial management of esophageal perforation?

A: NPO, IV fluids, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics, proton pump inhibitor, and urgent thoracic surgical consultation.

Q959: Which patients usually require operative or endoscopic intervention for esophageal perforation?

A: Patients with large leaks, sepsis, mediastinal contamination, or unstable clinical status.

Achalasia

Q960: What is achalasia?

A: Failure of lower esophageal sphincter (LES) relaxation with absent normal esophageal peristalsis.

Q961: What are the classic symptoms of achalasia?

A: Progressive dysphagia to both solids and liquids, regurgitation, and weight loss.

Q962: Which imaging finding is characteristic of achalasia on barium esophagram?

A: Bird-beak narrowing of the distal esophagus.

Q963: What is the gold standard diagnostic test for achalasia?

A: High-resolution esophageal manometry.

Q964: What is the definitive treatment for achalasia in appropriate surgical candidates?

A: Laparoscopic Heller myotomy with partial fundoplication or peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) in selected patients.

Esophageal Cancer

Q965: What are the two major histologic types of esophageal cancer?

A: Squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma.

Q966: Which risk factors are most strongly associated with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma?

A: Tobacco use, alcohol use, achalasia, caustic injury, and prior head and neck cancer.

Q967: Which condition is the strongest precursor for esophageal adenocarcinoma?

A: Barrett esophagus.

Q968: What is the most common symptom of esophageal cancer?

A: Progressive dysphagia, initially to solids and later to liquids.

Q969: What is the preferred diagnostic test for suspected esophageal cancer?

A: Upper endoscopy (esophagogastroduodenoscopy) with biopsy.

Q970: Which imaging modality is commonly used for staging esophageal cancer?

A: CT chest/abdomen with contrast and endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) for local staging; PET/CT may be used for metastatic evaluation.

Lung Cancer

Q971: What is the strongest risk factor for lung cancer?

A: Cigarette smoking.

Q972: What is the most common type of lung cancer?

A: Adenocarcinoma.

Q973: Which lung cancer is most commonly found in the peripheral lung?

A: Adenocarcinoma.

Q974: Which lung cancer is classically associated with central cavitary lesions and hypercalcemia?

A: Squamous cell carcinoma.

Q975: Which lung cancer is strongly associated with ectopic ACTH production and SIADH?

A: Small cell lung carcinoma.

Q976: Which lung cancer is usually treated primarily with chemotherapy and radiation rather than surgery?

A: Small cell lung carcinoma.

Q977: What is the preferred imaging study for evaluating a suspicious lung mass?

A: Contrast-enhanced CT scan of the chest.

Q978: What is the definitive diagnostic test for lung cancer?

A: Tissue biopsy (bronchoscopy, CT-guided biopsy, or surgical biopsy depending on lesion location).

Solitary Pulmonary Nodule

Q979: Which features increase the likelihood that a pulmonary nodule is malignant?

A: Larger size, irregular or spiculated borders, upper lobe location, interval growth, older age, and smoking history.

Q980: Which imaging modality helps assess metabolic activity of an indeterminate pulmonary nodule?

A: FDG-PET/CT.

Mediastinal Masses

Q981: What are the classic "4 Ts" of anterior mediastinal masses?

A: Thymoma, Teratoma (and other germ cell tumors), Thyroid mass, and "Terrible" lymphoma.

Q982: Which mediastinal tumor is strongly associated with myasthenia gravis?

A: Thymoma.

Thoracic Outlet Syndrome

Q983: What causes thoracic outlet syndrome?

A: Compression of the brachial plexus and/or subclavian vessels at the thoracic outlet.

Q984: What are common symptoms of neurogenic thoracic outlet syndrome?

A: Arm pain, paresthesias, weakness, and hand fatigue, especially with overhead activity.

Q985: What is the initial treatment for most patients with neurogenic thoracic outlet syndrome?

A: Physical therapy and postural rehabilitation.

Diaphragmatic Injury

Q986: Which mechanism commonly causes traumatic diaphragmatic rupture?

A: High-energy blunt trauma (especially motor vehicle collisions) or penetrating trauma.

Q987: Which side is more commonly affected in blunt diaphragmatic rupture?

A: Left side.

Q988: Which imaging study is preferred in a hemodynamically stable patient with suspected diaphragmatic injury?

A: CT scan of the chest and abdomen.

Q989: What is the definitive treatment for traumatic diaphragmatic rupture?

A: Surgical repair.

Video-Assisted Thoracoscopic Surgery (VATS)

Q990: What is VATS?

A: A minimally invasive thoracic surgical technique using small incisions and a thoracoscope.

Q991: What are common indications for VATS?

A: Lung biopsy, wedge resection, treatment of spontaneous pneumothorax, empyema management, and selected lung cancer resections.

Postoperative Thoracic Complications

Q992: What is the most common pulmonary complication after thoracic or upper abdominal surgery?

A: Atelectasis.

Q993: How can postoperative atelectasis be prevented?

A: Early ambulation, incentive spirometry, effective pain control, and pulmonary hygiene.

Lung Resection

Q994: What is the standard surgical treatment for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) in appropriate candidates?

A: Anatomic lobectomy with lymph node evaluation.

Q995: Which preoperative pulmonary function parameter is important before major lung resection?

A: Predicted postoperative pulmonary function (e.g., FEV₁ and DLCO).

Thoracic Surgical Emergencies

Q996: Which esophageal condition is a surgical emergency because of rapidly developing mediastinitis?

A: Boerhaave syndrome (spontaneous esophageal rupture).

Q997: Which thoracic malignancy is considered an oncologic emergency when causing facial swelling and venous congestion?

A: Superior vena cava (SVC) syndrome, often due to lung cancer (especially small cell) or lymphoma.

Q998: What is the initial management of suspected SVC syndrome?

A: Confirm the diagnosis with imaging and obtain tissue diagnosis when feasible; manage airway compromise urgently and treat the underlying malignancy.

High-Yield Review

Q999: What is the key difference between achalasia and esophageal cancer?

A: Achalasia typically causes dysphagia to both solids and liquids from the onset, whereas esophageal cancer usually causes progressive dysphagia beginning with solids.

Q1000: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for thoracic surgery?

A: Never miss Boerhaave syndrome after forceful vomiting. Progressive dysphagia requires endoscopy with biopsy to exclude malignancy. Remember the "4 Ts" of anterior mediastinal masses, and treat early-stage NSCLC with surgical resection when appropriate, while small cell lung cancer is usually managed primarily with systemic therapy.

Topic: Pediatric Surgery

Q1001: What is hypertrophic pyloric stenosis?

A: Hypertrophy of the pyloric muscle causing gastric outlet obstruction in young infants.

Q1002: At what age does hypertrophic pyloric stenosis most commonly present?

A: 2–8 weeks of age (classically 3–6 weeks).

Q1003: What is the classic presentation of hypertrophic pyloric stenosis?

A: Progressive nonbilious projectile vomiting after feeding in a hungry infant.

Q1004: Why is vomiting nonbilious in hypertrophic pyloric stenosis?

A: The obstruction is proximal to the duodenum, before bile enters the gastrointestinal tract.

Q1005: What classic abdominal examination finding is associated with hypertrophic pyloric stenosis?

A: A palpable "olive-like" mass in the right upper quadrant or epigastrium.

Q1006: Which visible physical examination finding may be seen after feeding?

A: Visible gastric peristaltic waves moving from left to right.

Q1007: What is the preferred diagnostic test for hypertrophic pyloric stenosis?

A: Abdominal ultrasound.

Q1008: What electrolyte abnormality is classically associated with prolonged pyloric stenosis?

A: Hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis.

Q1009: Why does metabolic alkalosis occur in hypertrophic pyloric stenosis?

A: Loss of gastric hydrochloric acid (HCl) from repeated vomiting.

Q1010: What is the first step in management of hypertrophic pyloric stenosis?

A: Correct dehydration and electrolyte abnormalities before surgery.

Q1011: What is the definitive treatment for hypertrophic pyloric stenosis?

A: Ramstedt pyloromyotomy.

Intussusception

Q1012: What is intussusception?

A: Telescoping of one segment of bowel into another, causing intestinal obstruction and possible ischemia.

Q1013: What is the most common age for intussusception?

A: 6–36 months, with a peak around 6–18 months.

Q1014: What is the classic triad of intussusception?

A: Intermittent colicky abdominal pain, vomiting, and currant jelly stool.

Q1015: Is the classic triad present in all patients with intussusception?

A: No. Many children do not present with all three findings.

Q1016: What is the characteristic physical examination finding?

A: Sausage-shaped abdominal mass.

Q1017: What is the preferred initial imaging study?

A: Abdominal ultrasound.

Q1018: What ultrasound finding is characteristic of intussusception?

A: Target sign (donut sign).

Q1019: What is both the diagnostic and therapeutic procedure for stable intussusception without perforation?

A: Air (or contrast) enema reduction.

Q1020: When is surgery indicated for intussusception?

A: Failed enema reduction, perforation, peritonitis, bowel ischemia, or hemodynamic instability.

Meckel Diverticulum

Q1021: What is Meckel diverticulum?

A: A congenital true diverticulum resulting from persistence of the vitelline (omphalomesenteric) duct.

Q1022: What is the "Rule of 2s" for Meckel diverticulum?

A: 2% of the population, ~2 inches long, within ~2 feet of the ileocecal valve, may contain 2 types of ectopic mucosa (gastric and pancreatic), often presents before age 2 (mnemonic only; not exact).

Q1023: What is the most common presentation of Meckel diverticulum in children?

A: Painless lower gastrointestinal bleeding.

Q1024: Why does Meckel diverticulum bleed?

A: Ectopic gastric mucosa secretes acid, causing adjacent ileal ulceration.

Q1025: What is the preferred diagnostic test?

A: Technetium-99m pertechnetate (Meckel) scan.

Q1026: What is the definitive treatment for symptomatic Meckel diverticulum?

A: Surgical resection.

Malrotation & Midgut Volvulus

Q1027: What causes intestinal malrotation?

A: Abnormal embryologic rotation and fixation of the midgut.

Q1028: What life-threatening complication can occur with malrotation?

A: Midgut volvulus.

Q1029: What is the hallmark symptom of midgut volvulus?

A: Bilious vomiting in a neonate.

Q1030: Why is bilious vomiting in a neonate considered a surgical emergency?

A: It suggests intestinal obstruction distal to the ampulla of Vater until proven otherwise.

Q1031: Which imaging study is the gold standard for diagnosing malrotation?

A: Upper gastrointestinal contrast series.

Q1032: What is the definitive treatment for malrotation with volvulus?

A: Emergent Ladd procedure.

Hirschsprung Disease

Q1033: What causes Hirschsprung disease?

A: Congenital absence of enteric ganglion cells in the distal bowel.

Q1034: Which portion of the colon is most commonly affected?

A: Rectosigmoid colon.

Q1035: What is the classic neonatal presentation?

A: Failure to pass meconium within the first 48 hours of life.

Q1036: What other symptoms are common in Hirschsprung disease?

A: Abdominal distension, bilious vomiting, chronic constipation, and poor feeding.

Q1037: Which imaging finding is characteristic on contrast enema?

A: Transition zone between dilated proximal bowel and narrow aganglionic distal bowel.

Q1038: What is the definitive diagnostic test?

A: Rectal suction biopsy demonstrating absence of ganglion cells.

Q1039: What is the definitive treatment?

A: Surgical pull-through procedure.

Necrotizing Enterocolitis (NEC)

Q1040: Which infants are at highest risk for necrotizing enterocolitis?

A: Premature, low-birth-weight infants.

Q1041: What are the classic symptoms of NEC?

A: Feeding intolerance, abdominal distension, bloody stools, lethargy, and temperature instability.

Q1042: What abdominal X-ray finding is pathognomonic for NEC?

A: Pneumatosis intestinalis.

Q1043: What additional radiographic finding suggests bowel perforation?

A: Free intraperitoneal air (pneumoperitoneum).

Q1044: What is the initial treatment for NEC?

A: NPO, nasogastric decompression, IV fluids, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics, and supportive care.

Q1045: When is surgery indicated in NEC?

A: Perforation, intestinal necrosis, clinical deterioration despite medical management, or fixed bowel loop concerning for necrosis.

Pediatric Surgical Principles

Q1046: What is the most important diagnosis to exclude in any neonate with bilious vomiting?

A: Midgut volvulus.

Q1047: Which pediatric abdominal emergency is treated with air enema rather than immediate surgery?

A: Intussusception (if stable and without perforation/peritonitis).

Q1048: Which congenital GI disorder classically presents with painless rectal bleeding?

A: Meckel diverticulum.

Q1049: Which neonatal disorder classically causes failure to pass meconium within 48 hours?

A: Hirschsprung disease.

Q1050: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for neonatal gastrointestinal emergencies?

A: Bilious vomiting in a neonate is a surgical emergency until proven otherwise. Projectile nonbilious vomiting suggests pyloric stenosis, currant jelly stool suggests intussusception, painless GI bleeding suggests Meckel diverticulum, and delayed meconium passage suggests Hirschsprung disease.

Q1051: What is esophageal atresia?

A: Congenital interruption of the esophagus, preventing continuity between the proximal and distal esophagus.

Q1052: What is the most common type of esophageal atresia?

A: Proximal esophageal atresia with a distal tracheoesophageal fistula (EA with distal TEF).

Q1053: What are the classic neonatal findings of esophageal atresia?

A: Excessive salivation, choking, coughing, cyanosis during feeding, and inability to pass a nasogastric tube.

Q1054: Which prenatal finding commonly suggests esophageal atresia?

A: Polyhydramnios.

Q1055: What radiographic finding supports the diagnosis of EA with distal TEF?

A: A nasogastric tube coiled in the proximal esophageal pouch with air in the stomach and intestines.

Q1056: What is the definitive treatment for EA/TEF?

A: Surgical repair after stabilization.

Q1057: Which congenital anomaly association should always be considered in infants with EA/TEF?

A: VACTERL association (Vertebral, Anal, Cardiac, Tracheoesophageal, Renal, Limb anomalies).

Congenital Diaphragmatic Hernia (CDH)

Q1058: What causes congenital diaphragmatic hernia?

A: Failure of complete diaphragm formation, allowing abdominal organs to herniate into the thoracic cavity.

Q1059: Which side is most commonly affected in CDH?

A: Left posterolateral side (Bochdalek hernia).

Q1060: What is the classic presentation of congenital diaphragmatic hernia?

A: Severe respiratory distress immediately after birth with a scaphoid abdomen.

Q1061: Why do infants with CDH develop severe respiratory failure?

A: Pulmonary hypoplasia and persistent pulmonary hypertension.

Q1062: What is the first priority in managing CDH?

A: Immediate endotracheal intubation and gastric decompression with a nasogastric tube.

Q1063: Should bag-mask ventilation be used in congenital diaphragmatic hernia?

A: No. It can worsen respiratory compromise by insufflating bowel within the thorax.

Q1064: What is the definitive treatment for CDH?

A: Surgical repair after cardiopulmonary stabilization.

Omphalocele

Q1065: What is an omphalocele?

A: Herniation of abdominal contents through the umbilical ring covered by a membranous sac.

Q1066: Which congenital abnormalities are commonly associated with omphalocele?

A: Cardiac defects, chromosomal abnormalities (e.g., trisomy 13 and 18), and Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome.

Q1067: What is the initial management of omphalocele?

A: Protect the sac, prevent heat and fluid loss, stabilize the infant, and plan surgical repair.

Gastroschisis

Q1068: What is gastroschisis?

A: Full-thickness abdominal wall defect, usually to the right of the umbilicus, with exposed bowel and no protective sac.

Q1069: How does gastroschisis differ from omphalocele?

A: Gastroschisis has no covering membrane and fewer associated congenital anomalies.

Q1070: What is the initial management of gastroschisis?

A: Cover exposed bowel with sterile saline-moistened dressings and plastic wrap, provide IV fluids, gastric decompression, temperature control, and urgent surgical consultation.

Q1071: What is the definitive treatment for gastroschisis?

A: Surgical reduction and abdominal wall closure (primary or staged using a silo).

Anorectal Malformations

Q1072: What is imperforate anus?

A: Congenital absence or abnormal location of the anal opening.

Q1073: What is the typical neonatal presentation of imperforate anus?

A: Failure to pass meconium with absent or abnormal anal opening.

Q1074: Which congenital association should be considered in imperforate anus?

A: VACTERL association.

Q1075: What is the definitive treatment for imperforate anus?

A: Surgical correction, often after evaluation for associated anomalies.

Pediatric Inguinal Hernia

Q1076: What causes indirect inguinal hernias in children?

A: Persistence of the processus vaginalis.

Q1077: Which children are at greatest risk for incarcerated inguinal hernia?

A: Premature infants and young infants.

Q1078: What is the treatment for pediatric inguinal hernia?

A: Elective surgical repair after diagnosis; urgent surgery if incarcerated or strangulated.

Hydrocele

Q1079: What causes a communicating hydrocele?

A: Persistent processus vaginalis allowing peritoneal fluid to enter the scrotum.

Q1080: When is surgery indicated for pediatric hydrocele?

A: Persistent communicating hydrocele (or one that does not resolve appropriately with observation), symptomatic hydrocele, or associated hernia.

Cryptorchidism (Undescended Testis)

Q1081: What is cryptorchidism?

A: Failure of one or both testes to descend into the scrotum.

Q1082: What are the long-term complications of untreated cryptorchidism?

A: Infertility, testicular cancer, inguinal hernia, and testicular torsion.

Q1083: At what age should orchiopexy ideally be performed?

A: Between 6–18 months of age (commonly by 12–18 months).

Q1084: Does orchiopexy eliminate the future risk of testicular cancer?

A: No, but it reduces risk and improves surveillance.

Pediatric Testicular Disorders

Q1085: What is the most common cause of acute scrotal pain requiring emergency surgery in children?

A: Testicular torsion.

Q1086: What is the first imaging study if torsion is uncertain?

A: Color Doppler scrotal ultrasound.

Q1087: If clinical suspicion for torsion is high, should surgery await imaging?

A: No. Immediate surgical exploration is indicated.

Necrotizing Soft Tissue Infection

Q1088: Which rapidly progressive pediatric soft tissue infection requires emergency surgery?

A: Necrotizing fasciitis.

Q1089: What is the treatment for necrotizing fasciitis?

A: Immediate surgical debridement plus broad-spectrum IV antibiotics and aggressive supportive care.

Pediatric Trauma

Q1090: Which solid abdominal organ is most commonly injured after blunt abdominal trauma in children?

A: Spleen.

Q1091: What is the preferred management of most hemodynamically stable pediatric splenic injuries?

A: Nonoperative management with close monitoring.

Q1092: Which imaging study is preferred for evaluating stable pediatric abdominal trauma?

A: Contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen and pelvis.

Pediatric Surgical Oncology

Q1093: What is the most common renal malignancy in children?

A: Wilms tumor (nephroblastoma).

Q1094: Which syndrome is classically associated with Wilms tumor?

A: WAGR syndrome (Wilms tumor, Aniridia, Genitourinary anomalies, intellectual disability).

Q1095: What is the initial treatment for localized Wilms tumor?

A: Radical nephrectomy with multimodal therapy based on staging.

Pediatric Surgical Principles

Q1096: Which congenital abdominal wall defect is covered by a membrane?

A: Omphalocele.

Q1097: Which congenital abdominal wall defect has exposed bowel without a membrane?

A: Gastroschisis.

Q1098: Which neonatal respiratory emergency requires immediate intubation and avoidance of bag-mask ventilation?

A: Congenital diaphragmatic hernia.

Q1099: Which congenital anomaly is frequently associated with multiple organ-system defects requiring systematic evaluation?

A: Esophageal atresia/tracheoesophageal fistula and imperforate anus (consider VACTERL association).

Q1100: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for pediatric surgery?

A: Membrane present = omphalocele; membrane absent = gastroschisis. Bilious vomiting is volvulus until proven otherwise, failure to pass an NG tube suggests esophageal atresia, avoid bag-mask ventilation in congenital diaphragmatic hernia, and perform early orchiopexy for undescended testes.

Topic: Plastic, Reconstructive, Skin & Soft Tissue Surgery

Q1101: What is the most common skin cancer?

A: Basal cell carcinoma (BCC).

Q1102: What is the strongest risk factor for BCC?

A: Chronic ultraviolet (UV) radiation exposure.

Q1103: What is the classic appearance of basal cell carcinoma?

A: Pearly papule with rolled borders and surface telangiectasias; ulceration may occur ("rodent ulcer").

Q1104: Which areas are most commonly affected by BCC?

A: Sun-exposed areas, especially the head and neck.

Q1105: Does basal cell carcinoma commonly metastasize?

A: No. Metastasis is extremely rare, but local tissue destruction can be significant.

Q1106: What is the definitive diagnosis of BCC?

A: Skin biopsy.

Q1107: What is the preferred treatment for most localized BCCs?

A: Surgical excision with histologically clear margins.

Q1108: When is Mohs micrographic surgery preferred for BCC?

A: High-risk lesions (e.g., cosmetically sensitive areas, recurrent tumors, or lesions with ill-defined borders).

Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)

Q1109: What is the second most common skin cancer?

A: Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (SCC).

Q1110: What are major risk factors for SCC?

A: Chronic UV exposure, immunosuppression, chronic wounds/scars (Marjolin ulcer), radiation exposure, and HPV for some anatomic sites.

Q1111: How does SCC typically appear?

A: Firm, hyperkeratotic, scaly plaque or nodule that may ulcerate.

Q1112: Does SCC have metastatic potential?

A: Yes, particularly for larger, deeper, high-risk, or immunosuppression-associated tumors.

Q1113: What is the preferred treatment for localized SCC?

A: Surgical excision; Mohs surgery is used for selected high-risk lesions.

Melanoma

Q1114: What is the most dangerous common skin cancer?

A: Melanoma.

Q1115: Which mnemonic is used to evaluate suspicious pigmented lesions?

A: ABCDE:

Asymmetry
Border irregularity
Color variation
Diameter ≥6 mm (or enlarging lesion)
Evolving lesion

Q1116: What is the strongest environmental risk factor for melanoma?

A: Intermittent intense UV exposure causing blistering sunburns.

Q1117: Which patients have increased melanoma risk?

A: Fair skin, numerous or atypical nevi, family history, prior melanoma, and immunosuppression.

Q1118: What is the preferred biopsy for suspected melanoma?

A: Excisional biopsy with narrow margins whenever feasible.

Q1119: Which histologic measurement is the most important prognostic factor for localized melanoma?

A: Breslow thickness.

Q1120: When is sentinel lymph node biopsy generally considered?

A: For melanomas with sufficient Breslow thickness and/or other high-risk features according to current guidelines.

Q1121: What is the definitive treatment for localized melanoma?

A: Wide local excision with margins based on Breslow thickness.

Pressure Injuries (Pressure Ulcers)

Q1122: What causes pressure injuries?

A: Prolonged pressure causing tissue ischemia, often over bony prominences.

Q1123: Which patients are at greatest risk for pressure injuries?

A: Immobile, elderly, malnourished, spinal cord–injured, and critically ill patients.

Q1124: Which area is most commonly affected?

A: Sacrum.

Q1125: How is a Stage I pressure injury defined?

A: Intact skin with nonblanchable erythema.

Q1126: How is a Stage II pressure injury defined?

A: Partial-thickness skin loss involving epidermis and/or dermis.

Q1127: How is a Stage III pressure injury defined?

A: Full-thickness skin loss extending into subcutaneous tissue.

Q1128: How is a Stage IV pressure injury defined?

A: Full-thickness tissue loss with exposed muscle, tendon, fascia, cartilage, or bone.

Q1129: What is the cornerstone of pressure injury prevention?

A: Frequent repositioning and pressure redistribution.

Q1130: What is the cornerstone of pressure injury treatment?

A: Pressure relief, wound care, nutritional optimization, and debridement of necrotic tissue when indicated.

Keloids & Hypertrophic Scars

Q1131: What is a hypertrophic scar?

A: Excess scar tissue confined to the original wound boundaries.

Q1132: What is a keloid?

A: Excess scar tissue extending beyond the original wound margins.

Q1133: Which patients are more prone to keloid formation?

A: Individuals with darker skin pigmentation and a positive family history.

Q1134: What is the first-line treatment for symptomatic keloids?

A: Intralesional corticosteroid injections (often combined with other therapies as needed).

Skin Grafts

Q1135: What is a split-thickness skin graft (STSG)?

A: Graft containing the epidermis and part of the dermis.

Q1136: What is a full-thickness skin graft (FTSG)?

A: Graft containing the epidermis and the entire dermis.

Q1137: Which type of graft generally has better cosmetic results?

A: Full-thickness skin graft.

Q1138: Which graft generally has higher take rates on less ideal wound beds?

A: Split-thickness skin graft.

Flaps

Q1139: What is the difference between a flap and a graft?

A: A flap retains or receives its own blood supply, whereas a graft relies on revascularization from the recipient bed.

Q1140: When are flaps preferred over grafts?

A: When vascularized tissue is needed to cover exposed bone, tendon, hardware, or poorly vascularized wounds.

Bite Wounds

Q1141: Which animal causes the most bite injuries?

A: Dogs.

Q1142: Which animal bite carries the highest infection risk?

A: Cat bites.

Q1143: Which organism is classically associated with cat and dog bites?

A: Pasteurella multocida.

Q1144: What is the first-line antibiotic for prophylaxis or treatment of many dog and cat bites?

A: Amoxicillin-clavulanate.

Q1145: Which organism is classically associated with human bites?

A: Eikenella corrodens.

Q1146: Which human bite injury is considered particularly high risk?

A: Closed-fist ("fight bite") injury over the metacarpophalangeal joint.

Soft Tissue Masses

Q1147: What is the most common benign soft tissue tumor?

A: Lipoma.

Q1148: Which features of a soft tissue mass raise concern for sarcoma?

A: Size >5 cm, deep location, rapid growth, firmness, fixation, or recurrence.

Q1149: Which imaging study is preferred before biopsy of a suspected soft tissue sarcoma?

A: MRI with contrast (for extremity lesions).

Q1150: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for skin and soft tissue surgery?

A: BCC rarely metastasizes but can be locally destructive. Melanoma prognosis depends largely on Breslow thickness. Cat bites have a high infection risk, human bites require aggressive management, pressure injuries are best prevented with frequent repositioning, and suspicious soft tissue masses should be evaluated for sarcoma before definitive treatment.

Section: Surgery 
Topic: Surgical Critical Care & ICU

Q1151: How is sepsis defined?

A: Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection.

Q1152: What bedside score is commonly used to rapidly identify patients at risk for poor outcomes from sepsis?

A: qSOFA (Quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment).

Q1153: What are the components of qSOFA?

A: Respiratory rate ≥22/min, altered mental status, and systolic blood pressure ≤100 mmHg.

Q1154: What is septic shock?

A: A subset of sepsis with persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors to maintain MAP ≥65 mmHg despite adequate fluid resuscitation, with elevated serum lactate indicating significant circulatory/metabolic dysfunction.

Q1155: What are the three priorities during the initial management of sepsis?

A: Early recognition, rapid IV fluid resuscitation, and prompt broad-spectrum antibiotics.

Q1156: How soon should broad-spectrum antibiotics ideally be administered after sepsis is recognized?

A: As soon as possible, ideally within 1 hour in septic shock or high suspicion.

Q1157: What is the preferred initial crystalloid for fluid resuscitation in septic shock?

A: Balanced crystalloids (e.g., lactated Ringer's) are often preferred; normal saline is also acceptable.

Q1158: What initial crystalloid volume is commonly recommended for septic shock?

A: Approximately 30 mL/kg of IV crystalloid for patients with hypoperfusion, while individualizing based on clinical status.

Q1159: What laboratory marker is commonly followed to assess tissue hypoperfusion in sepsis?

A: Serum lactate.

Q1160: What is source control in sepsis?

A: Elimination of the infection source (e.g., abscess drainage, debridement, removal of infected devices, surgery).

Vasopressors

Q1161: Which vasopressor is first-line for septic shock?

A: Norepinephrine.

Q1162: What is the target mean arterial pressure (MAP) in most patients with septic shock?

A: ≥65 mmHg.

Q1163: Which vasopressor is commonly added if norepinephrine alone is insufficient?

A: Vasopressin (typically as an adjunct).

Q1164: Which catecholamine is often used when there is persistent hypoperfusion with reduced cardiac output despite adequate MAP?

A: Dobutamine (when appropriate for myocardial dysfunction).

Q1165: When should vasopressors be started?

A: After or during adequate fluid resuscitation if hypotension persists.

Mechanical Ventilation

Q1166: What is the most common indication for endotracheal intubation in the ICU?

A: Acute respiratory failure with inability to maintain oxygenation, ventilation, or airway protection.

Q1167: What are the two major categories of respiratory failure?

A: Hypoxemic (Type I) and hypercapnic (Type II).

Q1168: Which ventilator strategy is recommended for ARDS?

A: Lung-protective ventilation using low tidal volumes.

Q1169: What tidal volume is recommended in ARDS?

A: Approximately 6 mL/kg predicted body weight.

Q1170: Why are low tidal volumes used in ARDS?

A: To reduce ventilator-induced lung injury.

Q1171: What is positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP)?

A: Pressure maintained in the lungs at end expiration to prevent alveolar collapse.

Q1172: What is a potential complication of excessive PEEP?

A: Reduced venous return leading to hypotension and possible barotrauma.

Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)

Q1173: What is ARDS?

A: Acute inflammatory lung injury causing diffuse alveolar damage and noncardiogenic pulmonary edema.

Q1174: What are common causes of ARDS?

A: Sepsis, pneumonia, aspiration, major trauma, pancreatitis, massive transfusion, and severe burns.

Q1175: What are the characteristic chest X-ray findings in ARDS?

A: Bilateral diffuse pulmonary infiltrates not fully explained by heart failure or volume overload.

Q1176: What is the hallmark physiologic abnormality in ARDS?

A: Severe hypoxemia due to intrapulmonary shunting.

Q1177: What is the primary treatment for ARDS?

A: Treat the underlying cause and provide lung-protective mechanical ventilation.

Q1178: Which positioning strategy improves oxygenation in severe ARDS?

A: Prone positioning.

Q1179: Should routine corticosteroids be given to every patient with ARDS?

A: No. Use depends on the underlying cause and current evidence/guidelines.

Sedation & Analgesia

Q1180: Which medication is commonly used for ICU sedation?

A: Propofol (among several acceptable options).

Q1181: What is a major adverse effect of propofol?

A: Hypotension.

Q1182: Which sedative provides anxiolysis with minimal respiratory depression and may facilitate lighter sedation?

A: Dexmedetomidine.

Q1183: Which medications are commonly used for analgesia in mechanically ventilated ICU patients?

A: Opioids such as fentanyl or hydromorphone, tailored to the clinical situation.

Q1184: Why are daily spontaneous awakening trials often performed?

A: To minimize oversedation, shorten ventilation duration, and facilitate neurologic assessment when appropriate.

ICU Delirium

Q1185: Which ICU complication is associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation and worse outcomes?

A: Delirium.

Q1186: What are important nonpharmacologic measures to reduce ICU delirium?

A: Early mobilization, sleep promotion, reorientation, minimizing unnecessary sedation, and ensuring use of hearing aids/glasses when needed.

Stress Ulcer Prophylaxis

Q1187: Which ICU patients generally require stress ulcer prophylaxis?

A: Selected high-risk patients (e.g., prolonged mechanical ventilation, coagulopathy, major burns, traumatic brain injury).

Q1188: Which medications are commonly used for stress ulcer prophylaxis?

A: Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) or H₂ receptor blockers.

Venous Thromboembolism (VTE) Prophylaxis

Q1189: Why do ICU patients require VTE prophylaxis?

A: They are at high risk for DVT and pulmonary embolism due to immobility and critical illness.

Q1190: What pharmacologic agents are commonly used for VTE prophylaxis?

A: Low-molecular-weight heparin or low-dose unfractionated heparin unless contraindicated.

Hemodynamic Monitoring

Q1191: What is the purpose of an arterial line?

A: Continuous blood pressure monitoring and frequent arterial blood gas sampling.

Q1192: What is the primary use of a central venous catheter in the ICU?

A: Administration of vasopressors, central venous access, and selected hemodynamic monitoring.

Oxygen Delivery

Q1193: What determines oxygen delivery (DO₂) to tissues?

A: Cardiac output × arterial oxygen content.

Q1194: What are the major components of arterial oxygen content?

A: Hemoglobin concentration and arterial oxygen saturation (with a small dissolved oxygen component).

ICU Infections

Q1195: What is the best method for preventing catheter-associated bloodstream infections?

A: Strict aseptic insertion technique and prompt removal when no longer needed.

Q1196: What is the most effective strategy for preventing ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP)?

A: Ventilator care bundles (e.g., head-of-bed elevation, oral hygiene, minimizing sedation, and daily assessment for extubation readiness).

High-Yield Review

Q1197: Which vasopressor is first-line for septic shock?

A: Norepinephrine.

Q1198: Which ventilator strategy reduces mortality in ARDS?

A: Low tidal volume (lung-protective) ventilation.

Q1199: What is the definitive treatment for septic shock beyond antibiotics and vasopressors?

A: Source control (e.g., drainage, debridement, or surgery when indicated).

Q1200: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for surgical critical care?

A: Recognize sepsis early, administer prompt antibiotics, aggressively resuscitate with crystalloids, use norepinephrine as the first-line vasopressor if hypotension persists, perform source control, and ventilate ARDS patients with low tidal volumes and appropriate PEEP.

Q1201: What is the preferred route of nutritional support in critically ill patients with a functional gastrointestinal tract?

A: Enteral nutrition (EN).

Q1202: Why is enteral nutrition preferred over total parenteral nutrition (TPN)?

A: It maintains gut mucosal integrity, reduces bacterial translocation, lowers infectious complications, and is generally less costly.

Q1203: When should enteral nutrition generally be initiated in hemodynamically stable ICU patients?

A: Within 24–48 hours of ICU admission if feasible.

Q1204: When is total parenteral nutrition (TPN) indicated?

A: When the gastrointestinal tract cannot be used or enteral feeding is contraindicated or insufficient.

Q1205: What are common complications of TPN?

A: Catheter-related bloodstream infection, hyperglycemia, electrolyte abnormalities, hepatic dysfunction, and thrombosis.

Refeeding Syndrome

Q1206: What is refeeding syndrome?

A: Potentially life-threatening electrolyte and fluid shifts that occur after restarting nutrition in severely malnourished patients.

Q1207: Which electrolyte abnormality is the hallmark of refeeding syndrome?

A: Hypophosphatemia.

Q1208: Which other electrolyte abnormalities commonly occur in refeeding syndrome?

A: Hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia.

Q1209: Which patients are at highest risk for refeeding syndrome?

A: Patients with prolonged starvation, anorexia nervosa, chronic alcohol use disorder, severe malnutrition, or significant recent weight loss.

Q1210: How can refeeding syndrome be prevented?

A: Begin nutrition slowly, replace electrolytes (especially phosphate, potassium, and magnesium), and administer thiamine before or with refeeding in high-risk patients.

Blood Transfusion

Q1211: What blood component is used to increase oxygen-carrying capacity?

A: Packed red blood cells (PRBCs).

Q1212: What blood component is used for thrombocytopenia with bleeding or before invasive procedures when indicated?

A: Platelets.

Q1213: What blood component is used to replace multiple clotting factors?

A: Fresh frozen plasma (FFP).

Q1214: Which blood product is rich in fibrinogen?

A: Cryoprecipitate.

Q1215: Which blood type is considered the universal donor for packed red blood cells?

A: O Rh-negative.

Q1216: Which blood type is the universal recipient for packed red blood cells?

A: AB Rh-positive.

Transfusion Reactions

Q1217: What is the most serious acute transfusion reaction?

A: Acute hemolytic transfusion reaction due to ABO incompatibility.

Q1218: What are common symptoms of an acute hemolytic transfusion reaction?

A: Fever, chills, flank pain, hypotension, hemoglobinuria, and possible disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).

Q1219: What is the first step in managing any suspected transfusion reaction?

A: Immediately stop the transfusion while maintaining IV access with normal saline and begin evaluation.

Q1220: What is transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI)?

A: Acute noncardiogenic pulmonary edema occurring during or within 6 hours of transfusion.

Massive Transfusion

Q1221: What is massive transfusion?

A: Commonly defined as transfusion of ≥10 units of PRBCs within 24 hours or equivalent replacement of blood volume.

Q1222: What is the goal of a massive transfusion protocol (MTP)?

A: Rapid balanced replacement of blood components while correcting coagulopathy and maintaining perfusion.

Q1223: Which electrolyte abnormality commonly develops during massive transfusion because of citrate?

A: Hypocalcemia.

Q1224: Why should calcium levels be monitored during massive transfusion?

A: Citrate in stored blood binds ionized calcium, which may impair myocardial function and coagulation.

Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)

Q1225: What is DIC?

A: Widespread activation of coagulation leading to simultaneous thrombosis and bleeding.

Q1226: What are common causes of DIC?

A: Sepsis, severe trauma, obstetric emergencies, and malignancy.

Q1227: What laboratory abnormalities are typical of DIC?

A: ↓ Platelets, ↑ PT/INR, ↑ aPTT, ↓ fibrinogen, and ↑ D-dimer.

Q1228: What is the cornerstone of DIC treatment?

A: Treat the underlying cause and provide supportive transfusion therapy when clinically indicated.

Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

Q1229: What is the most common cause of AKI in postoperative patients?

A: Prerenal azotemia due to hypovolemia or decreased renal perfusion.

Q1230: What is the first step in evaluating postoperative oliguria?

A: Assess volume status, hemodynamics, urine output trends, medications, and exclude urinary tract obstruction.

Q1231: What is the preferred initial treatment for prerenal AKI?

A: Restore adequate intravascular volume and improve renal perfusion.

Q1232: Which urinary electrolyte finding suggests prerenal AKI?

A: Low urine sodium (typically <20 mEq/L) and low fractional excretion of sodium (FeNa <1%) in appropriate clinical settings.

Compartment Syndrome

Q1233: What causes compartment syndrome?

A: Increased pressure within a closed fascial compartment causing impaired tissue perfusion.

Q1234: What is the earliest and most important symptom of compartment syndrome?

A: Severe pain out of proportion to the injury, especially with passive stretch.

Q1235: Which "6 Ps" are associated with compartment syndrome?

A: Pain, pallor, paresthesia, paralysis, pulselessness, and poikilothermia (pulselessness is a late finding).

Q1236: What is the definitive treatment for compartment syndrome?

A: Emergent fasciotomy.

Rhabdomyolysis

Q1237: What is rhabdomyolysis?

A: Skeletal muscle breakdown causing release of intracellular contents, including myoglobin.

Q1238: Which laboratory value is most sensitive for rhabdomyolysis?

A: Markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK).

Q1239: Which electrolyte abnormality commonly occurs early in rhabdomyolysis?

A: Hyperkalemia.

Q1240: What is the primary treatment for rhabdomyolysis?

A: Aggressive IV isotonic fluid resuscitation while treating the underlying cause.

ICU Electrolytes

Q1241: Which electrolyte abnormality is most likely to cause life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias?

A: Hyperkalemia.

Q1242: What ECG changes are classically seen in hyperkalemia?

A: Peaked T waves, PR prolongation, QRS widening, and, in severe cases, a sine-wave pattern.

Q1243: What is the first medication given for hyperkalemia with ECG changes?

A: IV calcium (calcium gluconate or calcium chloride, depending on the clinical setting) to stabilize the cardiac membrane.

Q1244: Which therapies shift potassium into cells?

A: IV insulin with glucose, β₂-agonists (e.g., albuterol), and sodium bicarbonate in selected patients with metabolic acidosis.

Q1245: Which treatment removes potassium from the body most rapidly in severe or refractory cases?

A: Hemodialysis.

Acid–Base Disorders

Q1246: Which acid–base disorder is commonly seen in septic shock?

A: High anion gap metabolic acidosis due to lactic acidosis.

Q1247: Which acid–base disorder is classically associated with prolonged vomiting?

A: Hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis.

Q1248: Which acid–base disorder commonly occurs with severe diarrhea?

A: Non–anion gap (hyperchloremic) metabolic acidosis.

High-Yield ICU Review

Q1249: What is the preferred nutritional route in critically ill patients with a functioning gastrointestinal tract?

A: Enteral nutrition.

Q1250: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for surgical critical care?

A: Feed the gut when possible, recognize hypophosphatemia as the hallmark of refeeding syndrome, stop any transfusion immediately if a reaction is suspected, treat compartment syndrome with emergent fasciotomy, aggressively hydrate rhabdomyolysis, and stabilize the myocardium first with IV calcium in hyperkalemia with ECG changes.

Topic: Anesthesia Basics

Q1401: What are the goals of the pre-anesthetic evaluation?

A: Assess perioperative risk, optimize medical conditions, identify difficult airway, review medications/allergies, determine anesthesia plan, and obtain informed consent.

Q1402: Which patient history is most important before anesthesia?

A: Previous anesthesia complications, difficult intubation, malignant hyperthermia, allergies, medications, cardiopulmonary disease, and obstructive sleep apnea.

Q1403: Why should previous anesthesia records be reviewed?

Q1404: Which inherited anesthetic complication should always be asked about during the preoperative assessment?

A: Malignant hyperthermia.

Q1405: Which medications require special perioperative planning?

A: Anticoagulants, antiplatelets, insulin, oral hypoglycemics, corticosteroids, antihypertensives, and immunosuppressants.

ASA Physical Status Classification

Q1406: What is the ASA Physical Status Classification used for?

A: Estimating perioperative anesthetic risk based on overall systemic health.

Q1407: What is ASA Class I?

A: Healthy patient with no systemic disease.

Q1408: What is ASA Class II?

A: Mild systemic disease without functional limitation (e.g., well-controlled hypertension or diabetes).

Q1409: What is ASA Class III?

A: Severe systemic disease with functional limitation (e.g., stable angina, poorly controlled diabetes).

Q1410: What is ASA Class IV?

A: Severe systemic disease that is a constant threat to life (e.g., decompensated heart failure).

Q1411: What is ASA Class V?

A: Moribund patient not expected to survive without the operation.

Q1412: What is ASA Class VI?

A: Brain-dead organ donor.

Q1413: What does the suffix "E" indicate in ASA classification?

A: Emergency surgery.

Airway Assessment

Q1414: Why is airway assessment essential before anesthesia?

A: To predict difficult ventilation or intubation and prepare appropriate airway management.

Q1415: Which bedside airway classification is most commonly used?

A: Mallampati classification.

Q1416: How is Mallampati Class I defined?

A: Soft palate, uvula, fauces, and tonsillar pillars are fully visible.

Q1417: How is Mallampati Class II defined?

A: Uvula is partially obscured but mostly visible.

Q1418: How is Mallampati Class III defined?

A: Only the base of the uvula and soft palate are visible.

Q1419: How is Mallampati Class IV defined?

A: Only the hard palate is visible.

Q1420: Which Mallampati classes predict the greatest likelihood of difficult intubation?

A: Class III and Class IV.

Thyromental Distance

Q1421: What is the thyromental distance?

A: Distance from the thyroid notch to the mentum (chin).

Q1422: Which thyromental distance suggests difficult intubation?

A: Less than approximately 6 cm (or fewer than 3 fingerbreadths).

Mouth Opening

Q1423: What minimum mouth opening is generally needed for conventional laryngoscopy?

A: Approximately 3 fingerbreadths.

Q1424: Which conditions reduce mouth opening?

A: TMJ disorders, facial trauma, radiation fibrosis, and trismus.

Neck Mobility

Q1425: Why is neck extension important during intubation?

A: It aligns the oral, pharyngeal, and laryngeal axes for improved visualization.

Q1426: Which patients commonly have limited neck mobility?

A: Cervical spine injury, rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, and severe cervical spondylosis.

Difficult Airway Predictors

Q1427: Name common predictors of a difficult airway.

A: Mallampati III/IV, obesity, OSA, short neck, limited mouth opening, reduced neck mobility, facial trauma, and prior difficult intubation.

Q1428: Which historical factor is the strongest predictor of future difficult intubation?

A: Previous documented difficult intubation.

STOP-BANG

Q1429: What is STOP-BANG used for?

A: Screening for obstructive sleep apnea (OSA).

Q1430: What does STOP stand for?

A: Snoring, Tiredness, Observed apnea, high blood Pressure.

Q1431: What does BANG stand for?

A: BMI >35 kg/m², Age >50 years, Neck circumference >40 cm, Gender male.

Q1432: Why is OSA important before surgery?

A: It increases the risk of difficult airway and postoperative respiratory depression.

Laboratory Evaluation

Q1433: Should routine preoperative laboratory testing be ordered for every patient?

A: No. Testing should be guided by the patient's history, physical examination, and planned procedure.

Q1434: Which laboratory test is commonly indicated before major surgery with anticipated blood loss?

A: CBC and type & screen (or type & crossmatch if transfusion is likely).

Q1435: When should coagulation studies be obtained preoperatively?

A: In patients with bleeding disorders, liver disease, anticoagulant use, or suspected coagulopathy.

Cardiac Evaluation

Q1436: Which active cardiac conditions generally require evaluation before elective surgery?

A: Unstable angina, decompensated heart failure, significant arrhythmias, and severe symptomatic valvular disease.

Q1437: Is a resting ECG indicated for every surgical patient?

A: No. It is obtained selectively based on age, cardiovascular risk factors, symptoms, and surgical risk.

Pulmonary Evaluation

Q1438: Which patients are at highest risk for postoperative pulmonary complications?

A: Smokers, patients with COPD, advanced age, obesity, OSA, and those undergoing thoracic or upper abdominal surgery.

Q1439: What is the most effective preoperative intervention for smokers?

A: Smoking cessation as early as possible before surgery.

Medication Management

Q1440: Should chronic corticosteroids be stopped abruptly before surgery?

A: No. Patients may require perioperative glucocorticoid supplementation depending on adrenal suppression risk.

Q1441: Which diabetic medication is commonly withheld on the day of surgery to reduce hypoglycemia risk?

A: Short-acting insulin doses are adjusted, and many oral hypoglycemic agents are withheld; management depends on the regimen.

Q1442: Why are anticoagulants reviewed carefully before surgery?

A: To balance bleeding risk against thromboembolic risk and determine if interruption or bridging is needed.

Informed Consent

Q1443: What are the four essential elements of informed consent?

A: Decision-making capacity, adequate information, voluntary choice, and patient understanding.

Q1444: Who should obtain informed consent for surgery?

A: The physician performing (or responsible for) the procedure.

Pregnancy

Q1445: Why should pregnancy status be considered before anesthesia?

A: Pregnancy affects anesthetic management and fetal safety, and may alter surgical planning.

Obesity

Q1446: Why are obese patients at higher anesthetic risk?

A: Increased risk of difficult airway, hypoventilation, aspiration, OSA, and postoperative pulmonary complications.

Aspiration Risk

Q1447: Which patients are at increased risk for aspiration during anesthesia?

A: Full stomach, bowel obstruction, pregnancy, obesity, gastroparesis, GERD, and emergency surgery.

Q1448: Which airway technique is commonly used in patients at high aspiration risk?

A: Rapid Sequence Intubation (RSI).

High-Yield Review

Q1449: What is the most important predictor of difficult intubation?

A: Previous difficult intubation.

Q1450: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK anesthesia principle?

A: A thorough pre-anesthetic evaluation—including ASA classification, airway assessment (Mallampati, mouth opening, neck mobility), medication review, and aspiration risk assessment—is essential to anticipate complications and guide safe anesthetic management.

Q1451: Why are preoperative fasting (NPO) guidelines important?

A: To reduce the risk of pulmonary aspiration during induction of anesthesia.

Q1452: How long before elective anesthesia may healthy patients drink clear liquids?

A: 2 hours.

Q1453: Which fluids are considered clear liquids?

A: Water, pulp-free juice, black coffee, clear tea, and carbohydrate-rich clear beverages.

Q1454: How long before anesthesia may infants receive breast milk?

A: 4 hours.

Q1455: How long before anesthesia should infant formula be stopped?

A: 6 hours.

Q1456: How long before anesthesia should a light meal be stopped?

A: 6 hours.

Q1457: How long before anesthesia should a fatty meal be stopped?

A: 8 hours or longer.

Q1458: Does emergency surgery wait until NPO guidelines are met?

A: No. Emergencies proceed with aspiration precautions, usually using Rapid Sequence Intubation (RSI).

Types of Anesthesia

Q1459: What are the four major categories of anesthesia?

A: General anesthesia, regional anesthesia, local anesthesia, and monitored anesthesia care (MAC).

Q1460: What is general anesthesia?

A: Drug-induced unconsciousness with loss of protective airway reflexes, requiring airway management.

Q1461: What is regional anesthesia?

A: Temporary blockade of nerve transmission to a specific region while the patient may remain awake or lightly sedated.

Q1462: What is local anesthesia?

A: Local infiltration of anesthetic to numb a small area without affecting consciousness.

Q1463: What is monitored anesthesia care (MAC)?

A: Sedation with continuous monitoring while maintaining spontaneous ventilation, with readiness to convert to general anesthesia if needed.

General Anesthesia

Q1464: What are the three major components of balanced general anesthesia?

A: Hypnosis (unconsciousness), analgesia, and muscle relaxation.

Q1465: Which airway reflexes are lost during general anesthesia?

A: Protective reflexes such as coughing and gagging.

Q1466: Why do patients receiving general anesthesia often require endotracheal intubation?

A: To secure the airway, facilitate ventilation, and reduce aspiration risk.

Regional Anesthesia

Q1467: What is spinal anesthesia?

A: Injection of local anesthetic into the cerebrospinal fluid in the subarachnoid space.

Q1468: Where is spinal anesthesia administered?

A: Into the lumbar subarachnoid space, usually below the termination of the spinal cord (e.g., L3–L4 or L4–L5).

Q1469: What is epidural anesthesia?

A: Injection of local anesthetic into the epidural space outside the dura.

Q1470: What is a major advantage of epidural anesthesia over spinal anesthesia?

A: A catheter can provide continuous dosing and prolonged analgesia.

Q1471: Which surgeries commonly use spinal anesthesia?

A: Lower abdominal, pelvic, perineal, and lower-extremity procedures.

Q1472: Which complication is more common after spinal anesthesia than epidural anesthesia?

A: Post-dural puncture headache.

Peripheral Nerve Blocks

Q1473: What is the purpose of a peripheral nerve block?

A: To provide targeted anesthesia and analgesia to a specific limb or body region.

Q1474: What is a major advantage of peripheral nerve blocks?

A: Reduced systemic opioid requirements and excellent postoperative pain control.

Airway Devices

Q1475: What is the simplest airway maneuver to relieve upper airway obstruction?

A: Head tilt–chin lift (or jaw thrust if cervical spine injury is suspected).

Q1476: When is the jaw-thrust maneuver preferred?

A: In patients with possible cervical spine injury.

Q1477: What is an oropharyngeal airway (OPA)?

A: An airway adjunct used in unconscious patients without an intact gag reflex.

Q1478: When is an oropharyngeal airway contraindicated?

A: In awake or semiconscious patients with an intact gag reflex.

Q1479: What is a nasopharyngeal airway (NPA)?

A: A soft airway inserted through the nose to help maintain airway patency in selected patients.

Q1480: When should a nasopharyngeal airway generally be avoided?

A: Suspected basilar skull fracture or severe facial trauma.

Laryngeal Mask Airway (LMA)

Q1481: What is a laryngeal mask airway (LMA)?

A: A supraglottic airway device positioned over the laryngeal inlet.

Q1482: What is the major advantage of an LMA?

A: Easier insertion than endotracheal intubation with less airway stimulation.

Q1483: What is a major limitation of the LMA?

A: It does not reliably protect against aspiration.

Endotracheal Intubation

Q1484: What is the gold standard for airway protection during general anesthesia?

A: Cuffed endotracheal intubation.

Q1485: How is correct endotracheal tube placement confirmed?

A: Continuous waveform capnography is the most reliable confirmation after intubation.

Q1486: Which bedside finding alone is insufficient to confirm proper endotracheal tube placement?

A: Bilateral breath sounds.

Rapid Sequence Intubation (RSI)

Q1487: What is the primary indication for Rapid Sequence Intubation?

A: Patients at high risk of aspiration.

Q1488: Which patients commonly require RSI?

A: Emergency surgery, bowel obstruction, trauma, pregnancy, obesity, gastroparesis, and full stomach.

Q1489: What are the basic steps of RSI?

A: Preoxygenation → induction agent → neuromuscular blocker → intubation without bag-mask ventilation (unless necessary) → confirmation with capnography.

Q1490: Why is positive-pressure bag-mask ventilation minimized during RSI?

A: To reduce gastric insufflation and aspiration risk.

Difficult Airway

Q1491: What is the first priority in difficult airway management?

A: Maintain oxygenation.

Q1492: What is the most dangerous difficult airway scenario?

A: Cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate (CICO).

Q1493: What is the definitive emergency treatment for a CICO airway?

A: Emergency cricothyrotomy.

Q1494: When should a surgical airway be considered?

A: When oxygenation cannot be maintained despite noninvasive airway attempts.

Q1495: Which device is commonly used for anticipated difficult intubation while maintaining spontaneous breathing?

A: Flexible fiberoptic bronchoscope (awake fiberoptic intubation in appropriate patients).

Airway Confirmation

Q1496: What is the most reliable bedside method for confirming tracheal intubation?

A: Continuous waveform capnography.

Q1497: What finding strongly suggests esophageal intubation?

A: Absence of persistent end-tidal CO₂ after several ventilations.

High-Yield Review

Q1498: Which airway device provides the best protection against aspiration?

A: Cuffed endotracheal tube.

Q1499: What is the emergency airway procedure for a "cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate" situation?

A: Cricothyrotomy.

Q1500: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK anesthesia principle?

A: Know the fasting guidelines, recognize aspiration risk, understand when to perform Rapid Sequence Intubation, confirm endotracheal tube placement with continuous waveform capnography, and remember that oxygenation takes priority over intubation—perform an emergency cricothyrotomy in a cannot-intubate, cannot-oxygenate scenario.

Q1501: What are the three major classes of drugs used to induce general anesthesia?

A: Induction agents (hypnotics), analgesics (usually opioids), and neuromuscular blocking agents.

Q1502: Does any induction agent provide all three components of balanced anesthesia?

A: No. Multiple agents are typically combined to provide hypnosis, analgesia, and muscle relaxation.

Propofol

Q1503: What is propofol?

A: A rapid-onset, short-acting IV hypnotic used for induction and maintenance of anesthesia and procedural sedation.

Q1504: What is the mechanism of action of propofol?

A: Potentiates GABA-A receptor activity, increasing inhibitory neurotransmission.

Q1505: What are the advantages of propofol?

A: Rapid onset, rapid recovery, antiemetic properties, and decreased intracranial pressure (ICP).

Q1506: What is the most common hemodynamic adverse effect of propofol?

A: Hypotension due to vasodilation and myocardial depression.

Q1507: Which respiratory adverse effect is commonly seen with propofol?

A: Respiratory depression and apnea.

Q1508: In which patients should propofol be used cautiously?

A: Patients with hypovolemia, severe hypotension, or hemodynamic instability.

Etomidate

Q1509: Why is etomidate commonly chosen for induction in critically ill patients?

A: It has minimal cardiovascular depression and maintains hemodynamic stability.

Q1510: What is the mechanism of action of etomidate?

A: Potentiates GABA-A receptor activity.

Q1511: What is the major adverse effect of etomidate?

A: Transient adrenal suppression (via inhibition of 11β-hydroxylase).

Q1512: Which patient is an ideal candidate for etomidate induction?

A: A hypotensive patient requiring emergency intubation.

Ketamine

Q1513: What is the mechanism of action of ketamine?

A: NMDA receptor antagonism.

Q1514: What type of anesthesia does ketamine produce?

A: Dissociative anesthesia.

Q1515: What cardiovascular effect distinguishes ketamine from most other induction agents?

A: It increases heart rate, blood pressure, and cardiac output through sympathetic stimulation.

Q1516: Which patients particularly benefit from ketamine induction?

A: Patients with hypotension, asthma, or severe bronchospasm.

Q1517: Why is ketamine useful in patients with asthma?

A: It has bronchodilatory properties.

Q1518: What are common adverse effects of ketamine?

A: Emergence reactions (hallucinations), hypersalivation, nausea, and vomiting.

Q1519: Which medication may reduce ketamine-associated emergence reactions?

A: A benzodiazepine (e.g., midazolam) in selected patients.

Benzodiazepines

Q1520: Which benzodiazepine is most commonly used perioperatively?

A: Midazolam.

Q1521: What is the mechanism of action of benzodiazepines?

A: They enhance GABA-A receptor activity by increasing the frequency of chloride channel opening.

Q1522: What are the major clinical effects of midazolam?

A: Anxiolysis, sedation, anterograde amnesia, and anticonvulsant activity.

Q1523: What is the major adverse effect of benzodiazepines?

A: Respiratory depression, especially when combined with opioids.

Dexmedetomidine

Q1524: What is the mechanism of action of dexmedetomidine?

A: Selective α₂-adrenergic agonist.

Q1525: What is a major advantage of dexmedetomidine?

A: Sedation with minimal respiratory depression.

Q1526: What are common adverse effects of dexmedetomidine?

A: Bradycardia and hypotension.

Opioids

Q1527: Which opioid is most commonly used during anesthesia?

A: Fentanyl.

Q1528: Why is fentanyl favored intraoperatively?

A: Rapid onset, potent analgesia, and relatively short duration of action.

Q1529: What is the mechanism of action of fentanyl?

A: μ-opioid receptor agonist.

Q1530: What are common adverse effects of fentanyl?

A: Respiratory depression, bradycardia, nausea, constipation, and chest wall rigidity with rapid high-dose administration.

Q1531: Which opioid is commonly used for postoperative pain control?

A: Morphine.

Q1532: What are common adverse effects of morphine?

A: Respiratory depression, constipation, urinary retention, pruritus, nausea, vomiting, and histamine-mediated hypotension.

Succinylcholine

Q1533: What type of neuromuscular blocker is succinylcholine?

A: Depolarizing neuromuscular blocker.

Q1534: What is the mechanism of action of succinylcholine?

A: Persistent depolarization of the neuromuscular junction by acting as an acetylcholine receptor agonist.

Q1535: What is the onset and duration of succinylcholine?

A: Rapid onset (30–60 seconds) with short duration (approximately 5–10 minutes).

Q1536: Why is succinylcholine commonly used during RSI?

A: It provides rapid paralysis with a short duration.

Q1537: Which electrolyte abnormality can succinylcholine cause?

A: Hyperkalemia.

Q1538: In which patients is succinylcholine contraindicated because of severe hyperkalemia risk?

A: Major burns (>24–48 hours after injury), crush injuries, prolonged immobilization, denervation injuries, neuromuscular diseases, and known hyperkalemia.

Q1539: Which inherited enzyme deficiency prolongs succinylcholine paralysis?

A: Pseudocholinesterase (butyrylcholinesterase) deficiency.

Nondepolarizing Neuromuscular Blockers

Q1540: Name two commonly used nondepolarizing neuromuscular blockers.

A: Rocuronium and vecuronium.

Q1541: What is the mechanism of action of nondepolarizing neuromuscular blockers?

A: Competitive antagonism of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction.

Q1542: Which nondepolarizing neuromuscular blocker is commonly used as an alternative to succinylcholine for RSI?

A: Rocuronium.

Q1543: Which neuromuscular blocker undergoes Hofmann elimination and is useful in severe renal or hepatic dysfunction?

A: Cisatracurium.

Reversal Agents

Q1544: Which medication rapidly reverses rocuronium and vecuronium?

A: Sugammadex.

Q1545: How does sugammadex work?

A: It encapsulates steroidal neuromuscular blockers (especially rocuronium and vecuronium), rendering them inactive.

Q1546: Which medication reverses many nondepolarizing neuromuscular blockers by increasing acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction?

A: Neostigmine.

Q1547: Why is glycopyrrolate or atropine commonly administered with neostigmine?

A: To reduce muscarinic side effects such as bradycardia and excessive secretions.

Drug Reversal

Q1548: Which medication reverses opioid-induced respiratory depression?

A: Naloxone.

Q1549: Which medication reverses benzodiazepine-induced sedation?

A: Flumazenil.

Q1550: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK anesthesia pharmacology principle?

A: Choose the induction agent based on the patient's physiology: propofol for routine induction (but avoid in severe hypotension), etomidate for hemodynamic instability, ketamine for hypotension or bronchospasm. Remember succinylcholine causes hyperkalemia and is contraindicated in burns, denervation, and neuromuscular disease; rocuronium is a common alternative. Know the reversal agents: naloxone (opioids), flumazenil (benzodiazepines), sugammadex (rocuronium/vecuronium), and neostigmine (nondepolarizing neuromuscular blockers).

Q1551: What is the primary role of inhalational anesthetics?

A: Maintenance of general anesthesia after induction.

Q1552: Which inhalational anesthetics are commonly used in modern practice?

A: Sevoflurane, desflurane, isoflurane, and nitrous oxide.

Q1553: What does MAC (Minimum Alveolar Concentration) represent?

A: The alveolar concentration of an inhaled anesthetic that prevents movement in response to a surgical stimulus in 50% of patients.

Q1554: How does MAC relate to anesthetic potency?

A: Lower MAC = greater anesthetic potency.

Q1555: Which inhalational anesthetic has the fastest induction and emergence?

A: Desflurane.

Q1556: Which inhalational anesthetic is commonly preferred for inhalational induction, especially in children?

A: Sevoflurane.

Q1557: Why is sevoflurane preferred over desflurane for mask induction?

A: It is less irritating to the airway.

Q1558: Which inhalational anesthetic may irritate the airway and provoke coughing or laryngospasm?

A: Desflurane.

Q1559: Which inhalational anesthetic has significant analgesic properties?

A: Nitrous oxide.

Q1560: Why is nitrous oxide rarely used as the sole anesthetic?

A: It is a weak anesthetic and cannot reliably produce surgical anesthesia alone.

Q1561: Why should nitrous oxide be avoided in patients with pneumothorax or bowel obstruction?

A: It diffuses into air-filled spaces and expands trapped gas.

Q1562: What is diffusion hypoxia?

A: Rapid diffusion of nitrous oxide from blood into alveoli after discontinuation, transiently diluting alveolar oxygen.

Q1563: How is diffusion hypoxia prevented?

A: Administer 100% oxygen for several minutes after discontinuing nitrous oxide.

Local Anesthetics

Q1564: What is the mechanism of action of local anesthetics?

A: Reversible blockade of voltage-gated sodium channels, preventing nerve impulse conduction.

Q1565: Which nerve fibers are blocked first by local anesthetics?

A: Small, myelinated pain fibers (Aδ) and unmyelinated C fibers.

Q1566: Name two commonly used local anesthetics.

A: Lidocaine and bupivacaine.

Q1567: Which local anesthetic has the fastest onset?

A: Lidocaine.

Q1568: Which local anesthetic has the longest duration?

A: Bupivacaine.

Q1569: Why is epinephrine sometimes added to local anesthetics?

A: It causes vasoconstriction, prolonging anesthetic duration and reducing systemic absorption.

Q1570: In which body areas should epinephrine-containing local anesthetics generally be avoided?

A: Areas with end-arterial blood supply (e.g., digits, penis, nose, and ears), although practice is evolving and may vary by clinical context.

Local Anesthetic Systemic Toxicity (LAST)

Q1571: What causes Local Anesthetic Systemic Toxicity (LAST)?

A: Accidental intravascular injection or excessive systemic absorption of a local anesthetic.

Q1572: What are the earliest neurologic symptoms of LAST?

A: Perioral numbness, tinnitus, metallic taste, dizziness, agitation, and circumoral paresthesias.

Q1573: What severe neurologic complication may occur in LAST?

A: Generalized seizures.

Q1574: What cardiovascular complications occur in severe LAST?

A: Hypotension, ventricular arrhythmias, cardiac depression, and cardiac arrest.

Q1575: Which local anesthetic carries the greatest risk of severe cardiotoxicity?

A: Bupivacaine.

Q1576: What is the definitive treatment for severe LAST?

A: Intravenous lipid emulsion therapy plus standard advanced resuscitation.

Q1577: How does lipid emulsion therapy work?

A: It acts as a "lipid sink," reducing the free concentration of lipophilic local anesthetic in target tissues.

Malignant Hyperthermia (MH)

Q1578: What is malignant hyperthermia?

A: A life-threatening pharmacogenetic disorder causing uncontrolled skeletal muscle calcium release after exposure to certain anesthetic agents.

Q1579: Which receptor is abnormal in most cases of malignant hyperthermia?

A: Ryanodine receptor (RYR1).

Q1580: Which anesthetic agents trigger malignant hyperthermia?

A: Volatile inhalational anesthetics (e.g., sevoflurane, desflurane, isoflurane) and succinylcholine.

Q1581: Which commonly used anesthetic agents do not trigger malignant hyperthermia?

A: Propofol, ketamine, etomidate, opioids, benzodiazepines, dexmedetomidine, nitrous oxide, and nondepolarizing neuromuscular blockers.

Q1582: What is usually the earliest sign of malignant hyperthermia?

A: Rapid rise in end-tidal CO₂ (hypercapnia) despite increased ventilation.

Q1583: What is the classic muscle finding in malignant hyperthermia?

A: Generalized muscle rigidity, often including masseter muscle rigidity.

Q1584: Is hyperthermia the earliest manifestation of malignant hyperthermia?

A: No. Hypercapnia and muscle rigidity usually occur first.

Q1585: What metabolic abnormalities are common in malignant hyperthermia?

A: Hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, elevated CK, myoglobinuria, and hyperthermia.

Q1586: Which medication is the definitive treatment for malignant hyperthermia?

A: Dantrolene.

Q1587: How does dantrolene work?

A: It inhibits calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum by blocking the ryanodine receptor.

Q1588: Besides dantrolene, what supportive measures are essential?

A: Discontinue triggering agents, provide 100% oxygen, active cooling, correct hyperkalemia and acidosis, treat arrhythmias, and monitor for renal injury.

Q1589: Why is CK markedly elevated in malignant hyperthermia?

A: Massive skeletal muscle breakdown (rhabdomyolysis).

Q1590: Which inherited muscle disease is strongly associated with malignant hyperthermia susceptibility?

A: Central core disease.

Post-Anesthesia Care

Q1591: What is the most common cause of hypoxemia immediately after surgery?

A: Upper airway obstruction or hypoventilation due to residual anesthetic or opioid effects.

Q1592: What is the first step in managing postoperative hypoxemia?

A: Ensure airway patency and administer supplemental oxygen while assessing ventilation.

Q1593: Which patients are at highest risk for postoperative respiratory depression?

A: Patients receiving opioids, those with OSA, obesity, advanced age, or significant pulmonary disease.

Q1594: What is the most common cause of delayed emergence from anesthesia?

A: Residual anesthetic or sedative medications.

Q1595: What should always be considered in a patient who fails to awaken appropriately after surgery?

A: Residual drugs, hypoglycemia, electrolyte disturbances, hypoxia, hypercapnia, stroke, and other neurologic causes.

Postoperative Nausea and Vomiting (PONV)

Q1596: Which patients are at highest risk for postoperative nausea and vomiting?

A: Female sex, nonsmoking status, prior history of PONV or motion sickness, and postoperative opioid use.

Q1597: Which antiemetic is commonly used for PONV prophylaxis?

A: Ondansetron (5-HT₃ receptor antagonist).

High-Yield Review

Q1598: What is the antidote for malignant hyperthermia?

A: Dantrolene.

Q1599: What is the antidote for Local Anesthetic Systemic Toxicity (LAST)?

A: Intravenous lipid emulsion.

Q1600: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK anesthesia emergency principle?

A: Think malignant hyperthermia in any patient who develops rapidly rising end-tidal CO₂, muscle rigidity, hyperkalemia, and hyperthermia after exposure to volatile anesthetics or succinylcholine—stop triggering agents immediately and administer dantrolene. Think LAST when neurologic symptoms (e.g., tinnitus, perioral numbness, seizures) or cardiovascular collapse follows local anesthetic administration—treat promptly with intravenous lipid emulsion.

Topic: Surgical Emergencies

Q1601: What is the first priority in any patient with a suspected surgical emergency?

A: Assess and stabilize Airway, Breathing, and Circulation (ABCs) before pursuing diagnostic testing.

Q1602: What are the first three simultaneous interventions in an unstable patient with an acute abdomen?

A: Large-bore IV access, isotonic IV fluids, and continuous monitoring (plus supplemental oxygen as needed).

Q1603: Which laboratory studies are commonly obtained in patients with an acute surgical abdomen?

A: CBC, CMP, coagulation profile (if indicated), serum lactate, lipase (if pancreatitis suspected), urinalysis, β-hCG in women of reproductive age, and type & screen/crossmatch if significant bleeding is possible.

Q1604: Why is serum lactate measured in suspected surgical emergencies?

A: It may indicate tissue hypoperfusion or ischemia, although a normal lactate does not exclude early mesenteric ischemia.

Q1605: Which imaging study is preferred for most stable adults with an undifferentiated acute abdomen?

A: CT abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast (when appropriate and not contraindicated).

Q1606: Which imaging modality is preferred first for suspected biliary disease?

A: Right upper quadrant ultrasound.

Q1607: Which imaging modality is preferred first for unstable trauma patients?

A: FAST (Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma).

Q1608: When should imaging not delay surgery?

A: When there is clear evidence of a life-threatening surgical emergency (e.g., diffuse peritonitis, ruptured AAA with instability, tension pneumothorax).

Acute Abdomen

Q1609: What is meant by an "acute abdomen"?

A: Sudden severe abdominal pain that may require urgent surgical intervention.

Q1610: Which physical examination finding most strongly suggests peritoneal irritation?

A: Involuntary guarding (rigidity).

Q1611: What is rebound tenderness?

A: Increased pain when pressure is suddenly released from the abdomen, suggesting peritoneal inflammation.

Q1612: Which finding is more specific for peritonitis: voluntary guarding or involuntary guarding?

A: Involuntary guarding.

Q1613: Which patients may have minimal abdominal findings despite severe intra-abdominal disease?

A: Elderly patients, immunocompromised patients, and those receiving corticosteroids.

Peritonitis

Q1614: What is peritonitis?

A: Inflammation of the peritoneal lining, usually due to infection or chemical irritation from gastrointestinal contents.

Q1615: What are the classic clinical features of generalized peritonitis?

A: Severe abdominal pain, rigidity, rebound tenderness, fever, tachycardia, and absent or markedly decreased bowel sounds.

Q1616: Which bowel sound abnormality is commonly present in generalized peritonitis?

A: Hypoactive or absent bowel sounds due to paralytic ileus.

Q1617: Why do patients with peritonitis often lie still?

A: Movement worsens pain because of peritoneal irritation.

Q1618: What are the major causes of secondary peritonitis?

A: Perforated peptic ulcer, perforated diverticulitis, perforated appendicitis, bowel ischemia with perforation, traumatic bowel injury, and anastomotic leak.

Q1619: What are the four initial management priorities for generalized peritonitis?

A: IV fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, analgesia, and urgent surgical consultation.

Q1620: What is the definitive treatment for most cases of secondary peritonitis?

A: Source control, usually through surgery or image-guided drainage when appropriate.

Hollow Viscus Perforation

Q1621: What is meant by a hollow viscus perforation?

A: A full-thickness perforation of a gastrointestinal organ, allowing air and/or contents to enter the peritoneal cavity.

Q1622: Which radiographic finding strongly suggests perforation of a hollow viscus?

A: Free intraperitoneal air (pneumoperitoneum).

Q1623: Which upright radiograph may demonstrate free intraperitoneal air?

A: Upright chest X-ray showing subdiaphragmatic free air.

Q1624: Which CT finding is most sensitive for detecting bowel perforation?

A: Extraluminal free air adjacent to the gastrointestinal tract.

Q1625: What are common causes of hollow viscus perforation?

A: Perforated peptic ulcer, diverticulitis, ischemic bowel, colorectal cancer, trauma, and inflammatory bowel disease.

Q1626: Which antibiotics are appropriate empirically for suspected bowel perforation?

A: Broad-spectrum agents covering gram-negative organisms and anaerobes (e.g., piperacillin-tazobactam or cefepime plus metronidazole, depending on local practice).

Q1627: Is a normal white blood cell count sufficient to exclude bowel perforation?

A: No.

Sepsis from Intra-abdominal Infection

Q1628: Which patients with an acute abdomen require early sepsis recognition and treatment?

A: Patients with hypotension, fever, tachycardia, altered mental status, elevated lactate, or evidence of organ dysfunction.

Q1629: Besides antibiotics, what intervention is critical for survival in intra-abdominal sepsis?

A: Early source control.

Q1630: Why are antibiotics alone usually insufficient in perforated bowel?

A: Because infected material remains in the abdomen until source control is achieved.

Analgesia

Q1631: Should opioid analgesia be withheld until the diagnosis is established?

A: No. Adequate pain control should be provided while evaluation proceeds.

Q1632: Does opioid administration significantly reduce diagnostic accuracy for acute abdominal pain?

A: No. Appropriate analgesia does not preclude accurate diagnosis.

Nasogastric Tube

Q1633: When is nasogastric tube decompression commonly indicated?

A: Significant vomiting, bowel obstruction, gastric distention, or aspiration risk.

Q1634: Is routine nasogastric tube placement required in every patient with abdominal pain?

A: No.

Urinary Catheter

Q1635: Why is a Foley catheter commonly placed in critically ill surgical patients?

A: To monitor urine output as a marker of renal perfusion and resuscitation.

Operative Decision-Making

Q1636: Which findings strongly favor immediate surgical exploration?

A: Diffuse peritonitis, hemodynamic instability due to intra-abdominal pathology, bowel perforation, strangulated bowel, or ischemic bowel.

Q1637: Can localized peritonitis sometimes be managed initially without surgery?

A: Yes, in selected cases (e.g., contained perforation or localized diverticular abscess) depending on clinical stability and imaging findings.

Imaging Pearls

Q1638: Which imaging modality best detects free intraperitoneal air?

A: CT scan.

Q1639: Which imaging modality is often the fastest initial study when perforation is suspected?

A: Upright chest X-ray, although CT is more sensitive.

Q1640: Which imaging study should generally be avoided in unstable patients requiring immediate surgery?

A: Time-consuming CT imaging that delays definitive management.

Differential Diagnosis

Q1641: Which vascular emergency should always be considered in an older patient with sudden abdominal pain and hypotension?

A: Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm.

Q1642: Which diagnosis should be suspected when abdominal pain is severe but physical findings are initially mild?

A: Acute mesenteric ischemia.

Q1643: Which diagnosis should be considered in women of reproductive age with acute abdominal pain?

A: Ectopic pregnancy until excluded.

High-Yield NBME Pearls

Q1644: What is the most important principle in evaluating the acute abdomen?

A: Stabilize first; diagnose while resuscitating.

Q1645: What is the most common error tested on USMLE surgical emergency questions?

A: Delaying surgery for additional imaging in a patient with clear indications for operative intervention.

Q1646: Which finding is more concerning than the severity of abdominal pain itself?

A: Hemodynamic instability or signs of generalized peritonitis.

Q1647: What is the "red flag" vital sign that may be the earliest indicator of serious intra-abdominal pathology?

A: Persistent unexplained tachycardia.

Q1648: What is the cornerstone of treatment for intra-abdominal infection?

A: Source control plus appropriate antibiotics.

Q1649: Which patients require urgent surgical consultation regardless of imaging results?

A: Patients with diffuse peritonitis, perforation, strangulated bowel, ischemic bowel, or persistent hemodynamic instability from suspected surgical disease.

Q1650: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for acute surgical emergencies?

A: Recognize peritonitis early, begin aggressive resuscitation immediately, administer broad-spectrum antibiotics when infection is suspected, and never delay definitive source control or operative management for unnecessary diagnostic testing.

Q1651: What is a small bowel obstruction (SBO)?

A: Mechanical or functional interruption of the normal passage of intestinal contents through the small intestine.

Q1652: What is the most common cause of SBO in developed countries?

A: Postoperative adhesions (≈60–75% of cases).

Q1653: What are other common causes of SBO?

A: Hernias, Crohn disease, tumors, volvulus, intussusception, gallstone ileus, and radiation strictures.

Q1654: What are the classic symptoms of SBO?

A: Colicky abdominal pain, vomiting, abdominal distension, and obstipation (failure to pass stool or flatus).

Q1655: Which symptom tends to occur earliest in proximal SBO?

A: Vomiting.

Q1656: Which symptom is more prominent in distal SBO?

A: Marked abdominal distension.

Q1657: What bowel sounds are typically heard early in SBO?

A: High-pitched, hyperactive ("tinkling") bowel sounds.

Q1658: What happens to bowel sounds as SBO progresses with ischemia or perforation?

A: They become hypoactive or absent.

Q1659: What laboratory abnormalities may occur in SBO?

A: Hemoconcentration, hypokalemia, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis (from vomiting), leukocytosis, and elevated lactate if ischemia develops.

Q1660: What is the preferred imaging study for stable adults with suspected SBO?

A: CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast.

Q1661: What CT findings are characteristic of SBO?

A: Dilated proximal small bowel (>3 cm), collapsed distal bowel, air-fluid levels, and a transition point.

Q1662: What is the "transition point" on CT?

A: The site where dilated bowel abruptly changes to collapsed bowel, indicating the level of obstruction.

Q1663: What is the initial treatment of uncomplicated adhesive SBO?

A: NPO, IV fluids, electrolyte correction, nasogastric tube decompression (if indicated), analgesia, and close observation.

Q1664: Which patients with SBO require immediate surgery?

A: Patients with strangulation, ischemia, perforation, closed-loop obstruction, or generalized peritonitis.

Closed-Loop Obstruction

Q1665: What is a closed-loop obstruction?

A: A bowel segment obstructed at two points, creating a closed loop with a high risk of ischemia and perforation.

Q1666: Why is a closed-loop obstruction a surgical emergency?

A: Rapid progression to bowel ischemia, necrosis, and perforation.

Strangulated Bowel

Q1667: What is strangulated bowel?

A: Obstruction with compromised blood supply leading to ischemia.

Q1668: Which symptoms suggest strangulation rather than uncomplicated SBO?

A: Continuous severe pain (rather than colicky pain), fever, tachycardia, leukocytosis, metabolic acidosis, and peritoneal signs.

Q1669: Which CT findings suggest bowel ischemia?

A: Bowel wall thickening, decreased enhancement, pneumatosis intestinalis, portal venous gas, mesenteric edema, and free fluid.

Q1670: Which laboratory finding raises concern for bowel ischemia?

A: Elevated serum lactate (though it may be normal early).

Large Bowel Obstruction (LBO)

Q1671: What is the most common cause of LBO in adults?

A: Colorectal carcinoma.

Q1672: What are other important causes of LBO?

A: Volvulus, diverticular stricture, inflammatory bowel disease, and fecal impaction.

Q1673: What symptoms characterize LBO?

A: Progressive abdominal distension, constipation, obstipation, abdominal pain, and later vomiting.

Q1674: What imaging study is preferred in stable suspected LBO?

A: CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast.

Q1675: Which colonic diameter is particularly concerning for perforation risk?

A: Cecal diameter >12 cm.

Volvulus

Q1676: What is volvulus?

A: Twisting of the bowel around its mesentery causing obstruction and possible vascular compromise.

Q1677: Which two locations account for most colonic volvulus?

A: Sigmoid colon and cecum.

Sigmoid Volvulus

Q1678: Which patients are at greatest risk for sigmoid volvulus?

A: Elderly, institutionalized, chronically constipated, or neurologically impaired patients.

Q1679: What is the classic abdominal X-ray finding in sigmoid volvulus?

A: Coffee-bean sign.

Q1680: How is uncomplicated sigmoid volvulus initially treated?

A: Endoscopic detorsion with flexible sigmoidoscopy, followed by elective sigmoid resection because recurrence is common.

Q1681: When is surgery required for sigmoid volvulus?

A: If there is perforation, ischemia, peritonitis, or failed endoscopic decompression.

Cecal Volvulus

Q1682: How is cecal volvulus usually managed?

A: Surgical resection or fixation; endoscopic decompression is generally ineffective.

Q1683: Why is colonoscopic decompression not routinely recommended for cecal volvulus?

A: It has a low success rate and a high recurrence rate.

Hernias

Q1684: What is an incarcerated hernia?

A: A hernia that cannot be reduced back into the abdominal cavity.

Q1685: What is a strangulated hernia?

A: An incarcerated hernia with compromised blood supply.

Q1686: Which hernia has the highest risk of strangulation?

A: Femoral hernia.

Q1687: Which findings suggest a strangulated hernia?

A: Severe pain, erythema, tenderness, skin discoloration, fever, leukocytosis, bowel obstruction, or peritonitis.

Q1688: What is the treatment for a strangulated hernia?

A: Emergency surgery.

Acute Mesenteric Ischemia

Q1689: What is acute mesenteric ischemia?

A: Sudden interruption of intestinal blood flow causing bowel ischemia and infarction.

Q1690: What are the four major causes of acute mesenteric ischemia?

A: Arterial embolism, arterial thrombosis, mesenteric venous thrombosis, and nonocclusive mesenteric ischemia.

Q1691: Which is the most common source of arterial emboli causing mesenteric ischemia?

A: Atrial fibrillation with cardiac embolization to the superior mesenteric artery.

Q1692: What is the classic symptom of acute mesenteric ischemia?

A: Severe abdominal pain out of proportion to physical examination.

Q1693: Why may the abdominal examination initially appear relatively benign in mesenteric ischemia?

A: Ischemia precedes infarction and peritoneal irritation.

Q1694: Which imaging study is preferred in a stable patient with suspected mesenteric ischemia?

A: CT angiography.

Q1695: Which laboratory findings support the diagnosis of mesenteric ischemia?

A: Elevated lactate, metabolic acidosis, leukocytosis, and elevated D-dimer (nonspecific).

Q1696: Which finding indicates bowel infarction requiring urgent surgery?

A: Peritonitis.

Q1697: What are the initial management priorities for acute mesenteric ischemia?

A: Aggressive IV fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, anticoagulation (when appropriate), urgent vascular/surgical consultation, and revascularization if feasible.

Q1698: Why should broad-spectrum antibiotics be started early in mesenteric ischemia?

A: Ischemic bowel loses mucosal integrity, allowing bacterial translocation and increasing the risk of sepsis.

High-Yield Review

Q1699: What are the most important clinical clues that bowel obstruction has progressed to ischemia?

A: Continuous pain, fever, tachycardia, leukocytosis, metabolic acidosis, elevated lactate, peritoneal signs, and CT evidence of poor bowel perfusion.

Q1700: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for bowel obstruction?

A: Simple adhesive SBO is usually managed initially with bowel rest, IV fluids, and decompression, but any evidence of strangulation, ischemia, closed-loop obstruction, perforation, or generalized peritonitis mandates urgent operative intervention. In acute mesenteric ischemia, think "pain out of proportion," confirm with CT angiography in stable patients, and never delay definitive treatment once bowel infarction is suspected.

Q1701: What is upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB)?

A: Bleeding originating proximal to the ligament of Treitz, including the esophagus, stomach, or duodenum.

Q1702: What are the most common causes of UGIB?

A: Peptic ulcer disease, esophageal varices, erosive gastritis/esophagitis, Mallory-Weiss tear, and less commonly Dieulafoy lesion or malignancy.

Q1703: What are the classic presentations of UGIB?

A: Hematemesis, coffee-ground emesis, melena, and, in severe cases, hematochezia with hemodynamic instability.

Q1704: Which vital sign abnormality may be the earliest clue to significant blood loss?

A: Persistent tachycardia.

Q1705: Which laboratory tests should be obtained in suspected UGIB?

A: CBC, BMP/CMP, coagulation studies, type & screen (or crossmatch), and liver function tests if liver disease is suspected.

Initial Management

Q1706: What is the first priority in a patient with massive UGIB?

A: ABCs with airway protection if indicated, followed by hemodynamic stabilization.

Q1707: When should endotracheal intubation be considered in UGIB?

A: Massive hematemesis, inability to protect the airway, altered mental status, or impending respiratory failure.

Q1708: What is the initial fluid therapy for unstable UGIB?

A: Isotonic crystalloid while preparing blood products if needed.

Q1709: When should packed red blood cells (PRBCs) be transfused in most stable adults with UGIB?

A: A restrictive strategy is generally preferred, with transfusion at hemoglobin <7 g/dL, unless clinical circumstances (e.g., active ischemia, massive ongoing hemorrhage) warrant earlier transfusion.

Q1710: Why is a restrictive transfusion strategy often preferred?

A: It is associated with similar or improved outcomes compared with liberal transfusion in most patients.

Endoscopy

Q1711: What is the definitive diagnostic test for most cases of UGIB?

A: Upper endoscopy (esophagogastroduodenoscopy, EGD).

Q1712: When should EGD generally be performed after stabilization?

A: Within 24 hours, and sooner in high-risk or actively bleeding patients.

Q1713: Besides diagnosis, what is the role of EGD?

A: Therapeutic hemostasis using injection, clips, thermal coagulation, or band ligation, depending on the lesion.

Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)

Q1714: What is the most common cause of nonvariceal UGIB?

A: Peptic ulcer disease.

Q1715: What are the two major causes of peptic ulcer disease?

A: Helicobacter pylori infection and NSAID use.

Q1716: Which medication should be started in suspected bleeding peptic ulcer?

A: High-dose intravenous proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy.

Q1717: What is the definitive endoscopic treatment for a bleeding peptic ulcer?

A: Endoscopic hemostasis (e.g., clips, thermal therapy, or injection therapy).

Q1718: When is surgery indicated for peptic ulcer bleeding?

A: Failure of endoscopic and/or angiographic therapy, recurrent life-threatening bleeding, or perforation.

Perforated Peptic Ulcer

Q1719: What is the classic presentation of a perforated peptic ulcer?

A: Sudden severe epigastric pain with generalized peritonitis.

Q1720: Which imaging finding strongly supports perforated peptic ulcer?

A: Free intraperitoneal air beneath the diaphragm (pneumoperitoneum).

Q1721: What is the preferred imaging study if the diagnosis is uncertain in a stable patient?

A: CT abdomen with IV contrast.

Q1722: What are the initial management steps for perforated peptic ulcer?

A: NPO, IV fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, IV PPI, analgesia, and urgent surgical consultation.

Q1723: What is the definitive treatment for generalized perforated peptic ulcer?

A: Surgical repair with source control.

Esophageal Variceal Hemorrhage

Q1724: What causes esophageal varices?

A: Portal hypertension, usually due to cirrhosis.

Q1725: What is the typical presentation of variceal hemorrhage?

A: Massive painless hematemesis in a patient with chronic liver disease.

Q1726: Which vasoactive medication should be started immediately when variceal bleeding is suspected?

A: Octreotide.

Q1727: Why are prophylactic antibiotics recommended in variceal hemorrhage?

A: They reduce infection, rebleeding, and mortality in patients with cirrhosis.

Q1728: Which antibiotic is commonly used for prophylaxis in cirrhotic patients with variceal bleeding?

A: Ceftriaxone (choice may vary based on local resistance patterns).

Q1729: What is the definitive endoscopic treatment for bleeding esophageal varices?

A: Endoscopic band ligation.

Q1730: What rescue procedure is considered for uncontrolled variceal bleeding despite medications and endoscopy?

A: TIPS (Transjugular Intrahepatic Portosystemic Shunt).

Mallory-Weiss Tear

Q1731: What is a Mallory-Weiss tear?

A: A longitudinal mucosal laceration at the gastroesophageal junction.

Q1732: What usually precipitates a Mallory-Weiss tear?

A: Repeated forceful vomiting or retching.

Q1733: How does Mallory-Weiss syndrome typically present?

A: Hematemesis following episodes of vomiting.

Q1734: What is the treatment for most Mallory-Weiss tears?

A: Supportive care; endoscopic therapy if bleeding persists.

Boerhaave Syndrome

Q1735: What is Boerhaave syndrome?

A: Full-thickness spontaneous esophageal rupture, usually after forceful vomiting.

Q1736: What is the classic presentation of Boerhaave syndrome?

A: Severe chest pain after vomiting, often with subcutaneous emphysema and signs of sepsis.

Q1737: What imaging study is preferred in a stable patient with suspected Boerhaave syndrome?

A: CT chest with water-soluble oral contrast (or contrast esophagography, depending on availability and clinical scenario).

Q1738: What is the definitive treatment for Boerhaave syndrome?

A: Urgent surgical (or selected endoscopic) management plus broad-spectrum antibiotics.

Dieulafoy Lesion

Q1739: What is a Dieulafoy lesion?

A: A large-caliber submucosal artery that erodes through a small mucosal defect, causing significant bleeding.

Q1740: How is a Dieulafoy lesion treated?

A: Endoscopic hemostasis (clips, coagulation, or injection therapy).

Aortoenteric Fistula

Q1741: What is an aortoenteric fistula?

A: An abnormal communication between the aorta and the gastrointestinal tract, often after prior aortic graft surgery.

Q1742: What is the classic warning sign of an aortoenteric fistula?

A: A sentinel GI bleed followed by catastrophic hemorrhage.

Q1743: What should be suspected in a patient with prior abdominal aortic graft repair and GI bleeding?

A: Aortoenteric fistula until proven otherwise.

Q1744: What is the definitive treatment for aortoenteric fistula?

A: Emergency vascular surgery.

Gastric Emergencies

Q1745: What is gastric volvulus?

A: Abnormal twisting of the stomach causing obstruction and possible ischemia.

Q1746: What is Borchardt's triad in acute gastric volvulus?

A: Severe epigastric pain, retching without productive vomiting, and inability to pass a nasogastric tube.

Q1747: Why is gastric volvulus a surgical emergency?

A: It can rapidly progress to gastric ischemia, necrosis, and perforation.

Q1748: What is gastric outlet obstruction?

A: Mechanical obstruction of gastric emptying, most commonly due to malignancy in adults or chronic peptic ulcer-related scarring.

High-Yield Review

Q1749: Which upper GI emergencies require immediate operative or procedural intervention?

A: Perforated peptic ulcer, uncontrolled variceal hemorrhage, Boerhaave syndrome, aortoenteric fistula, gastric volvulus with ischemia, and persistent massive UGIB despite endoscopic therapy.

Q1750: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for upper GI emergencies?

A: Always stabilize the patient first (ABCs and hemodynamics). Treat suspected variceal bleeding immediately with octreotide and prophylactic antibiotics before endoscopy. Start IV PPIs for suspected peptic ulcer bleeding. Sudden severe abdominal pain with free intraperitoneal air indicates perforated peptic ulcer requiring urgent surgery. Chest pain after forceful vomiting suggests Boerhaave syndrome, which requires urgent recognition and definitive management.

Q1751: What is the most common cause of acute cholecystitis?

A: Persistent obstruction of the cystic duct by a gallstone, leading to inflammation and possible secondary bacterial infection.

Q1752: What are the classic symptoms of acute cholecystitis?

A: Constant right upper quadrant (RUQ) pain, fever, nausea/vomiting, and pain lasting >6 hours.

Q1753: What physical examination finding is classically associated with acute cholecystitis?

A: Positive Murphy sign (arrest of inspiration during RUQ palpation).

Q1754: Which laboratory abnormalities are commonly seen in acute cholecystitis?

A: Leukocytosis with mild elevations in bilirubin and liver enzymes; marked cholestatic abnormalities suggest possible choledocholithiasis.

Q1755: What is the preferred initial imaging study for suspected acute cholecystitis?

A: Right upper quadrant ultrasound.

Q1756: What ultrasound findings support the diagnosis of acute cholecystitis?

A: Gallstones, gallbladder wall thickening (>3 mm), pericholecystic fluid, sonographic Murphy sign, and gallbladder distention.

Q1757: If ultrasound is nondiagnostic but suspicion remains high, what is the next imaging study?

A: HIDA (hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid) scan.

Q1758: What HIDA scan finding confirms acute cholecystitis?

A: Nonvisualization of the gallbladder due to cystic duct obstruction.

Q1759: What are the initial management steps for acute cholecystitis?

A: NPO, IV fluids, analgesia, antiemetics, antibiotics when indicated, and early surgical consultation.

Q1760: What is the definitive treatment for acute calculous cholecystitis?

A: Early laparoscopic cholecystectomy, ideally during the index hospitalization.

Emphysematous Cholecystitis

Q1761: What is emphysematous cholecystitis?

A: A severe form of cholecystitis caused by gas-forming organisms within the gallbladder wall or lumen.

Q1762: Which patients are at highest risk for emphysematous cholecystitis?

A: Elderly patients, especially men with diabetes mellitus.

Q1763: Why is emphysematous cholecystitis considered a surgical emergency?

A: It carries a high risk of gangrene, perforation, and sepsis.

Ascending Cholangitis

Q1764: What is ascending cholangitis?

A: Infection of the biliary tree caused by biliary obstruction, most commonly from a common bile duct stone.

Q1765: What is Charcot triad?

A: Fever, RUQ pain, and jaundice.

Q1766: What is Reynolds pentad?

A: Charcot triad plus hypotension and altered mental status, indicating severe cholangitis with sepsis.

Q1767: Which laboratory pattern is typical of ascending cholangitis?

A: Leukocytosis with a cholestatic liver enzyme pattern (elevated alkaline phosphatase, GGT, and direct bilirubin).

Q1768: What is the preferred initial imaging study for suspected cholangitis?

A: Right upper quadrant ultrasound to evaluate for biliary dilation and gallstones.

Q1769: What are the initial management priorities in ascending cholangitis?

A: IV fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, blood cultures, and urgent biliary decompression.

Q1770: What is the definitive treatment for ascending cholangitis?

A: Urgent ERCP with biliary drainage and stone extraction (when feasible).

Q1771: When is emergency surgery considered in ascending cholangitis?

A: If ERCP is unsuccessful or unavailable and biliary decompression is still required.

Acute Pancreatitis

Q1772: What are the two most common causes of acute pancreatitis?

A: Gallstones and alcohol.

Q1773: What are other important causes of acute pancreatitis?

A: Hypertriglyceridemia, medications, ERCP, hypercalcemia, trauma, infections, and pancreatic tumors.

Q1774: What is the classic presentation of acute pancreatitis?

A: Sudden severe epigastric pain radiating to the back with nausea and vomiting.

Q1775: Which laboratory test is most useful for diagnosing acute pancreatitis?

A: Serum lipase (more sensitive and specific than amylase).

Q1776: What are the diagnostic criteria for acute pancreatitis?

A: At least 2 of 3:

Typical abdominal pain
Lipase or amylase ≥3× the upper limit of normal
Imaging findings consistent with pancreatitis

Q1777: What is the cornerstone of initial treatment for acute pancreatitis?

A: Aggressive IV crystalloid fluid resuscitation, adequate analgesia, and early enteral nutrition when tolerated.

Q1778: Should prophylactic antibiotics be routinely used in uncomplicated acute pancreatitis?

A: No.

Gallstone Pancreatitis

Q1779: What mechanism causes gallstone pancreatitis?

A: Transient obstruction of the ampulla of Vater by a gallstone, leading to pancreatic enzyme activation.

Q1780: Which laboratory findings suggest gallstone pancreatitis?

A: Elevated lipase with elevated bilirubin and/or ALT (especially markedly elevated ALT).

Q1781: When is urgent ERCP indicated in gallstone pancreatitis?

A: If there is concurrent ascending cholangitis or persistent biliary obstruction.

Q1782: When should cholecystectomy be performed after mild gallstone pancreatitis?

A: During the same hospitalization, after clinical improvement.

Necrotizing Pancreatitis

Q1783: What is necrotizing pancreatitis?

A: Pancreatic inflammation with nonviable pancreatic and/or peripancreatic tissue.

Q1784: Which imaging study best identifies pancreatic necrosis?

A: Contrast-enhanced CT scan, typically obtained if the diagnosis is uncertain or if the patient fails to improve after 48–72 hours.

Q1785: Which complication of necrotizing pancreatitis is associated with the highest mortality?

A: Infected pancreatic necrosis.

Q1786: How is infected pancreatic necrosis suspected?

A: Persistent fever, leukocytosis, sepsis, clinical deterioration, or gas within a necrotic collection on CT.

Q1787: What is the preferred management strategy for infected pancreatic necrosis?

A: Antibiotics plus delayed drainage/debridement using a step-up approach (percutaneous or endoscopic drainage first, when appropriate).

Pancreatic Pseudocyst

Q1788: What is a pancreatic pseudocyst?

A: A mature, encapsulated, enzyme-rich fluid collection without an epithelial lining, usually developing ≥4 weeks after pancreatitis.

Q1789: Which patients with pancreatic pseudocysts require intervention?

A: Those with symptoms (pain, gastric outlet obstruction, biliary obstruction), infection, bleeding, or progressive enlargement.

Q1790: Which asymptomatic pseudocysts can often be managed conservatively?

A: Stable, asymptomatic pseudocysts without complications.

Pancreatic Abscess

Q1791: What is a pancreatic abscess?

A: A localized collection of pus developing after pancreatitis, often due to infection of pancreatic necrosis or fluid collections.

Q1792: What are the usual treatment principles for a pancreatic abscess?

A: Broad-spectrum antibiotics plus drainage (percutaneous, endoscopic, or surgical, depending on the case).

Liver Abscess

Q1793: What are common causes of a pyogenic liver abscess?

A: Biliary infection, portal venous spread from intra-abdominal infection, hematogenous spread, or direct extension.

Q1794: What is the classic clinical presentation of a liver abscess?

A: Fever, RUQ pain, malaise, and leukocytosis.

Q1795: Which imaging study is most sensitive for diagnosing a liver abscess?

A: Contrast-enhanced CT scan (ultrasound is also commonly used initially).

Q1796: What is the preferred treatment for most pyogenic liver abscesses?

A: Broad-spectrum antibiotics plus percutaneous drainage when feasible.

Gallbladder Perforation

Q1797: Which complication of acute cholecystitis should be suspected in a patient who suddenly develops diffuse peritonitis?

A: Gallbladder perforation.

Q1798: What is the treatment for gallbladder perforation?

A: Emergency surgery with source control and broad-spectrum antibiotics.

High-Yield Review

Q1799: Which hepatobiliary emergency requires urgent ERCP rather than immediate surgery?

A: Ascending cholangitis with biliary obstruction.

Q1800: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for hepatobiliary and pancreatic emergencies?

A: Acute cholecystitis is treated with early laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Ascending cholangitis requires prompt antibiotics and urgent ERCP for biliary decompression. Acute pancreatitis is managed initially with aggressive IV fluids, analgesia, and early enteral nutrition—not routine antibiotics. Perform urgent ERCP in gallstone pancreatitis only when there is cholangitis or persistent biliary obstruction. Recognize infected pancreatic necrosis as a life-threatening complication requiring antibiotics and drainage.

Q1801: What is the most common cause of acute appendicitis?

A: Obstruction of the appendiceal lumen, usually by a fecalith or lymphoid hyperplasia.

Q1802: What is the classic progression of pain in acute appendicitis?

A: Initially periumbilical visceral pain that later localizes to the right lower quadrant (McBurney point).

Q1803: Why does appendicitis pain migrate from the periumbilical area to the right lower quadrant?

A: Early visceral inflammation causes poorly localized pain (T10 dermatome); later parietal peritoneal irritation localizes the pain.

Q1804: What are the classic symptoms of acute appendicitis?

A: Anorexia, nausea, vomiting (typically after pain begins), fever, and right lower quadrant pain.

Q1805: What is McBurney point?

A: A point located approximately one-third of the distance from the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) to the umbilicus on the right.

Q1806: What is Rovsing sign?

A: Right lower quadrant pain elicited by palpation of the left lower quadrant, suggesting peritoneal irritation.

Q1807: What is the psoas sign?

A: Right lower quadrant pain with passive extension of the right hip, suggesting a retrocecal appendix.

Q1808: What is the obturator sign?

A: Right lower quadrant pain with internal rotation of the flexed right hip, suggesting a pelvic appendix.

Q1809: What is the preferred imaging study for suspected appendicitis in nonpregnant adults?

A: CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast.

Q1810: What is the preferred initial imaging study for suspected appendicitis in children and pregnant patients?

A: Ultrasound (MRI if ultrasound is nondiagnostic in pregnancy).

Q1811: What are CT findings consistent with acute appendicitis?

A: Enlarged appendix (>6 mm), wall thickening, periappendiceal fat stranding, appendicolith, and possible periappendiceal fluid.

Q1812: What is the definitive treatment for uncomplicated appendicitis?

A: Laparoscopic appendectomy.

Q1813: What antibiotics should be given before appendectomy?

A: Broad-spectrum antibiotics covering gram-negative organisms and anaerobes.

Q1814: What complication should be suspected if appendicitis pain suddenly decreases followed by worsening diffuse abdominal pain?

A: Appendiceal perforation with generalized peritonitis.

Diverticulitis

Q1815: What is the most common location of diverticulitis?

A: Sigmoid colon.

Q1816: What are the classic symptoms of acute diverticulitis?

A: Left lower quadrant pain, fever, leukocytosis, and altered bowel habits.

Q1817: What is the preferred imaging study for suspected diverticulitis?

A: CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast.

Q1818: Which CT findings support diverticulitis?

A: Colonic wall thickening, pericolic fat stranding, diverticula, abscess, or perforation.

Q1819: How is uncomplicated diverticulitis usually managed?

A: Bowel rest (or modified diet), analgesia, selective antibiotic use depending on severity and patient factors, and outpatient management for appropriate stable patients.

Q1820: Which patients with diverticulitis require hospitalization?

A: Patients with sepsis, peritonitis, inability to tolerate oral intake, immunosuppression, significant comorbidities, or complicated disease.

Q1821: What complications of diverticulitis usually require intervention?

A: Abscess, fistula, obstruction, perforation, or generalized peritonitis.

Q1822: How are diverticular abscesses larger than approximately 3 cm commonly managed?

A: Percutaneous drainage plus antibiotics (when technically feasible).

Toxic Megacolon

Q1823: What is toxic megacolon?

A: Acute nonobstructive colonic dilation with systemic toxicity due to severe colitis.

Q1824: Which diseases most commonly cause toxic megacolon?

A: Ulcerative colitis and Clostridioides difficile colitis.

Q1825: What symptoms suggest toxic megacolon?

A: Severe abdominal pain, distension, fever, tachycardia, bloody diarrhea (often), and systemic toxicity.

Q1826: Which radiographic finding defines toxic megacolon?

A: Colonic dilation ≥6 cm, usually involving the transverse colon.

Q1827: What is the initial management of toxic megacolon?

A: Bowel rest, IV fluids, correction of electrolytes, appropriate antibiotics if infectious, close monitoring, and surgical consultation.

Q1828: When is emergency colectomy indicated in toxic megacolon?

A: Perforation, uncontrolled bleeding, worsening sepsis, peritonitis, or failure of medical therapy.

Fulminant Clostridioides difficile Colitis

Q1829: Which patients are at greatest risk for C. difficile colitis?

A: Recent antibiotic exposure, hospitalization, advanced age, and immunocompromised patients.

Q1830: What is the preferred treatment for fulminant C. difficile infection?

A: High-dose oral (or NG) vancomycin plus IV metronidazole; rectal vancomycin may be added if ileus is present.

Q1831: Which complication of fulminant C. difficile infection requires emergency surgery?

A: Toxic megacolon, perforation, or refractory septic shock.

Lower Gastrointestinal Bleeding

Q1832: What is lower gastrointestinal bleeding (LGIB)?

A: Bleeding originating distal to the ligament of Treitz, usually from the colon, rectum, or anus.

Q1833: What is the most common cause of significant LGIB in adults?

A: Diverticular bleeding.

Q1834: What is the initial priority in a patient with massive LGIB?

A: ABCs, hemodynamic stabilization, IV access, fluids, and blood products as indicated.

Q1835: What is the preferred diagnostic test for hemodynamically stable patients with LGIB after stabilization?

A: Colonoscopy following bowel preparation.

Q1836: Which imaging study is useful for ongoing brisk bleeding when colonoscopy is not feasible or nondiagnostic?

A: CT angiography.

Ischemic Colitis

Q1837: Which patients are most commonly affected by ischemic colitis?

A: Older adults with atherosclerotic disease or episodes of hypotension.

Q1838: What is the classic presentation of ischemic colitis?

A: Sudden crampy abdominal pain followed by bloody diarrhea or hematochezia.

Q1839: Which part of the colon is commonly involved in ischemic colitis?

A: Watershed areas, especially the splenic flexure and rectosigmoid junction.

Q1840: What is the initial treatment for uncomplicated ischemic colitis?

A: IV fluids, bowel rest, correction of underlying causes, and close observation; antibiotics are often used in moderate to severe cases.

Q1841: Which finding in ischemic colitis mandates urgent surgery?

A: Peritonitis, transmural necrosis, perforation, or persistent clinical deterioration.

Ogilvie Syndrome (Acute Colonic Pseudo-obstruction)

Q1842: What is Ogilvie syndrome?

A: Acute dilation of the colon without a mechanical obstruction.

Q1843: Which hospitalized patients are at highest risk for Ogilvie syndrome?

A: Elderly, postoperative, critically ill, or immobilized patients.

Q1844: What imaging finding suggests Ogilvie syndrome?

A: Marked colonic dilation without an obstructing lesion.

Q1845: What is the initial treatment for uncomplicated Ogilvie syndrome?

A: Bowel rest, correction of electrolytes, discontinuation of offending medications, mobilization, and decompression if needed.

Q1846: Which medication is used if conservative treatment fails and there is no contraindication?

A: Neostigmine (with cardiac monitoring).

Q1847: When is colonoscopic decompression considered in Ogilvie syndrome?

A: If neostigmine is contraindicated or ineffective.

Colonic Perforation

Q1848: What are common causes of colonic perforation?

A: Diverticulitis, colon cancer, ischemia, inflammatory bowel disease, trauma, toxic megacolon, and iatrogenic injury (e.g., colonoscopy).

Q1849: What is the definitive treatment for generalized colonic perforation with peritonitis?

A: Emergency surgical source control plus broad-spectrum antibiotics.

Q1850: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for lower GI emergencies?

A: Acute appendicitis is treated with appendectomy after diagnosis. Uncomplicated diverticulitis is usually managed nonoperatively, whereas perforation, abscess, obstruction, fistula, or diffuse peritonitis require intervention. Toxic megacolon and fulminant C. difficile colitis demand early recognition, aggressive medical therapy, and prompt surgical consultation. In ischemic colitis, surgery is indicated for transmural necrosis or perforation.

Q1851: What is an abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA)?

A: A permanent dilation of the abdominal aorta to ≥3 cm in diameter.

Q1852: What are the major risk factors for AAA?

A: Age >65 years, smoking (strongest modifiable risk factor), male sex, hypertension, atherosclerosis, and family history.

Q1853: What is the classic triad of ruptured AAA?

A: Abdominal/back pain, hypotension, and a pulsatile abdominal mass.

Q1854: Does the absence of the classic triad exclude ruptured AAA?

A: No. Many patients present without all three findings.

Q1855: Which diagnosis should always be considered in an older patient with sudden abdominal or back pain and hypotension?

A: Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm.

Q1856: What is the preferred imaging study for a hemodynamically stable patient with suspected AAA?

A: CT angiography (CTA).

Q1857: Should an unstable patient with suspected ruptured AAA undergo CT before surgery?

A: No. Immediate operative management is indicated; bedside ultrasound may rapidly confirm an aneurysm without delaying treatment.

Q1858: What is the definitive treatment for ruptured AAA?

A: Emergency vascular repair (endovascular or open, depending on anatomy and resources).

Acute Limb Ischemia

Q1859: What is acute limb ischemia?

A: Sudden reduction in arterial blood flow threatening limb viability.

Q1860: What are the six P's of acute limb ischemia?

A: Pain, Pallor, Pulselessness, Paresthesia, Paralysis, and Poikilothermia (cold limb).

Q1861: What are the most common causes of acute limb ischemia?

A: Arterial embolism (often from atrial fibrillation) and acute thrombosis of an atherosclerotic artery.

Q1862: Which artery is most commonly occluded by a cardiac embolus?

A: The femoral artery.

Q1863: What is the first medication given when acute arterial occlusion is suspected and there is no contraindication?

A: Immediate IV unfractionated heparin.

Q1864: What is the preferred imaging study in a stable patient with acute limb ischemia?

A: CT angiography (or catheter angiography if proceeding directly to intervention).

Q1865: Which patients require immediate revascularization without unnecessary delay?

A: Patients with a viable or threatened limb, especially those with motor deficits or rapidly progressive ischemia.

Q1866: What are options for definitive treatment of acute limb ischemia?

A: Surgical embolectomy, catheter-directed thrombolysis (selected patients), thrombectomy, bypass, or endovascular intervention.

Aortic Dissection (Surgical Perspective)

Q1867: Which type of aortic dissection requires emergency surgery?

A: Stanford Type A (ascending aorta involved).

Q1868: Which type of aortic dissection is usually managed medically unless complicated?

A: Stanford Type B (descending aorta only).

Q1869: What is the initial medical treatment while preparing a patient with aortic dissection?

A: IV beta-blocker (e.g., esmolol) to reduce heart rate and shear stress; vasodilators may be added after adequate beta blockade if needed.

Q1870: What imaging study is preferred in a stable patient with suspected aortic dissection?

A: CT angiography.

Necrotizing Fasciitis

Q1871: What is necrotizing fasciitis?

A: A rapidly progressive infection of the fascia and subcutaneous tissues causing tissue necrosis.

Q1872: Which organisms commonly cause necrotizing fasciitis?

A: Group A Streptococcus, polymicrobial infections (including anaerobes), MRSA, and marine organisms in specific exposures.

Q1873: What is the classic early clinical clue to necrotizing fasciitis?

A: Pain out of proportion to physical examination.

Q1874: Which skin findings develop later in necrotizing fasciitis?

A: Bullae, skin discoloration, ecchymosis, anesthesia, crepitus (sometimes), and tissue necrosis.

Q1875: Is crepitus required to diagnose necrotizing fasciitis?

A: No. Many patients have no crepitus early in the disease.

Q1876: Should imaging delay surgery when necrotizing fasciitis is strongly suspected?

A: No. Immediate surgical exploration and debridement are essential.

Q1877: What is the cornerstone of treatment for necrotizing fasciitis?

A: Urgent, aggressive surgical debridement plus broad-spectrum IV antibiotics and resuscitation.

Q1878: Which antibiotics are commonly included empirically for necrotizing fasciitis?

A: Broad gram-positive, gram-negative, and anaerobic coverage (e.g., vancomycin plus piperacillin-tazobactam) plus clindamycin to inhibit streptococcal toxin production.

Fournier Gangrene

Q1879: What is Fournier gangrene?

A: Necrotizing fasciitis involving the perineum, genitalia, or perianal region.

Q1880: Which patients are at greatest risk for Fournier gangrene?

A: Patients with diabetes mellitus, alcoholism, immunosuppression, obesity, or chronic kidney disease.

Q1881: What is the treatment for Fournier gangrene?

A: Immediate surgical debridement, broad-spectrum antibiotics, aggressive supportive care, and repeated debridements as needed.

Compartment Syndrome

Q1882: What causes acute compartment syndrome?

A: Elevated pressure within a closed fascial compartment, compromising tissue perfusion.

Q1883: What are common causes of compartment syndrome?

A: Fractures (especially tibial), crush injuries, reperfusion injury, burns, vascular injury, and tight casts or dressings.

Q1884: What is the earliest symptom of compartment syndrome?

A: Severe pain, especially pain with passive stretch of the involved muscles.

Q1885: Are absent pulses an early finding in compartment syndrome?

A: No. Pulses may remain present until late; do not wait for pulse loss to diagnose.

Q1886: What is the definitive treatment for compartment syndrome?

A: Emergency fasciotomy.

Gas Gangrene (Clostridial Myonecrosis)

Q1887: Which organism most commonly causes gas gangrene?

A: Clostridium perfringens.

Q1888: What is the typical presentation of gas gangrene?

A: Severe pain, rapidly progressive swelling, crepitus, foul-smelling discharge, systemic toxicity, and muscle necrosis after trauma or surgery.

Q1889: What imaging finding may be seen in gas gangrene?

A: Gas within soft tissues or muscle.

Q1890: What is the definitive treatment for gas gangrene?

A: Immediate surgical debridement (or amputation if necessary) plus high-dose IV antibiotics (e.g., penicillin plus clindamycin).

Surgical Sepsis

Q1891: What is the most important principle in managing surgical sepsis?

A: Early source control in addition to sepsis-directed resuscitation and antibiotics.

Q1892: What is source control?

A: Elimination of the focus of infection by drainage, debridement, repair, or resection.

Q1893: When should broad-spectrum antibiotics be administered in septic shock?

A: As soon as possible, ideally within the first hour after recognition, after obtaining cultures if this does not cause significant delay.

Q1894: Which vasopressor is first-line if hypotension persists after adequate fluid resuscitation?

A: Norepinephrine.

Damage-Control Surgery

Q1895: What is damage-control surgery?

A: A staged operative approach that controls hemorrhage and contamination first, with definitive repair after physiologic stabilization.

Q1896: Which patients benefit most from damage-control surgery?

A: Patients with severe trauma, profound shock, hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy.

High-Yield Clinical Pearls

Q1897: Which diagnosis should be suspected in any diabetic patient with rapidly progressive perineal pain and systemic toxicity?

A: Fournier gangrene.

Q1898: Which diagnosis should be suspected when pain is extreme but the skin initially appears relatively normal?

A: Necrotizing fasciitis or acute compartment syndrome, depending on the clinical context.

Q1899: Which vascular emergency should be suspected in a patient with atrial fibrillation and sudden cold, painful, pulseless leg?

A: Acute arterial embolism causing acute limb ischemia.

Q1900: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for vascular and soft tissue emergencies?

A: Recognize life-threatening surgical emergencies early and avoid delays. Ruptured AAA requires immediate vascular repair, acute limb ischemia requires prompt IV heparin and urgent revascularization, necrotizing fasciitis and Fournier gangrene require immediate surgical debridement plus broad-spectrum antibiotics, compartment syndrome requires emergent fasciotomy, and successful treatment of surgical sepsis depends on rapid source control in addition to standard sepsis management.

Q1901: What is the purpose of the ATLS primary survey?

A: Rapid identification and treatment of immediately life-threatening injuries.

Q1902: What does the ATLS primary survey stand for?

A:
A – Airway with cervical spine protection
B – Breathing and ventilation
C – Circulation and hemorrhage control
D – Disability (neurologic assessment)
E – Exposure/environmental control

Airway Management

Q1903: What is the first priority in trauma management?

A: Ensure a patent airway while protecting the cervical spine.

Q1904: What maneuver opens the airway in a trauma patient with possible cervical spine injury?

A: Jaw-thrust maneuver.

Q1905: What airway maneuver should generally be avoided in suspected cervical spine injury?

A: Head tilt–chin lift.

Q1906: What are signs of airway compromise in trauma?

A: Hoarseness, stridor, facial trauma, expanding neck hematoma, altered mental status, and inability to maintain oxygenation.

Q1907: What is the definitive airway in a trauma patient unable to protect their airway?

A: Endotracheal intubation.

Q1908: What is the emergency airway procedure if intubation fails and oxygenation cannot be maintained?

A: Cricothyrotomy.

Breathing and Chest Trauma

Q1909: What life-threatening chest injuries must be identified during the breathing assessment?

A: Tension pneumothorax, massive hemothorax, open pneumothorax, flail chest, and cardiac tamponade.

Tension Pneumothorax

Q1910: What is a tension pneumothorax?

A: Accumulation of air under pressure in the pleural space causing lung collapse and impaired venous return.

Q1911: What causes hemodynamic instability in tension pneumothorax?

A: Compression of the vena cava → decreased venous return → obstructive shock.

Q1912: What are classic findings of tension pneumothorax?

A: Severe respiratory distress, hypotension, unilateral absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation away from affected side, and distended neck veins.

Q1913: Is tracheal deviation required to diagnose tension pneumothorax?

A: No. It is a late finding.

Q1914: What is the immediate treatment for suspected tension pneumothorax?

A: Immediate needle decompression followed by chest tube placement.

Q1915: Should imaging be obtained before treating suspected tension pneumothorax?

A: No. Treatment should not be delayed.

Open Pneumothorax

Q1916: What is an open pneumothorax?

A: A penetrating chest wound that creates communication between the pleural space and atmosphere.

Q1917: What is the classic physical finding of an open pneumothorax?

A: A sucking chest wound.

Q1918: How is an open pneumothorax initially managed?

A: Apply a three-sided occlusive dressing followed by chest tube placement away from the wound.

Q1919: Why is a three-sided dressing used instead of a completely sealed dressing?

A: It allows air to escape and prevents development of tension physiology.

Massive Hemothorax

Q1920: What is a hemothorax?

A: Blood accumulation within the pleural cavity.

Q1921: What defines a massive hemothorax?

A: Approximately >1500 mL blood immediately after chest tube placement or continued large-volume output (classically >200 mL/hr for several hours).

Q1922: What are findings of massive hemothorax?

A: Respiratory distress, hypotension, decreased breath sounds, and dullness to percussion.

Q1923: What is the initial treatment of hemothorax?

A: Large-bore chest tube placement and resuscitation.

Q1924: When is thoracotomy considered for hemothorax?

A: Massive initial output or persistent high chest tube output suggesting ongoing bleeding.

Flail Chest

Q1925: What is flail chest?

A: Fracture of multiple consecutive ribs in multiple locations causing a free-floating chest wall segment.

Q1926: What is the hallmark physical finding of flail chest?

A: Paradoxical chest wall movement.

Q1927: What is the major cause of respiratory failure in flail chest?

A: Associated pulmonary contusion, pain-related hypoventilation, and impaired mechanics.

Q1928: What is the initial management of flail chest?

A: Oxygen, aggressive pain control, pulmonary hygiene, and respiratory support if needed.

Pulmonary Contusion

Q1929: What is a pulmonary contusion?

A: Traumatic injury causing alveolar hemorrhage and edema.

Q1930: When do pulmonary contusions typically become clinically apparent?

A: Within the first 24–48 hours after injury.

Q1931: What imaging finding is typical of pulmonary contusion?

A: Patchy pulmonary infiltrates that do not respect anatomical boundaries.

Q1932: What is the treatment of pulmonary contusion?

A: Supportive care, oxygenation, pain control, and mechanical ventilation if severe.

Cardiac Tamponade

Q1933: What is cardiac tamponade?

A: Accumulation of fluid/blood in the pericardial sac causing impaired cardiac filling.

Q1934: What are the classic findings of cardiac tamponade (Beck triad)?

A: Hypotension, muffled heart sounds, and jugular venous distension.

Q1935: Is Beck triad always present?

A: No. It is classically described but often incomplete.

Q1936: What FAST finding suggests cardiac tamponade?

A: Pericardial fluid.

Q1937: What is the emergency treatment for traumatic cardiac tamponade?

A: Surgical drainage (often emergency thoracotomy in unstable trauma patients).

Circulation & Hemorrhagic Shock

Q1938: What is the most common cause of preventable death after trauma?

A: Uncontrolled hemorrhage.

Q1939: What are early signs of hemorrhagic shock?

A: Tachycardia, anxiety, cool extremities, and delayed capillary refill.

Q1940: What is the most sensitive early vital sign change in hemorrhage?

A: Increasing heart rate.

Q1941: What are the four classes of hemorrhagic shock based on ATLS?

A: Class I, II, III, and IV based on blood loss severity and physiologic response.

Q1942: What is the initial fluid strategy in major trauma?

A: Control hemorrhage while providing balanced resuscitation with blood products when indicated.

Massive Transfusion Protocol (MTP)

Q1943: When should massive transfusion protocol be activated?

A: When a patient is expected to require large-volume blood replacement due to severe hemorrhage.

Q1944: What is the goal of massive transfusion?

A: Rapid replacement of blood components while preventing trauma-induced coagulopathy.

Q1945: What blood products are commonly given in balanced transfusion?

A: Packed RBCs, plasma, and platelets in approximately a balanced ratio.

Q1946: Why is calcium monitored during massive transfusion?

A: Citrate in blood products binds calcium, causing hypocalcemia.

FAST Examination

Q1947: What does FAST stand for?

A: Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma.

Q1948: What does FAST detect?

A: Free fluid in the abdomen, pelvis, and pericardium.

Q1949: In an unstable trauma patient, what does a positive FAST usually indicate?

A: Need for urgent operative management if intra-abdominal bleeding is suspected.

Q1950: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK trauma principle?

A: Follow ATLS priorities: secure the airway with cervical spine protection, treat immediately life-threatening chest injuries without waiting for imaging, control hemorrhage early, use FAST in unstable trauma patients, and prioritize definitive hemorrhage control over complete diagnostic evaluation.

Q1951: What are the two major categories of abdominal trauma?

A: Blunt trauma and penetrating trauma.

Q1952: What is the most common cause of blunt abdominal trauma?

A: Motor vehicle collisions.

Q1953: Which abdominal organs are most commonly injured in blunt trauma?

A: Spleen, liver, kidney, and bowel.

Q1954: What is the most important initial question when evaluating abdominal trauma?

A: Is the patient hemodynamically stable or unstable?

Q1955: What is the preferred diagnostic approach in an unstable trauma patient with suspected abdominal bleeding?

A: FAST examination.

Q1956: What is the preferred diagnostic test in a stable trauma patient with suspected intra-abdominal injury?

A: CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast.

Splenic Injury

A: Spleen.

Q1958: What clinical finding suggests splenic injury?

A: Left upper quadrant pain, left shoulder pain (Kehr sign), hypotension, and abdominal tenderness.

Q1959: What is Kehr sign?

A: Referred pain to the left shoulder due to diaphragmatic irritation from intra-abdominal blood.

Q1960: What imaging study is preferred for suspected splenic injury in stable patients?

A: Contrast-enhanced CT.

Q1961: How is splenic injury managed in stable patients?

A: Nonoperative management with observation and/or splenic artery embolization depending on severity.

Q1962: When is splenectomy indicated after trauma?

A: Hemodynamic instability, uncontrolled hemorrhage, or severe splenic destruction.

Q1963: What is the major long-term risk after splenectomy?

A: Contrast-enhanced CT.

Q1967: How are most stable liver injuries managed?

A: Nonoperative management with monitoring and supportive care.

Q1968: When is surgery required for liver trauma?

A: Persistent hemodynamic instability or uncontrolled hemorrhage.

Hollow Viscus Injury

Q1969: Which organs are considered hollow viscera?

A: Stomach, small intestine, colon, and bladder.

Q1970: What imaging finding suggests hollow viscus injury?

Blunt Cardiac Injury

Q1978: What is blunt cardiac injury?

Increased Intracranial Pressure (ICP)

Q2050: What is the treatment for severe local anesthetic toxicity?

Q2051: What is malignant hyperthermia?

A: A life-threatening hypermetabolic reaction of skeletal muscle triggered by certain anesthetic agents.

Q2052: What drugs commonly trigger malignant hyperthermia?

A:

Volatile inhaled anesthetics
Succinylcholine

Q2053: What is the genetic defect associated with malignant hyperthermia?

A: Mutation in the RYR1 gene, causing abnormal calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum.

Q2054: What is the earliest sign of malignant hyperthermia?

A: Rapid increase in end-tidal CO₂ (ETCO₂).

Q2055: What are other signs of malignant hyperthermia?

A:

Muscle rigidity
Tachycardia
Hyperthermia
Acidosis
Hyperkalemia
Elevated creatine kinase (CK)

Q2056: Why does malignant hyperthermia cause hyperkalemia?

A: Massive muscle breakdown releases intracellular potassium.

Q2057: What is the treatment for malignant hyperthermia?

A: Dantrolene.

Q2058: How does dantrolene work?

A: Blocks calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum by inhibiting the ryanodine receptor.

Q2059: What laboratory finding suggests severe muscle injury from malignant hyperthermia?

A: Markedly elevated creatine kinase.

Q2062: What is Mendelson syndrome?

A: Aspiration pneumonitis caused by gastric acid entering the lungs.

Q2063: What is the classic presentation of aspiration pneumonitis?

A: Sudden respiratory distress, hypoxia, cough, and diffuse pulmonary infiltrates after aspiration.

Q2064: What is the initial management of aspiration pneumonitis?

Q2065: Are antibiotics routinely indicated immediately after aspiration pneumonitis?

A: No, unless bacterial pneumonia develops.

Postoperative Ileus

Q2096: What is postoperative ileus?

A: Temporary impairment of intestinal motility after surgery without mechanical obstruction.

Obstruction:

Transition point

Often hyperactive sounds early

Q2100: What is the highest-yield USMLE Step 2 CK principle for anesthesia and postoperative emergencies?

A: Early postoperative complications are usually related to airway, breathing, circulation, and anesthesia. Malignant hyperthermia requires immediate dantrolene. Postoperative fever should be approached according to timing. Respiratory compromise requires rapid evaluation of oxygenation and ventilation. Bleeding requires stabilization and possible reoperation. Always distinguish postoperative ileus from mechanical obstruction before deciding management.
