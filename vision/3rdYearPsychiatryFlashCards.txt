Section:Psychiatry
Topic:Mood disorders — major depression, bipolar I & II

Front: Minimum duration of symptoms required to diagnose a Major Depressive Episode (MDE)?
Back: Two weeks.

Front: Number of DSM-5 symptoms required to diagnose a Major Depressive Episode?
Back: At least five symptoms, with at least one being depressed mood or anhedonia.

Front: What does the mnemonic SIGECAPS stand for in depression screening?
Back: Sleep, Interest (Anhedonia), Guilt, Energy, Concentration, Appetite, Psychomotor, Suicidal ideation.

Front: True or False: A history of a manic or hypomanic episode excludes the diagnosis of MDD?
Back: True.

Front: Maximum time window required for "with peripartum onset" specifier in mood disorders?
Back: Onset of symptoms during pregnancy or within 4 weeks postpartum.

Front: Cardinal feature differentiating atypical depression from classic MDD?
Back: Mood reactivity (mood brightens in response to positive events).

Front: Three key vegetative/associated symptoms of atypical depression?
Back: Hypersomnia, hyperphagia (leaden paralysis), and long-standing interpersonal rejection sensitivity.

Front: Defining feature of MDD with melancholic features regarding morning symptoms?
Back: Depression is regularly worse in the morning, accompanied by early morning awakening.

Front: What defines MDD with catatonia?
Back: Severe psychomotor disturbance involving stupor, blunting, mutism, negativity, or waxy flexibility.

Front: Treatment of choice for MDD with catatonia if benzodiazepines fail?
Back: Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT).

Front: Define MDD with seasonal pattern (Seasonal Affective Disorder).
Back: Onset and remission of MDEs at characteristic times of the year for at least 2 consecutive years.

Front: Normal time frame for typical grief (bereavement) symptoms to peak?
Back: Within the first 2 months following the loss.

Front: Pathognomonic feature distinguishing Prolonged Grief Disorder from standard MDE?
Back: Intense longing/preoccupation with the deceased lasting at least 12 months (6 months for children).

MDD: Pathophysiology & Neurobiology
Front: Primary monoamine neurotransmitters implicated in the pathophysiology of MDD?
Back: Serotonin (5-HT), Norepinephrine (NE), and Dopamine (DA).

Front: Neuroendocrine axis frequently found to be hyperactive in patients with MDD?
Back: Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to elevated cortisol.

Front: Structural brain change most consistently demonstrated in chronic, untreated MDD?
Back: Decreased hippocampal volume.

Front: Sleep architecture change: What happens to REM latency in MDD?
Back: REM latency is shortened (REM sleep begins earlier in the night).

Front: Sleep architecture change: What happens to slow-wave (deep) sleep in MDD?
Back: Decreased slow-wave sleep (stages N3).

MDD: Psychopharmacology (First-Line & Mechanisms)
Front: First-line medication class of choice for uncomplicated MDD?
Back: Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs).

Front: Average timeline required to assess the full therapeutic efficacy of an antidepressant dose?
Back: 4 to 8 weeks.

Front: Most common early, transient side effects of SSRIs that lead to non-adherence?
Back: Gastrointestinal distress (nausea, diarrhea) and headaches.

Front: Long-term side effect of SSRIs that does not usually resolve with time?
Back: Sexual dysfunction (decreased libido, anorgasmia).

Front: Mechanism of action of Venlafaxine and Duloxetine?
Back: Serotonin-Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitors (SNRIs).

Front: Major medical contraindication specific to initiating Venlafaxine?
Back: Uncontrolled hypertension (due to dose-dependent noradrenergic effects).

Front: SNRI preferred for patients with comorbid depression and chronic neuropathic pain?
Back: Duloxetine.

Front: Unique mechanism of action of Mirtazapine?
Back: Alpha-2 adrenergic antagonist, increasing release of 5-HT and NE.

Front: Two primary clinical benefits/side effects utilized when choosing Mirtazapine?
Back: Sedation (histamine-1 blockade) and appetite stimulation/weight gain.

Front: Mechanism of action of Bupropion?
Back: Norepinephrine-Dopamine Reuptake Inhibitor (NDRI).

Front: Two major clinical advantages of Bupropion over SSRIs?
Back: Lack of sexual side effects and lack of weight gain.

Front: Absolute medical contraindications for prescribing Bupropion?
Back: Seizure disorders, anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, or active alcohol/benzo withdrawal.

MDD: Advanced Psychopharmacology & Toxicity
Front: Mechanism of Trazodone and its primary clinical utility in depression?
Back: 5-HT2 receptor antagonist and reuptake inhibitor; primarily used low-dose as a non-addictive hypnotic.

Front: Rare, medical emergency side effect associated with Trazodone?
Back: Priapism.

Front: Mechanism of action of Vortioxetine?
Back: SSRI combined with 5-HT1A agonism and 5-HT3/5-HT7 antagonism.

Front: Primary toxic mechanism of Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs) in overdose?
Back: Inhibition of fast sodium channels in the myocardium, leading to QRS widening and arrhythmias.

Front: Triad of classic toxicities seen in TCA overdose?
Back: Cardiotoxicity (arrhythmias), Convulsions (seizures), and Coma.

Front: Immediate medical antidote for TCA-induced cardiac arrhythmias?
Back: Intravenous Sodium Bicarbonate.

Front: Dietary restriction mandatory for patients taking Monoamine Oxidase Inhibitors (MAOIs)?
Back: Low-tyramine diet (avoiding aged cheeses, cured meats, red wine).

Front: Pathophysiology of the hypertensive crisis caused by combining MAOIs with tyramine?
Back: Tyramine displaces stored norepinephrine into the synaptic cleft, causing massive vasoconstriction.

Front: Immediate pharmacologic intervention for an MAOI-induced hypertensive crisis?
Back: Phentolamine (alpha-adrenergic antagonist).

Front: Mechanism behind Serotonin Syndrome when combining MAOIs and SSRIs?
Back: Overactivation of central and peripheral 5-HT1A and 5-HT2A receptors.

Front: Minimum washout period required when switching from an SSRI to an MAOI?
Back: 2 weeks (5 weeks specifically for Fluoxetine due to long half-life).

Front: Classic clinical triad of Serotonin Syndrome?
Back: Autonomic instability, altered mental status, and neuromuscular hyperactivity.

Front: Pathognomonic physical exam finding that distinguishes Serotonin Syndrome from NMS?
Back: Hyperreflexia and clonus (spontaneous, inducible, or ocular).

Front: First-line medical treatment for severe Serotonin Syndrome?
Back: Cyproheptadine (5-HT2A antagonist) along with aggressive cooling and benzodiazepines.

Front: Evidence-based psychotherapy modalities proven effective as monotherapy for mild-to-moderate MDD?
Back: Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) and Interpersonal Therapy (IPT).

Front: Quickest, most effective treatment for severe, treatment-resistant MDD or psychotic depression?
Back: Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT).

Front: Absolute medical contraindication to performing ECT?
Back: None (increased intracranial pressure is a relative contraindication requiring caution).

Front: Most common acute side effect associated with ECT courses?
Back: Retrograde and anterograde amnesia (usually resolves within a few months).

Front: Indication for Esketamine nasal spray in major depression?
Back: Treatment-Resistant Depression (TRD) or acute suicidal ideation, acting via NMDA receptor antagonism.

Front: Standard maintenance guideline duration for an antidepressant after a patient's first MDE?
Back: Continue effective dose for 6 to 12 months after achieving full remission.

Front: Indication for lifelong maintenance antidepressant therapy?
Back: History of 3 or more MDEs, or chronic severe episodes.

Bipolar I Disorder: Diagnostics & Clinical Features
Front: Minimal duration required to diagnose a manic episode?
Back: 1 week (or any duration if hospitalization is required).

Front: Core requirement regarding mood change to diagnose a manic episode?
Back: Abnormally, persistently elevated, expansive, or irritable mood, PLUS persistently increased goal-directed activity/energy.

Front: Minimum number of symptoms required for a manic episode diagnosis?
Back: Three (four if the mood is only irritable).

Front: Mnemonic DIGFAST for mania symptoms stands for?
Back: Distractibility, Indiscretion (impulsivity), Grandiosity, Flight of ideas, Activity increase, Sleep deficit, Talkativeness (pressured speech).

Front: True or False: A history of a Major Depressive Episode is required to diagnose Bipolar I Disorder?
Back: False (only 1 lifetime manic episode is required).

Front: True or False: The presence of hallucinations or delusions automatically classifies an episode as manic rather than hypomanic?
Back: True.

Front: Defining social/occupational criteria separating mania from hypomania?
Back: Mania causes marked impairment in functioning or requires hospitalization; hypomania does not.

Front: What is Bipolar I with rapid cycling?
Back: Four or more distinct mood episodes (mania, hypomania, or depression) within a 12-month period.

Front: Class of medications known to precipitate or accelerate rapid cycling in Bipolar patients?
Back: Antidepressants (specifically TCAs and SNRIs/SSRIs).

Front: Peak age of onset for Bipolar I disorder?
Back: Late adolescence to early adulthood (18 to 25 years).

Front: Diagnostic criteria combination required to diagnose Bipolar II Disorder?
Back: At least one lifetime Hypomanic Episode AND at least one lifetime Major Depressive Episode.

Front: True or False: A history of a single manic episode permanently changes a Bipolar II diagnosis to Bipolar I?
Back: True.

Front: Minimal duration required to diagnose a hypomanic episode?
Back: 4 consecutive days.

Front: Define Cyclothymic Disorder criteria regarding symptom duration?
Back: At least 2 years (1 year in children/adolescents) of numerous periods of hypomanic and depressive symptoms that do not meet full criteria for an episode.

Front: Maximum time a patient with Cyclothymia can be symptom-free during the 2-year diagnostic window?
Back: 2 months.

Front: Strongest risk factor for developing Bipolar I Disorder?
Back: Family history (monozygotic twin concordance rates approach 40% to 70%).

Front: Neurotransmitter alteration typically associated with acute mania?
Back: Hyperactivity of dopamine and norepinephrine pathways.

Bipolar Treatment: Lithium
Front: First-line mood stabilizer uniquely shown to reduce suicide risk in Bipolar Disorder?
Back: Lithium.

Front: Baseline laboratory investigations mandatory before initiating Lithium?
Back: Renal function (BUN/Creatinine), Thyroid Function Tests (TSH), Electrolytes, and beta-hCG (pregnancy test).

Front: Classic congenital cardiac anomaly associated with first-trimester Lithium exposure?
Back: Ebstein's anomaly (displacement of tricuspid valve leaflets into the right ventricle).

Front: Narrow therapeutic index range for Lithium in acute mania?
Back: 1.0 to 1.5 mEq/L.

Front: Maintenance therapeutic index range for Lithium?
Back: 0.6 to 1.2 mEq/L.

Front: Common, benign neurologic side effect of therapeutic Lithium?
Back: Fine resting/intention tremor.

Front: Long-term endocrine adverse effect of Lithium therapy requiring tracking?
Back: Hypothyroidism (often treatable by adding Levothyroxine without stopping Lithium).

Front: Renal pathology caused by long-term Lithium use resulting in polyuria/polydipsia?
Back: Nephrogenic Diabetes Insipidus (NDI).

Front: Pharmacologic treatment of choice for Lithium-induced Nephrogenic Diabetes Insipidus?
Back: Amiloride (blocks Lithium entry into collecting duct epithelial cells).

Front: Medication classes that acutely elevate Lithium levels and trigger toxicity?
Back: Thiazide diuretics, NSAIDs, and ACE inhibitors/ARBs.

Front: Gastrointestinal and neurologic signs of acute Lithium toxicity?
Back: Nausea, vomiting, severe diarrhea, coarse tremor, ataxia, slurred speech, and seizures.

Front: Definitive treatment intervention for acute Lithium toxicity with levels greater than 4.0 mEq/L (or greater than 2.5 mEq/L with severe neuro toxicity)?
Back: Hemodialysis.

Front: First-line mood stabilizer preferred for Bipolar with mixed features or rapid cycling?
Back: Valproic Acid (Valproate).

Front: Baseline and maintenance laboratory tracking required for Valproate?
Back: Liver Function Tests (LFTs) and Complete Blood Count (CBC) for platelets.

Front: Two black box warning toxicities associated with Valproate?
Back: Hepatotoxicity and Pancreatitis.

Front: Teratogenic defect caused by Valproate exposure in early pregnancy?
Back: Neural tube defects (e.g., spina bifida via folate antagonism).

Front: Therapeutic plasma concentration range for Valproate in mood disorders?
Back: 50 to 125 mcg/mL.

Front: Primary clinical indication for Lamotrigine in Bipolar Disorder management?
Back: Maintenance therapy to prevent Bipolar Depression (ineffective for acute mania).

Front: Serious, life-threatening dermatologic adverse effect of Lamotrigine?
Back: Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) / Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN).

Front: Clinical titration strategy used to minimize the risk of Lamotrigine-induced SJS?
Back: Extremely slow, low-dose upward titration over several weeks.

Front: Drug-drug interaction effect of Valproate on Lamotrigine metabolism?
Back: Valproate doubles Lamotrigine levels; Lamotrigine dose must be halved.

Bipolar Treatment: Second-Generation Antipsychotics & Carbamazepine
Front: Mechanism of action making Carbamazepine useful in Bipolar mania?
Back: Blockade of voltage-gated sodium channels.

Front: Serious hematologic side effect associated with Carbamazepine requiring CBC tracking?
Back: Agranulocytosis/Aplastic anemia.

Front: Electrolyte abnormality frequently caused by Carbamazepine (especially in elderly)?
Back: Hyponatremia (SIADH-like effect).

Front: Role of Second-Generation Antipsychotics (SGAs) in managing acute manic episodes?
Back: First-line monotherapy or adjunct to mood stabilizers for rapid control of agitation and psychosis.

Front: Which SGAs are specifically FDA-approved for the treatment of acute Bipolar Depression?
Back: Quetiapine, Lurasidone, Cariprazine, and the Olanzapine-Fluoxetine combination.

Front: Primary metabolic side effects requiring long-term monitoring with Olanzapine and Clozapine?
Back: Weight gain, dyslipidemia, and hyperglycemia/Type 2 Diabetes.


Front: Initial step in treating a patient presenting with an acute MDE who has a family history of Bipolar I?
Back: Screen carefully for past spontaneous manic/hypomanic episodes before starting an antidepressant.

Front: Dangerous clinical outcome of treating a Bipolar patient with SSRI monotherapy?
Back: Treatment-emergent mania or hypomania switch.

Front: First-line treatment strategy for a pregnant patient experiencing severe, life-threatening manic or depressive episodes?
Back: Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT).

Front: Most common psychiatric comorbidity found in individuals with Bipolar Disorder?
Back: Anxiety disorders (followed closely by Substance Use Disorders).

Front: Percentage of individuals with Bipolar Disorder who attempt suicide at least once?
Back: Approximately 25% to 50%.

Front: First-line pharmacologic strategy for a patient presenting with a manic episode with mixed features?
Back: Valproate or an atypical antipsychotic (avoid lithium if severe depressive features dominate).

Front: Difference between a "response" and "remission" in mood disorder treatment?
Back: Response is a 50% or greater reduction in symptoms; Remission is the near-total absence of symptoms.

Front: Define Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder (DMDD) age window for diagnosis?
Back: Onset must be before age 10; cannot diagnose before age 6 or after age 18.

Front: Core clinical feature differentiating DMDD from Bipolar disorder in children?
Back: DMDD features persistent, chronic irritability between temper outbursts, rather than episodic shifts.

Front: Minimum symptom duration required to diagnose Persistent Depressive Disorder (Dysthymia) in adults?
Back: 2 years.

Front: Minimum symptom duration required to diagnose Persistent Depressive Disorder (Dysthymia) in children?
Back: 1 year.

Front: Maximum period a patient can be symptom-free during the course of Persistent Depressive Disorder?
Back: 2 months.

Front: Core diagnostic symptom timing requirement for Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD)?
Back: Symptoms express in the week before menses onset, improve within a few days after menses starts, and become minimal/absent post-menses.

Front: First-line pharmacologic treatment options for severe PMDD?
Back: SSRIs (administered either daily or luteal-phase-only).

Front: Primary defense mechanism implicated in depression according to classic psychoanalytic theory?
Back: Introjection (turning anger inward against the self after the loss of an object).

Front: Beck's Cognitive Triad of depression consists of negative views of what three things?
Back: The self, the world, and the future.

Front: Structural brain change seen in Bipolar Disorder during neuroimaging structural studies?
Back: Nonspecific white matter hyperintensities and ventricular enlargement.

Front: Most common cause of treatment failure in patients diagnosed with Bipolar Disorder?
Back: Medication non-adherence (often due to missing the high of hypomania/mania or side effects).

Front: Preferred maintenance drug for a bipolar patient with severe renal impairment?
Back: Valproate (avoid Lithium).

Front: Preferred maintenance drug for a bipolar patient with active hepatic failure?
Back: Lithium (avoid Valproate/Carbamazepine).

Front: Primary lab marker utilized to monitor for Carbamazepine auto-induction?
Back: Serum Carbamazepine levels (Carbamazepine induces its own metabolism over the first 4-6 weeks).

Front: HLA allele screening required before starting Carbamazepine in Asian patients?
Back: HLA-B*1502 (due to high risk of SJS/TEN).

Front: Pharmacologic treatment of choice for severe Bipolar depression when multiple monotherapies fail?
Back: Mood stabilizer (Lithium or Valproate) PLUS an atypical antipsychotic (e.g., Lurasidone or Quetiapine).

Front: True or False: Psychotherapy alone is sufficient as a primary treatment for acute Bipolar I mania?
Back: False (pharmacotherapy is mandatory; psychotherapy is an adjunct for maintenance).

Front: What is "double depression"?
Back: Major Depressive Episodes superimposed on a chronic course of Persistent Depressive Disorder.

Front: Major clinical feature that distinguishes Bipolar I with psychotic features from Schizoaffective Disorder?
Back: In Bipolar, psychotic symptoms occur only during a mood episode; in Schizoaffective, psychosis occurs for 2 or more weeks in the absence of prominent mood symptoms.

Front: Most common over-the-counter medication class that can precipitate depressive symptoms?
Back: Systemic corticosteroids (e.g., Prednisone).

Front: Cardiovascular medication class classically associated with treatment-emergent depression?
Back: Beta-blockers (e.g., Propranolol).

Front: Neurological medication used for Parkinson’s disease that can precipitate manic-like symptoms?
Back: Levodopa (due to increase in central dopamine).

Front: Endocrine disorder that mimics a manic episode and must be ruled out with a TSH test?
Back: Hyperthyroidism.

Front: Infectious disease characterized by profound fatigue, depressed mood, and atypical lymphocytosis?
Back: Epstein-Barr Virus (Infectious Mononucleosis).

Front: Pancreatic pathology classically presenting with sudden-onset depression before physical symptoms emerge?
Back: Pancreatic adenocarcinoma.

Front: Neurological pathology presenting with depression, chorea, and dementia?
Back: Huntington's disease.

Front: Substance withdrawal that presents with dysphoria, hypersomnia, hyperphagia, and vivid dreams?
Back: Cocaine or Amphetamine withdrawal ("crashing").

Front: Substance intoxication that presents with grandiosity, paranoia, tachycardia, and pupillary dilation?
Back: Amphetamine or Cocaine intoxication.

Front: Over-the-counter herbal supplement used for depression that can cause Serotonin Syndrome if combined with an SSRI?
Back: St. John’s Wort.

Front: What defines MDD with anxious distress specifier?
Back: Presence of at least two symptoms of anxiety (e.g., feeling keyed up, unusually restless) during a major depressive episode.

Front: Diagnostic minimum for Bipolar I or II "with mixed features"?
Back: A full mood episode accompanied by at least three symptoms of the opposite polarity.

Front: What defines MDD with peri-menopausal onset?
Back: Onset of depressive symptoms during the menopausal transition, linked to fluctuating estrogen levels.

Front: True or False: Irritability can substitute for depressed mood in children and adolescents to meet Criterion A for MDD?
Back: True.

Front: Minimum duration of symptoms required to diagnose Cyclothymic Disorder in pediatric patients?
Back: One year.

Front: True or False: Subclinical hypothyroidism can cause resistance to standard antidepressant therapy?
Back: True.

Front: Statistically, which demographic has the highest rate of completed suicide in the US?
Back: Elderly Caucasian males (age greater than 65).

Front: Strongest single predictive risk factor for a future completed suicide?
Back: A history of previous suicide attempts.

Front: Gender paradox of suicide: Which gender attempts more, and which completes more?
Back: Females attempt suicide more frequently; males complete suicide more frequently.

Front: Mirroring genetic risk: What is the concordance rate of Bipolar I disorder in monozygotic twins?
Back: Approximately 40% to 70%.

Front: Mirroring genetic risk: What is the concordance rate of Major Depressive Disorder in monozygotic twins?
Back: Approximately 40% to 50%.

Front: Lifetime prevalence range of Major Depressive Disorder in the general population?
Back: 10% to 25% for women; 5% to 12% for men.

Front: Antidepressant class characterized by strong antihistaminic (H1) and anti-alpha-1 adrenergic side effects?
Back: Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs).

Front: Symptom presentation of anti-alpha-1 adrenergic blockade from psychotropic medications?
Back: Orthostatic hypotension and reflex tachycardia.

Front: Receptor blockade responsible for dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention, and constipation from TCAs?
Back: Muscarinic acetylcholine receptor blockade (anticholinergic effects).

Front: SSRI with the longest half-life, minimizing the risk of discontinuation syndrome?
Back: Fluoxetine.

Front: SSRIs with the shortest half-lives, presenting the highest risk for discontinuation syndrome?
Back: Paroxetine and Venlafaxine.

Front: Triad of features characterizing Antidepressant Discontinuation Syndrome?
Back: Flu-like symptoms, electrical shock sensations ("brain zaps"), and vivid dreams.

Front: Mechanism of action of Vilazodone?
Back: SSRI combined with 5-HT1A receptor partial agonism.

Front: Second-generation antipsychotic with a specific black-box warning for severe neutropenia/agranulocytosis?
Back: Clozapine.

Front: Mandatory laboratory tracking protocol required for prescribing Clozapine?
Back: Absolute Neutrophil Count (ANC).

Front: Mechanism of action of Ramelteon, often used for sleep disturbances in mood disorders?
Back: Melatonin receptor (MT1 and MT2) agonist.

Front: Initial medical intervention for a patient with Bipolar I presenting with acute Lithium toxicity and a prolonged QTc?
Back: Discontinue Lithium, administer intravenous hydration, and obtain urgent serum Lithium levels.

Front: Preferred mood stabilizer for a patient with Bipolar I who has comorbid juvenile myoclonic epilepsy?
Back: Valproic Acid.

Front: Maintenance treatment of choice for a patient with Bipolar II whose primary impairment stems from recurrent depressive crashes?
Back: Lamotrigine.

Front: What step should be taken if a patient on Lithium develops mild, asymptomatic hypercalcemia and elevated parathyroid hormone?
Back: Monitor levels; Lithium can cause hyperparathyroidism.

Front: First-line pharmacotherapy for a patient with MDD with psychotic features?
Back: Combination of an antidepressant and an antipsychotic (or ECT).

Front: Preferred antidepressant for an elderly patient with MDD, low appetite, and insomnia?
Back: Mirtazapine.

Front: Antidepressant class absolutely contraindicated for use with any triptan medication due to Serotonin Syndrome risk?
Back: MAOIs (and cautiously with SSRIs/SNRIs).

Front: Next management step for a patient with MDD who has shown zero improvement after 4 weeks of maximum-dose SSRI therapy?
Back: Switch to a different class of antidepressant (e.g., SNRI or atypical).

Front: Next management step for a patient with MDD who has shown a partial response (30% improvement) after 6 weeks of maximum-dose SSRI therapy?
Back: Augment with a non-SSRI agent (e.g., Bupropion, Lithium, or a low-dose atypical antipsychotic).

Front: Psychotherapeutic approach focusing heavily on changing distorted core beliefs and automatic negative thoughts?
Back: Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT).

Front: Psychotherapeutic approach focusing on grief, interpersonal role disputes, and role transitions?
Back: Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT).

Front: Key neuromuscular physical exam finding that distinguishes Serotonin Syndrome from Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS)?
Back: Hyperreflexia and clonus are present in Serotonin Syndrome; "lead-pipe" rigidity is present in NMS.

Front: Autonomic sign distinguishing Anticholinergic Toxicity from Serotonin Syndrome?
Back: Anticholinergic toxicity presents with dry, flushed skin and absent bowel sounds; Serotonin Syndrome presents with diaphoresis (sweating) and hyperactive bowel sounds.

Front: Lab value abnormality characteristically elevated in both Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome and severe Serotonin Syndrome due to muscle breakdown?
Back: Creatine Kinase (CK).

Front: Cardiac conduction abnormality that is an absolute contraindication to using Geodon (Ziprasidone) or Celexa (Citalopram) at high doses?
Back: QTc prolongation.

Front: Maximum recommended daily dose of Citalopram in elderly patients due to risk of QTc prolongation?
Back: 20 mg per day.

Front: SSRI that is a potent inhibitor of CYP2D6 and can dramatically increase levels of beta-blockers and venlafaxine?
Back: Paroxetine (and Fluoxetine).

Front: Enzyme induced by Carbamazepine that causes it to clear itself and other anticonvulsants faster over the first month of treatment?
Back: CYP3A4.

Front: Mood stabilizer that acts as a potent CYP3A4 inhibitor, increasing levels of Carbamazepine if co-administered?
Back: Valproate.

Front: Antibiotic class (commonly used for UTIs) that can impair renal clearance and acutely increase Lithium levels?
Back: Tetracyclines (e.g., Doxycycline).

Front: Cardiac medication used for rate control that can decrease renal blood flow and elevate Lithium concentrations?
Back: Verapamil (and other calcium channel blockers).

Front: Structural brain abnormality found on MRI in patients with severe, chronic Bipolar Disorder?
Back: Enlargement of the lateral ventricles.

Front: Functional neuroimaging finding (PET/SPECT) typically seen in the prefrontal cortex of depressed patients?
Back: Hypometabolism and decreased blood flow.

Front: Neurotropic factor found to be significantly decreased in the serum and hippocampus of patients with active MDD?
Back: Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF).

Front: First-line pharmacotherapy for a patient with severe Bipolar Mania who is also in her first trimester of pregnancy and refuses ECT?
Back: Haloperidol or a second-generation antipsychotic (e.g., Olanzapine).

Front: Next clinical step when a patient on Lithium develops rapid-onset confusion, coarse tremors, and nystagmus with a normal serum Lithium level?
Back: Check checking serum electrolytes, renal function, and re-verify the timing of the last Lithium dose relative to the blood draw.

Front: Ideal time to draw blood for a steady-state therapeutic Lithium level check?
Back: 12 hours after the last dose (trough level).

Front: Medical diagnosis to suspect when an older adult develops severe depression for the first time in life with asymmetric resting tremor and bradykinesia?
Back: Parkinson’s disease presenting with comorbid depression.

Front: Treatment of choice for "Pseudodementia" (Depression-induced cognitive impairment in the elderly)?
Back: Antidepressants (SSRIs).

Front: Key clinical history feature distinguishing Pseudodementia from true Alzheimer’s Dementia?
Back: Patients with pseudodementia typically complain loudly about their memory loss and make little effort on cognitive tests ("I don't know" answers).

Front: True or False: Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS) is indicated for patients with severe psychotic depression?
Back: False (Psychotic depression requires ECT or combination pharmacotherapy; TMS is for non-psychotic treatment-resistant depression).

Front: What is the immediate next step in management for a depressed patient who admits to having a specific, actionable plan to commit suicide?
Back: Ensure patient safety via immediate voluntary or involuntary hospitalization.

Front: True or False: If a patient with severe mania refuses hospitalization but poses an immediate danger to themselves or others, you must obtain a court order before placing an emergency hold?
Back: False (an emergency physician-initiated hold can be placed immediately; court review follows later).

Front: What is the legal doctrine that allows the state to intervene and involuntarily hospitalize a severely manic patient who is unable to provide for their own basic food, clothing, or shelter?
Back: Parens patriae (the state acting as a protector of citizens who cannot care for themselves).

Front: Next step in management if a patient with severe Bipolar I mania refuses life-saving medication during an acute involuntary hold?
Back: Administer emergency intramuscular medication (e.g., Haloperidol or Olanzapine) under emergency treatment protocols if they present an imminent threat of violence.

Front: What is the immediate next step in management for a patient on Lithium who develops a mild, fine resting tremor but has a therapeutic serum level?
Back: Reassurance, or lower the dose slightly if clinically feasible, or add a low-dose beta-blocker (e.g., Propranolol).

Front: Next step in management for a patient taking an SSRI who develops acute, severe suicidal ideation within the first two weeks of starting the medication?
Back: Discontinue or lower the dose of the SSRI, monitor closely, and ensure immediate safety/hospitalization if necessary.

Front: What is the preferred next step when a pregnant woman with Bipolar I stable on Lithium chooses to stay on her medication after a thorough risk-benefit discussion?
Back: Continue Lithium, but schedule targeted fetal echocardiography at 16 to 20 weeks of gestation to screen for Ebstein's anomaly.

Front: Next step in management for a patient with MDD who achieves full remission on an SSRI but develops severe, treatment-limiting sexual dysfunction?
Back: Switch to Bupropion, or augment the SSRI with Bupropion or Sildenafil.

Front: Next step in management for an adolescent patient diagnosed with MDD who fails to respond to cognitive behavioral therapy alone?
Back: Initiate pharmacotherapy with Fluoxetine (the SSRI with the most robust evidence and FDA approval for pediatric depression).

Front: How frequently should a patient's steady-state Lithium level be checked during the initial stabilization phase?
Back: Every 1 to 2 weeks until a stable, therapeutic level is achieved.

Front: Once a patient is on a stable, long-term maintenance dose of Lithium, how often should renal function and TSH be checked?
Back: Every 6 to 12 months.

Front: What baseline metric must be established before prescribing any second-generation antipsychotic for Bipolar depression due to metabolic risks?
Back: Fasting lipid panel, fasting blood glucose/HbA1c, body mass index (BMI), and waist circumference.

Front: How often should a complete blood count (CBC) be monitored during the first few months of starting Carbamazepine?
Back: Every 2 to 4 weeks to screen for transient or severe leukopenia.

Section:Psychiatry
Topic:Psychotic disorders — schizophrenia spectrum


Front: Core symptom domains required for a DSM-5-TR diagnosis of Schizophrenia?
Back: Delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, grossly disorganized/catatonic behavior, and negative symptoms.

Front: Minimum number of core symptoms required for Schizophrenia diagnosis?
Back: At least two core symptoms.

Front: Which specific core symptoms must include at least one to diagnose Schizophrenia?
Back: Delusions, hallucinations, or disorganized speech.

Front: Minimum duration of active-phase symptoms required for Schizophrenia?
Back: One month.

Front: Minimum total duration of continuous signs of disturbance required for Schizophrenia?
Back: Six months.

Front: What does the 6-month Schizophrenia duration requirement include?
Back: Prodromal, active-phase, and residual-phase symptoms.

Front: Define a delusion.
Back: A fixed, false belief unchanged by conflicting evidence.

Front: Define a bizarre delusion.
Back: A delusion that is clearly implausible and not understandable from shared cultural experiences.

Front: Most common type of delusion in schizophrenia spectrum disorders?
Back: Persecutory delusions.

Front: Define a delusion of reference.
Back: The belief that random, innocuous environmental cues have highly personal significance.

Front: Define thought broadcasting.
Back: The delusion that one's thoughts are being transmitted aloud to others.

Front: Define thought insertion.
Back: The delusion that outside thoughts are being placed directly into one's mind.

Front: Define thought withdrawal.
Back: The delusion that an outside force is removing thoughts from one's mind.

Front: Define Capgras syndrome.
Back: The delusion that a familiar person has been replaced by an identical imposter.

Front: Define Cotard syndrome.
Back: The nihilistic delusion that one is dead, decaying, or non-existent.

Front: Define Fregoli syndrome.
Back: The delusion that different people are actually a single person in disguise.

Front: Most common modality of hallucinations in schizophrenia?
Back: Auditory hallucinations.

Front: Two specific types of auditory hallucinations traditionally tied to schizophrenia prognosis?
Back: Voices commenting on behavior and two or more voices conversing.

Front: What should visual, olfactory, or gustatory hallucinations immediately prompt the clinician to suspect?
Back: An underlying medical, organic, or substance-induced etiology.

Front: Define formal thought disorder.
Back: Disorganized thinking inferred objectively from speech patterns.

Front: Define derailment (loose associations).
Back: Speech where ideas slip off track from one topic to an unrelated oblique topic.

Front: Define tangentiality.
Back: Replying to questions in an oblique, irrelevant, or tangential manner.

Front: Define incoherence (word salad).
Back: Speech that is linguistically disorganized and totally incomprehensible.

Front: Define neologisms.
Back: Invented words created by combining parts of existing words.

Front: Define perseveration.
Back: Persistent repetition of words, phrases, or ideas despite a change in context.

Front: Define clang associations.
Back: Choosing words based on sound or rhyming rather than semantic meaning.

Front: Define catatonia.
Back: Marked psychomotor disturbance involving decreased motor activity or excessive, purposeless activity.

Front: Define waxy flexibility in catatonia.
Back: Slight, even resistance to positioning by examiner followed by maintenance of that new posture.

Front: Define stupor in catatonia.
Back: Complete lack of psychomotor activity and unresponsiveness to the environment.

Front: Define negativism in catatonia.
Back: Motiveless resistance to all instructions or maintenance of an opposite posture.

Front: Define echolalia.
Back: Pathological repeating of another person's words.

Front: Define echopraxia.
Back: Pathological mimicking of another person's movements.

Front: List the "5 A's" of primary negative symptoms in Schizophrenia.
Back: Affective flattening, alogia, avolition, anhedonia, and asociality.

Front: Define alogia.
Back: Poverty of speech and restriction in the amount or fluency of spontaneous speech.

Front: Define avolition.
Back: An inability to initiate and persist in goal-directed activities.

Front: Impact of negative symptoms vs. positive symptoms on long-term functional outcome?
Back: Negative symptoms are much stronger predictors of poor functional outcome.

Front: Functional requirement essential for diagnosing Schizophrenia?
Back: Marked impairment in one or more major areas of functioning like work, relationships, or self-care.

Front: Lifetime prevalence of Schizophrenia globally?
Back: Approximately 1%.

Front: Male-to-female ratio of Schizophrenia prevalence?
Back: Equal (1:1), though presentation details differ.

Front: Peak age of onset of Schizophrenia in males?
Back: Early to mid-20s (18-25 years).

Front: Peak age of onset of Schizophrenia in females?
Back: Late 20s to early 30s, with a smaller bimodal peak post-menopause.

Front: Gender correlation with Schizophrenia prognosis?
Back: Females generally have a better prognosis and milder course.

Front: Concordance rate of Schizophrenia in monozygotic twins?
Back: Approximately 40% to 50%.

Front: Concordance rate of Schizophrenia in dizygotic twins?
Back: Approximately 10% to 15%.

Front: Risk of developing Schizophrenia if one parent is diagnosed?
Back: Approximately 10% to 12%.

Front: Risk of developing Schizophrenia if both parents are diagnosed?
Back: Approximately 40%.

Front: Common neurodevelopmental birth complication linked to increased risk of Schizophrenia?
Back: In utero maternal infections (e.g., influenza) or obstetric complications causing fetal hypoxia.

Front: Environmental factor involving season of birth associated with Schizophrenia?
Back: Late winter and early spring births.

Front: Socioeconomic trend where Schizophrenia is more prevalent in lower socioeconomic classes?
Back: Social drift hypothesis (downward drift due to cognitive and behavioral impairment).

Front: Urban vs. rural environment risk for Schizophrenia?
Back: Urban environments double the risk of developing the disorder.

Front: Classic substance abuse habit that carries a clear causal link to triggering psychotic episodes?
Back: Heavy cannabis use during adolescence.

Front: Dopamine hypothesis: underlying mechanism of positive symptoms?
Back: Hyperactivity of dopamine neurotransmission in the mesolimbic pathway.

Front: Dopamine hypothesis: underlying mechanism of negative and cognitive symptoms?
Back: Hypoactivity of dopamine neurotransmission in the mesocortical pathway.

Front: Glutamate hypothesis of Schizophrenia?
Back: Hypofunction of NMDA receptors on GABAergic interneurons leading to downstream dopamine dysregulation.

Front: Serotonin hypothesis contribution to Schizophrenia?
Back: 5-HT2A receptor activation modulates dopamine release, serving as the basis for atypical antipsychotic action.

Front: Structural brain imaging finding most consistently replicated in Schizophrenia?
Back: Enlargement of the lateral and third ventricles.

Front: Cortical structural brain change observed over time in Schizophrenia?
Back: Diffuse reduction in gray matter volume, most prominently in the temporal and frontal lobes.

Front: Functional neuroimaging landmark pattern seen during executive task testing in Schizophrenia?
Back: Hypofrontality (decreased metabolic activity and blood flow in the prefrontal cortex).

Front: Microscopic histopathological finding in the hippocampus of Schizophrenia patients?
Back: Cellular disorientation and disarray of pyramidal neurons.

Front: Alteration in normal adolescent brain development accelerated in Schizophrenia?
Back: Excessive or exaggerated synaptic pruning.

Front: DSM-5-TR duration criteria for Brief Psychotic Disorder?
Back: At least 1 day but less than 1 month, with eventual full return to premorbid functioning.

Front: Key specifier often associated with Brief Psychotic Disorder?
Back: With marked stressors (brief reactive psychosis).

Front: DSM-5-TR duration criteria for Schizophreniform Disorder?
Back: At least 1 month but less than 6 months.

Front: Major diagnostic distinction between Schizophreniform Disorder and Schizophrenia?
Back: Schizophreniform requires a duration under 6 months and does not mandate social/occupational impairment.

Front: Core requirement for diagnosing Schizoaffective Disorder?
Back: An uninterrupted period of illness during which there is a major mood episode concurrent with Schizophrenia Criterion A symptoms.

Front: Essential rule for differentiating Schizoaffective Disorder from Mood Disorder with Psychotic Features?
Back: Delusions or hallucinations must be present for 2 or more weeks in the absence of a major mood episode.

Front: Name the two main diagnostic subtypes of Schizoaffective Disorder.
Back: Bipolar type (if a manic or mixed episode occurs) and Depressive type.

Front: Core DSM-5-TR requirement for Delusional Disorder?
Back: Presence of one or more delusions for a duration of 1 month or longer without other Criterion A schizophrenia symptoms.

Front: Relative level of global psychosocial functioning in Delusional Disorder compared to Schizophrenia?
Back: Functioning is significantly less impaired; behavior is not obviously bizarre outside the delusion.

Front: Define Erotomanic type Delusional Disorder.
Back: The central theme of the delusion is that another person (usually of higher status) is in love with the individual.

Front: Define Grandiose type Delusional Disorder.
Back: The conviction of having some great, unrecognized talent, insight, or important discovery.

Front: Define Jealous type Delusional Disorder.
Back: The central theme is that the individual's spouse or lover is unfaithful.

Front: Define Somatic type Delusional Disorder.
Back: The central theme involves bodily functions or sensations (e.g., foul odor, parasite infestation).

Front: Define Persecutory type Delusional Disorder.
Back: The belief that the individual is being conspired against, cheated, spied on, followed, or harassed.

Front: Subcortical dementia often presenting with prominent visual hallucinations and paranoid delusions early on?
Back: Lewy Body Dementia.

Front: Common neuroendocrine emergency characterized by neuropsychiatric symptoms, visual hallucinations, and autonomic instability?
Back: Thyroid storm (severe hyperthyroidism).

Front: Autoimmune encephalitis associated with ovarian teratomas presenting with rapid-onset psychosis and catatonia?
Back: Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis.

Front: Infectious disease screening labs required for all new-onset psychosis workups?
Back: RPR/VDRL (Syphilis) and HIV serology.

Front: Vital urine laboratory screening test for any acute first-episode psychosis evaluation?
Back: Urine toxicology screen (to rule out substance-induced psychosis).

Front: Primary mechanism of action of First-Generation (Typical) Antipsychotics?
Back: Strong, competitive post-synaptic antagonism of Dopamine D2 receptors.

Front: Pathway targeted by Typical Antipsychotics to resolve positive symptoms?
Back: Mesolimbic pathway.

Front: Pathway blocked by Typical Antipsychotics that induces Extrapyramidal Symptoms (EPS)?
Back: Nigrostriatal pathway.

Front: Name two classic high-potency first-generation antipsychotics.
Back: Haloperidol and Fluphenazine.

Front: Name two classic low-potency first-generation antipsychotics.
Back: Chlorpromazine and Thioridazine.

Front: High-potency vs. low-potency first-generation antipsychotics side effect profiles?
Back: High-potency have more EPS; low-potency have more antihistaminic, anticholinergic, and alpha-1 antiadrenergic effects.

Front: Specific ophthalmic complication unique to long-term Chlorpromazine use?
Back: Corneal and lens deposits.

Front: Specific ophthalmic complication unique to long-term Thioridazine use?
Back: Retinitis pigmentosa.

Front: Four main categories of Extrapyramidal Symptoms (EPS)?
Back: Acute dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism, and tardive dyskinesia.

Front: Timeframe for development of acute dystonia?
Back: Hours to days after starting or increasing medication.

Front: Presentation of acute dystonia?
Back: Sustained, painful muscle contractions of the neck (torticollis), jaw (trismus), or eyes (oculogyric crisis).

Front: Preferred first-line pharmacological treatment for acute dystonia?
Back: Benztropine or Diphenhydramine (intravenous or intramuscular).

Front: Define akathisia.
Back: Subjective feeling of intense inner restlessness and objective inability to sit still.

Front: First-line medical treatments for akathisia?
Back: Beta-blockers (Propranolol) or low-dose Benzodiazepines.

Front: Symptoms of antipsychotic-induced parkinsonism?
Back: Bradykinesia, rigidity, masked facies, and a resting pill-rolling tremor.

Front: Pharmacological management for antipsychotic-induced parkinsonism?
Back: Benztropine or Amantadine.

Front: Why should Levodopa never be used to treat antipsychotic-induced parkinsonism?
Back: It increases dopamine levels and will severely exacerbate the underlying psychosis.

Front: Timeframe for development of Tardive Dyskinesia?
Back: Months to years of chronic antipsychotic exposure.

Front: Classic visual presentation of Tardive Dyskinesia?
Back: Involuntary, choreoathetoid movements of the tongue, face, lips (puckering), or trunk.

Front: Underlying pathophysiology of Tardive Dyskinesia?
Back: Upregulation and supersensitivity of postsynaptic D2 receptors in the nigrostriatal pathway.

Front: Initial step in managing Tardive Dyskinesia if clinically feasible?
Back: Discontinue or taper the typical antipsychotic, or switch the patient to Clozapine.

Front: FDA-approved drug class for the targeted treatment of Tardive Dyskinesia?
Back: VMAT2 inhibitors (Valbenazine or Deutetrabenazine).

Front: Pathway blocked by antipsychotics that leads to hyperprolactinemia?
Back: Tuberoinfundibular pathway.

Front: Clinical manifestations of hyperprolactinemia in males and females?
Back: Gynecomastia, galactorrhea, amenorrhea, and erectile dysfunction.

Front: Life-threatening idiosyncratic reaction to antipsychotics marked by rigidity, hyperthermia, and autonomic instability?
Back: Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS).

Front: Key laboratory marker highly elevated in Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome?
Back: Creatine Phosphokinase (CPK).

Front: First management steps for Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome?
Back: Immediately discontinue the offending agent, initiate aggressive cooling, and provide IV hydration.

Front: Two specific medications used to treat severe cases of Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome?
Back: Dantrolene (skeletal muscle relaxant) and Bromocriptine (dopamine agonist).

Front: Primary mechanism of action of Second-Generation (Atypical) Antipsychotics?
Back: Combined Dopamine D2 antagonism and Serotonin 5-HT2A receptor antagonism.

Front: Main clinical advantage of second-generation over first-generation antipsychotics?
Back: Significantly lower incidence of extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS) and tardive dyskinesia.

Front: Primary long-term metabolic risk profile of second-generation antipsychotics?
Back: Weight gain, dyslipidemia, hyperglycemia, and new-onset Type 2 Diabetes.

Front: Two atypical antipsychotics carrying the highest risk for metabolic syndrome?
Back: Olanzapine and Clozapine.

Front: Two atypical antipsychotics carrying the lowest risk for metabolic syndrome?
Back: Ziprasidone and Aripiprazole.

Front: Specific cardiac risk assessment mandatory for patients taking Ziprasidone?
Back: Electrocardiogram (EKG) monitoring for corrected QT (QTc) interval prolongation.

Front: Unique mechanism of action of Aripiprazole?
Back: Partial D2 receptor agonist, partial 5-HT1A agonist, and 5-HT2A antagonist.

Front: Specific side effect most frequently associated with Risperidone among the atypicals?
Back: Significant hyperprolactinemia, even at lower doses.

Front: Atypical antipsychotic noted for causing significant sedation and cataracts in animal studies, requiring baseline eye exams?
Back: Quetiapine.

Front: Absolute gold-standard indication for initiating Clozapine therapy?
Back: Treatment-resistant schizophrenia (failure of two separate adequate antipsychotic trials).

Front: Only antipsychotic proven to significantly reduce the risk of suicidal behavior in schizophrenia?
Back: Clozapine.

Front: Most dangerous, black-box warning hematologic side effect of Clozapine?
Back: Agranulocytosis (absolute neutrophil count drops below 500/microliter).

Front: Mandatory laboratory monitoring protocol required for prescribing Clozapine?
Back: Regular tracking of absolute neutrophil count (ANC) via the Clozapine REMS program.

Front: Severe, non-hematologic, dose-dependent neurological side effect of Clozapine?
Back: Seizures.

Front: Gastrointestinal black-box warning associated with Clozapine that can become fatal if missed?
Back: Severe gastrointestinal hypomotility (paralytic ileus/fecal impaction).

Front: Cardiovascular emergency linked to Clozapine initiation requiring monitoring of troponins and CRP?
Back: Myocarditis or cardiomyopathy.

Front: Common, bothersome nocturnal side effect reported by patients taking Clozapine?
Back: Sialorrhea (excessive drooling).

Front: Preferred medication formulation utilized to improve treatment adherence in non-compliant schizophrenia patients?
Back: Long-Acting Injectable (LAI) antipsychotics (depot formulations).

Front: Name two atypical antipsychotics available as Long-Acting Injectables (LAIs).
Back: Risperidone, Paliperidone, or Olanzapine.

Front: Dangerous medical emergency that can occur immediately post-injection with Olanzapine pamoate LAI?
Back: Post-injection delirium sedation syndrome.

Front: Define the prodromal phase of Schizophrenia.
Back: A period of subclinical decline characterized by social withdrawal, irritability, uncharacteristic odd beliefs, and poor hygiene prior to the first active psychotic episode.

Front: Single strongest overall predictor of a poor prognosis in Schizophrenia?
Back: Insidious onset during early childhood or adolescence.

Front: List three features predictive of a good prognosis in Schizophrenia.
Back: Acute onset, clear precipitating stressors, late age of onset, and prominent positive symptoms.

Front: Impact of active substance abuse on the prognosis of Schizophrenia?
Back: Substantially worsens the course, increases relapse rates, and lowers medication adherence.

Front: Leading cause of premature death in patients diagnosed with Schizophrenia?
Back: Suicide.

Front: Lifetime percentage of Schizophrenia patients who attempt suicide?
Back: Approximately 20% to 40%.

Front: Specific demographic subset of Schizophrenia patients at the highest risk for suicide?
Back: Young, highly intelligent males with high premorbid functioning who have insight into their functional decline.

Front: Primary purpose of Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Psychosis (CBTp)?
Back: To reduce the distress and functional impairment caused by persistent positive symptoms, rather than eliminating them entirely.

Front: Family dynamic factor strongly correlated with high relapse and re-hospitalization rates in Schizophrenia?
Back: High Expressed Emotion (criticism, hostility, and emotional over-involvement).

Front: Evidence-based community treatment model utilizing a multidisciplinary team to deliver 24/7 comprehensive care directly to severe schizophrenia patients in real-world settings?
Back: Assertive Community Treatment (ACT).

Front: Recommended psychosocial intervention designed to improve real-world interpersonal interactions and independence?
Back: Social Skills Training (SST).

Front: What is the primary role of electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) within the psychotic disorders spectrum?
Back: Augmented treatment for severe catatonia or treatment-resistant schizophrenia with comorbid depression.

Front: Neurological disease presenting with chorea and early psychiatric symptoms like psychosis and depression?
Back: Huntington's Disease.

Front: Trinucleotide repeat expansion mutation responsible for Huntington's Disease?
Back: CAG repeats on chromosome 4.

Front: Metabolic disorder presenting with psychosis, abdominal pain, neuropathy, and dark purple urine?
Back: Acute Intermittent Porphyria.

Front: Deficient enzyme responsible for Acute Intermittent Porphyria?
Back: Porphobilinogen deaminase.

Front: Heavy metal poisoning presenting with psychosis, a blue-gray line on the gums, and foot drop?
Back: Lead poisoning.

Front: Vitamin deficiency classically presenting with psychosis, macrocytic anemia, and subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord?
Back: Vitamin B12 (Cobalamin) deficiency.

Front: Central nervous system infection to suspect in a patient with psychosis, Argyll Robertson pupils, and loss of proprioception?
Back: Neurosyphilis (Tertiary Syphilis).

Front: Define Argyll Robertson pupil.
Back: Pupils that constrict during accommodation but do not react to light.

Front: Endocrine disorder presenting with psychosis, weight gain, coarse hair, and a delayed relaxation phase of deep tendon reflexes?
Back: Severe hypothyroidism ("myxedema madness").

Front: Illicit drug intoxication characterized by psychosis, extreme agitation, and rotary nystagmus?

Back: Phencyclidine (PCP) intoxication.

Front: Illicit drug intoxication characterized by paranoia, visual hallucinations, pupillary dilation, and tactile hallucinations like formication?

Back: Cocaine or Amphetamine intoxication.

Front: Define formication.

Back: The tactile hallucination or sensation that bugs are crawling on or under the skin.  

Front: Over-the-counter medication class that can induce severe delirium, confusion, and visual hallucinations in elderly patients?

Back: Anticholinergics (e.g., Diphenhydramine).

Front: Prescription medication class frequently causing treatment-induced psychosis or mania in patients treated for inflammatory or autoimmune conditions?

Back: Corticosteroids (e.g., Prednisone).

Front: Life-threatening withdrawal state characterized by visual/tactile hallucinations, severe tremors, and autonomic instability 48 to 96 hours after the last drink?

Back: Delirium Tremens (Alcohol withdrawal delirium).

Front: Medical emergency characterized by hyperthermia, rigidity, and hyperreflexia/clonus when mixing psychotropic medications?
Back: Serotonin Syndrome.

Front: Main clinical differentiator between Serotonin Syndrome and Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS)?
Back: Serotonin Syndrome features hyperreflexia and clonus; NMS features severe "lead-pipe" rigidity and hyporeflexia.

Front: Antipsychotic medication that requires a major dose reduction if the patient completely stops smoking tobacco cigarettes?
Back: Clozapine (or Olanzapine).

Front: Why does stopping smoking alter Clozapine metabolism?
Back: Tobacco smoke induces the CYP1A2 enzyme; stopping smoke expression rapidly increases Clozapine blood concentrations.

Front: Atypical antipsychotic that is the active metabolite of Risperidone?
Back: Paliperidone.

Front: Atypical antipsychotic that must always be taken with food to ensure adequate gastrointestinal absorption?
Back: Ziprasidone (requires at least 500 calories) or Lurasidone (requires at least 350 calories).

Front: Mechanism by which low-potency antipsychotics cause severe orthostatic hypotension?
Back: Antagonism of peripheral alpha-1 adrenergic receptors.

Front: Baseline metabolic tracking labs required prior to initiating any Second-Generation Antipsychotic?
Back: Fasting lipid panel, fasting blood glucose (or HbA1c), weight/BMI, and blood pressure.

Front: Follow-up timeframe recommended by the ADA for checking lipids and blood glucose after starting an atypical antipsychotic?
Back: At 12 weeks, and then annually thereafter.

Front: Quantitative QTc interval threshold on an EKG where antipsychotics should typically be held or discontinued?
Back: A QTc interval greater than 500 milliseconds.

Front: Severe cardiac arrhythmia that a prolonged QTc interval predisposes a patient to develop?
Back: Torsades de Pointes.

Front: Baseline laboratory test required before starting Clozapine to rule out benign baseline patterns?
Back: Baseline Absolute Neutrophil Count (ANC).

Front: Absolute Neutrophil Count (ANC) cutoff value below which Clozapine must be immediately discontinued?
Back: An ANC less than 1,000/microliter (or less than 500/microliter in verified benign ethnic neutropenia).

Front: Define the "Deficit Syndrome" variant of Schizophrenia.
Back: A subtype characterized by severe, enduring, primary negative symptoms that persist even during periods of clinical stability.

Front: Association between the Deficit Syndrome and overall long-term prognosis?
Back: It carries a significantly poorer prognosis and is highly resistant to standard antipsychotic treatments.

Front: What is the diagnostic significance of "Post-psychotic Depression" in Schizophrenia?
Back: A major depressive episode that emerges during the residual phase of schizophrenia, carrying an exceptionally high risk for suicide.

Front: Electroencephalogram (EEG) changes typically observed in patients with chronic Schizophrenia?
Back: Non-specific focal abnormalities, localized slowing, and decreased alpha wave activity.

Front: Polysomnography (Sleep Study) architectural changes characteristically seen in Schizophrenia?
Back: Decreased slow-wave sleep (Stage N3) and shortened REM latency.

Front: Neurological soft signs commonly found on physical exam in patients with schizophrenia spectrum disorders?
Back: Impaired left-right confusion, poor stereognosis, agraphesthesia, and dysdiadochokinesia.

Front: Define stereognosis.
Back: The ability to perceive and recognize the form of an object in the absence of visual and auditory information, by using tactile sensations.

Front: Define agraphesthesia.
Back: The inability to recognize symbols, letters, or numbers traced on the skin.

Front: Eye-tracking physical exam abnormality heavily studied and linked as an endophenotype for Schizophrenia?
Back: Impairment in smooth-pursuit eye movements.

Front: Clinical strategy used when switching a patient from a high-metabolic atypical antipsychotic to a low-metabolic one?
Back: Cross-titration (gradually tapering the old medication while slowly uptitrating the new one over weeks).

Front: Why is an immediate, abrupt switch from Olanzapine to Aripiprazole dangerous?
Back: It can cause a rebound cholinergic crisis or an acute exacerbation of psychosis due to sudden D2 receptor unbinding.

Front: Symptoms of a rebound cholinergic crisis following abrupt withdrawal of low-potency or highly sedating antipsychotics?
Back: Diaphoresis, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and severe insomnia.

Front: Antipsychotic medication that possesses the unique property of having a highly prolonged half-life, making missing occasional doses less critical?
Back: Cariprazine.

Front: Atypical antipsychotic noted for a very low affinity for D2 receptors and rapid dissociation, making it ideal for treating psychosis in Parkinson's Disease?
Back: Pimavanserin (or low-dose Quetiapine).

Front: Mechanism of action of Pimavanserin?
Back: Inverse agonist and antagonist at Serotonin 5-HT2A receptors, completely lacking dopamine D2 blockade.

Front: Drug-drug interaction risk when combining an atypical antipsychotic with a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor like Clarithromycin or Ketoconazole?
Back: Substantially increased antipsychotic serum levels, markedly elevating the risk of QTc prolongation and toxicity.

Front: Drug-drug interaction risk when combining Clozapine with Carbamazepine?
Back: Synergistic bone marrow suppression, exponentially increasing the risk of life-threatening agranulocytosis.

Front: Serious respiratory side effect of high-dose low-potency typical antipsychotics used in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis?
Back: Increased risk of aspiration pneumonia due to impaired swallowing reflexes.

Front: Black Box warning shared by all antipsychotics regarding their use in elderly patients with dementia-related behavioral disturbances?
Back: Increased risk of all-cause mortality, primarily due to cardiovascular events (stroke, heart failure) or infectious complications.

Front: Primary legal justification allowing a physician to place a patient on an involuntary psychiatric hold?
Back: The presence of a mental illness causing the patient to be an imminent danger to themselves, an imminent danger to others, or gravely disabled.

Front: Does an involuntary psychiatric hold automatically give the physician the right to force medications against the patient's will?
Back: No, an involuntary hold and the right to refuse medication are legally distinct entities requiring separate legal or administrative review.

Front: Landmark legal case establishing that a psychiatrist has a "duty to protect" third parties from a patient's violent threats?
Back: Tarasoff v. Regents of the University of California.

Front: Next immediate step if a patient with active command auditory hallucinations to harm a named individual demands to sign out of the hospital?
Back: Deny the request, initiate an involuntary hold, and notify both law enforcement and the intended victim.

Front: Contrast medical "competence" versus medical "capacity".
Back: Competence is a legal determination made exclusively by a judge; capacity is a clinical assessment made by a treating physician.

Front: Four foundational elements required to determine that a psychiatric patient has decision-making capacity?
Back: The ability to communicate a clear choice, understand the relevant medical facts, appreciate the clinical consequences, and rationally manipulate the information.

Section: Psychiatry
Topic: Anxiety disorders — GAD, panic, PTSD, OCD

Front: Minimum duration of symptoms required for a DSM-5-TR diagnosis of Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)?
Back: Six months.

Front: Number of associated somatic symptoms required for adults to diagnose GAD under DSM-5-TR?
Back: At least three symptoms.

Front: Number of associated somatic symptoms required for children to diagnose GAD under DSM-5-TR?
Back: Only one symptom.

Front: List the six DSM-5-TR somatic symptoms associated with GAD.
Back: Restlessness, fatigue, difficulty concentrating, irritability, muscle tension, and sleep disturbance.

Front: Pathophysiological brain region showing hyperactivation in GAD during emotional processing?
Back: Amygdala.

Front: Pathophysiological brain region showing structural and functional hypoactivation in GAD?
Back: Prefrontal cortex.

Front: First-line pharmacotherapy classes for the long-term management of GAD?
Back: SSRIs and SNRIs.

Front: Non-benzodiazepine anxiolytic that acts as a 5-HT1A receptor partial agonist?
Back: Buspirone.

Front: Timeframe required for buspirone to achieve therapeutic efficacy in GAD?
Back: Two to four weeks.

Front: Primary clinical limitation of using benzodiazepines in the long-term management of GAD?
Back: Risk of physiological dependence, tolerance, and withdrawal.

Front: First-line, evidence-based psychotherapy modality for GAD?
Back: Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT).

Front: Alpha-2-delta ligand anticonvulsant frequently used off-label as a highly effective agent for GAD?
Back: Pregabalin.

Front: Common beta-blocker used primarily for the autonomic, somatic symptoms of performance anxiety rather than generalized anxiety?
Back: Propranolol.

Front: Minimum frequency and nature of panic attacks required for Panic Disorder diagnosis?
Back: Recurrent and unexpected panic attacks.

Front: Timeframe and criteria required following a panic attack to meet Panic Disorder diagnosis?
Back: One month or more of persistent worry about future attacks or maladaptive behavioral changes.

Front: Minimum number of physical or cognitive symptoms required to define a distinct panic attack?
Back: Four symptoms.

Front: List four cognitive symptoms that can count toward a DSM-5 panic attack definition.
Back: Fear of losing control, fear of going crazy, derealization/depersonalization, and fear of dying.

Front: Define agoraphobia according to the DSM-5-TR.
Back: Marked fear or anxiety about two or more public situations where escape might be difficult.

Front: Neuroanatomical structure considered the "panic center" or primary source of norepinephrine in panic attacks?
Back: Locus coeruleus.

Front: Acute, immediate pharmacological intervention for a patient experiencing a severe, disabling panic attack?
Back: Short-acting benzodiazepines (e.g., Alprazolam, Lorazepam).

Front: Gold-standard, first-line long-term maintenance pharmacotherapy for Panic Disorder?
Back: SSRIs (started at low doses to avoid initial activation/anxiety).

Front: Specific CBT technique involving deliberate exposure to feared bodily sensations to treat panic disorder?
Back: Interoceptive exposure.

Front: Medical emergency or toxic state that can perfectly mimic a panic attack and must be ruled out via TSH?
Back: Hyperthyroidism.

Front: Substance abuse/withdrawal state that can acutely precipitate panic attacks?
Back: Caffeine intoxication or Alcohol withdrawal.

Front: Acid-base disturbance characteristically seen on an ABG during an acute panic attack due to hyperventilation?
Back: Respiratory alkalosis.

Front: Minimum age required to apply standard DSM-5-TR PTSD criteria?
Back: Six years of age.

Front: Minimum symptom duration required for a diagnosis of Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)?
Back: One month.

Front: Diagnosis given if PTSD-like symptoms present after a trauma but resolve within one month?
Back: Acute Stress Disorder (ASD).

Front: Minimum and maximum timeframe for a diagnosis of Acute Stress Disorder?
Back: Three days to one month following trauma exposure.

Front: Four core symptom clusters required for the diagnosis of PTSD?
Back: Intrusion, Avoidance, Negative alterations in cognition/mood, and Alterations in arousal/reactivity.

Front: Neuroendocrine abnormality characteristically seen in the HPA axis of PTSD patients?
Back: Hypocortisolemia due to enhanced glucocorticoid receptor sensitivity.

Front: Structural brain changes classically observed on neuroimaging in chronic PTSD patients?
Back: Decreased hippocampal volume.

Front: Only two FDA-approved SSRIs for the treatment of PTSD?
Back: Sertraline and Paroxetine.

Front: Alpha-1 adrenergic antagonist used specifically to target trauma-related nightmares and sleep disruption in PTSD?
Back: Prazosin.

Front: Most prominent side effect of Prazosin that requires nighttime titration?
Back: Orthostatic hypotension.

Front: Preferred specialized trauma-focused psychotherapies for PTSD?
Back: Prolonged Exposure (PE) and Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR).

Front: Class of medications strictly avoided or contraindicated in PTSD due to high substance abuse comorbidity and lack of efficacy?
Back: Benzodiazepines.

Front: Timeframe required to diagnose an Adjustment Disorder following an identifiable stressor?
Back: Development within three months of the stressor, resolving within six months of stressor termination.

Back: OCD with good or fair insight.

Front: DSM-5-TR specifier for OCD when an individual thinks their OCD beliefs are probably true?
Back: OCD with poor insight.

Front: DSM-5-TR specifier for OCD when an individual is completely convinced that their OCD beliefs are true?
Back: OCD with absent insight/delusional beliefs.

Front: DSM-5-TR specifier for OCD when an individual has a lifetime history of a chronic tic disorder?
Back: OCD with tic-related specifier.

Front: Neurotransmitter theory regarding GABA activity in patients with Generalized Anxiety Disorder?

Back: Decreased GABA-A receptor binding or overall GABAergic hypoactivity.

Front: Neurotransmitter abnormality involving serotonin ($5\text{-HT}$) and norepinephrine ($\text{NE}$) in GAD?

Back: Dysregulation and imbalance of both serotonin and norepinephrine pathways.

Front: Brain pathway that exhibits structural abnormalities in neuroimaging studies of Panic Disorder?

Back: Amygdala-prefrontal cortex fear circuit.

Front: Infusion of which substance can reliably precipitate a panic attack in a susceptible Panic Disorder patient in a laboratory setting?

Back: Sodium lactate or carbon dioxide ($CO_2$) inhalation.

Front: Physiological explanation for why sodium lactate triggers panic attacks?

Back: It induces metabolic alkalosis and alters central cheoreceptor sensitivity in the brainstem.

Front: Neurotransmitter changes found in the cerebrospinal fluid ($\text{CSF}$) of patients with chronic, severe PTSD?

Back: Increased norepinephrine concentrations and decreased serotonin metabolites.

Front: Functional neuroimaging finding in PTSD regarding the activity of the medial prefrontal cortex?

Back: Hypoactivity, leading to a failure to top-down inhibit an overactive amygdala.

Front: Neuroanatomical structure responsible for contextualizing memories that is functionally impaired in PTSD?

Back: Hippocampus.

Front: Neurotransmitter pathway targeted by the primary, first-line pharmacological agents used to treat OCD?

Back: Serotonergic pathway.

Front: Neuroimaging finding regarding glucose metabolism in the orbitofrontal cortex of untreated OCD patients?

Back: Hypermetabolism or hyperactivation.

Front: Function of the caudate nucleus within the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical ($\text{CSTC}$) circuit that is faulty in OCD?

Back: Failure to filter out or "gate" intrusive, anxiety-provoking thoughts from reaching the cerebral cortex?

Front: Primary mechanism of action of Buspirone at the molecular level?

Back: Partial agonist at presynaptic and postsynaptic $5\text{-HT}_{1\text{A}}$ serotonin receptors.

Front: Why does Buspirone lack abuse potential compared to benzodiazepines?

Back: It does not modulate the GABA receptor complex and has no dopaminergic reward pathway activity.

Front: Mechanism of action of Hydroxyzine when used for acute anxiety management?

Back: Potent histamine $\text{H}_1$ receptor antagonist with mild $5\text{-HT}_{2}$ antagonist properties.

Front: Molecular target of benzodiazepines on the $\text{GABA}\text{-A}$ receptor complex?

Back: Allosteric binding site between the alpha and gamma subunits.

Front: Immediate intracellular effect of benzodiazepine binding to the $\text{GABA}\text{-A}$ receptor?

Back: Increased frequency of chloride channel opening, leading to hyperpolarization of the neuron.

Front: Molecular target of barbiturates on the $\text{GABA}\text{-A}$ receptor compared to benzodiazepines?

Back: Allosteric binding site that increases the duration of chloride channel opening.

Front: Receptor affinity responsible for the prominent weight gain and sedation caused by Mirtazapine?

Back: High affinity antagonism at the histamine $\text{H}_1$ receptor.

Front: Why does Mirtazapine cause less sexual dysfunction compared to standard SSRIs?

Back: It blocks $5\text{-HT}_2$ and $5\text{-HT}_3$ receptors, diverting serotonin selectively to $5\text{-HT}_1$ receptors.

Front: Mechanism of action of Clomipramine that distinguishes it from other tricyclic antidepressants ($\text{TCAs}$)?

Back: Exceptionally high selectivity and potency for inhibiting the serotonin reuptake transporter ($\text{SERT}$).

Front: Serious cardiovascular risk associated with an overdose of Clomipramine or any other TCA?

Back: Sodium channel blockade leading to QRS prolongation, cardiac arrhythmias, and seizures.

Front: Primary antidote for a tricyclic antidepressant ($\text{TCA}$) overdose presenting with a widened QRS complex?

Back: Intravenous sodium bicarbonate.

Front: Mechanism of action of Prazosin in treating PTSD-related nightmares?

Back: Competitive antagonist at central alpha-1 adrenergic receptors, reducing central sympathetic outflow.

Front: Pharmacological rationale for using atypical antipsychotics like Risperidone as augmentation in OCD?

Back: $5\text{-HT}_{2\text{A}}$ receptor antagonism modulates downstream dopamine and glutamate release in the $\text{CSTC}$ circuit.

Front: SSRI discontinuation syndrome is most acutely severe with which medication due to its lack of active metabolites?

Back: Paroxetine.

Front: List three common symptoms of SSRI discontinuation syndrome.

Back: Flu-like symptoms, irritability, dizziness, paresthesias described as "brain zaps," and vivid dreams.

Front: Life-threatening symptom complex seen in severe Serotonin Syndrome that distinguishes it from Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome ($\text{NMS}$)?

Back: Neuromuscular hyperactivity manifesting as hyperreflexia and clonus.

Front: Pharmacological antidote used for severe, refractory cases of Serotonin Syndrome?

Back: Cyproheptadine.

Front: Mechanism of action of Cyproheptadine?

Back: First-generation antihistamine with potent $5\text{-HT}_1$ and $5\text{-HT}_2$ receptor antagonist properties.

Back: Prolongation of the QTc interval leading to Torsades de Pointes.

Front: First-line pharmacological management for a patient presenting with an acute, unstable aortic dissection secondary to cocaine use?
Back: Intravenous Sodium Nitroprusside or Phentolamine combined with Benzodiazepines.

Front: Preferred alternative medication for acute opioid withdrawal if a patient develops severe bradycardia or hypotension from Clonidine?
Back: Oral Methadone or Buprenorphine induction (if appropriate) or supportive symptomatic medications like Loperamide and Acetaminophen.

Front: Clinical phenomenon where an alcohol withdrawal patient's tremor worsens drastically during active movement or targeted tasks?
Back: Intention tremor secondary to chronic alcohol-induced cerebellar degeneration.

Front: Specific region of the cerebellum that undergoes selective atrophy in chronic, heavy alcohol users?
Back: The anterior vermis of the cerebellum.

Front: Evidence-based behavioral management strategy that provides tangible rewards (e.g., vouchers) for verified negative drug screens?
Back: Contingency Management.

Front: Primary objective of the "Harm Reduction" model of addiction treatment?
Back: Minimizing the negative health and social consequences of substance use without strictly requiring immediate total abstinence.

Front: Standard peer-led, 12-step recovery program utilized worldwide for Alcohol Use Disorder support?
Back: Alcoholics Anonymous (AA).

Front: Core psychological concept of 12-step programs regarding control over addiction?
Back: Admission of powerlessness over the substance and surrender to a higher power or group structure.

Front: Mandatory reporting requirement for a medical student who suspects a resident or attending physician is practicing while acutely intoxicated?
Back: Reporting directly to the hospital’s Physician Health Program (PHP) or the state medical board to ensure patient safety.

Front: Genetic polymorphism linked to a significantly decreased risk of developing Alcohol Use Disorder due to severe flushing reactions?

Back: Homozygous $ALDH2*2$ allele mutation (inactive aldehyde dehydrogenase).

Front: Specific brain region responsible for the intense cue-induced cravings and relapse vulnerability seen in chronic substance users?

Back: The basolateral amygdala and prefrontal cortex.

Front: Neurotransmitters whose depletion is responsible for the profound "washed-out" lethargy and depression following a multi-day methamphetamine binge?

Back: Total depletion of central intracellular Dopamine and Serotonin stores.

Front: Structural adaptation in cardiac tissue directly responsible for sudden cardiac death in chronic cocaine users without an acute myocardial infarction?

Back: Focal myocardial fibrosis and contraction band necrosis.

Front: Patient with severe AUD presents with vomiting, a high anion gap metabolic acidosis, but a normal or low serum glucose level. Diagnosis?
Back: Alcoholic Ketoacidosis (AKA).

Front: Primary pathophysiological driver of Alcoholic Ketoacidosis?
Back: Glycogen depletion combined with a high NADH/NAD+ ratio that shifts metabolism toward ketone body production.

Front: Immediate initial medical treatment required for a patient in acute Alcoholic Ketoacidosis?
Back: Intravenous infusion of normal saline and dextrose (glucose) without the immediate need for insulin.

Front: Patient with a history of intravenous drug use presents with acute lower back pain, high fever, and focal spinal tenderness. Diagnosis?
Back: Spinal Epidural Abscess.

Front: Immediate diagnostic modality of choice for a suspected spinal epidural abscess in an injection drug user?
Back: Emergency MRI of the spine with gadolinium contrast.

Front: Chronic stimulant user presents with severe, diffuse abdominal pain out of proportion to physical exam findings following an intense binge. Diagnosis?
Back: Acute mesenteric ischemia secondary to cocaine-induced vasoconstriction.

Section: Psychiatry
Topic: Personality disorders


Front: What is the core definition of a personality disorder according to the DSM-5-TR?

Back: An enduring pattern of inner experience and behavior that deviates markedly from cultural expectations, is pervasive and inflexible, and leads to distress or impairment.

Front: At what developmental stage must the features of a personality disorder typically manifest?

Back: They must trace back at least to adolescence or early adulthood.

Front: Why can a personality disorder generally not be diagnosed before the age of 18?

Back: Personality traits are still developing and may represent transient developmental phases.

Front: What is the single exception allowing a personality disorder diagnosis in individuals under 18?

Back: The traits must be present for at least one year, and it cannot be Antisocial Personality Disorder.

Front: What does it mean for a personality disorder to be "ego-syntonic"?

Back: The individual perceives their behavior as natural, correct, and acceptable, rather than viewing it as a problem.

Front: What does it mean for a condition to be "ego-dystonic"?

Back: The symptoms are perceived by the individual as unacceptable, alien, and inconsistent with their self-concept.

Front: How do defense mechanisms in personality disorders differ from those in acute Axis I disorders?

Back: They are chronically deployed, deeply ingrained, and serve as the individual's primary way of navigating the world.

Front: Which three personality disorders comprise Cluster A?

Back: Paranoid, Schizoid, and Schizotypal personality disorders.

Front: What is the overarching descriptor commonly used for Cluster A disorders?

Back: Odd or eccentric.

Front: Which four personality disorders comprise Cluster B?

Back: Antisocial, Borderline, Histrionic, and Narcissistic personality disorders.

Front: What is the overarching descriptor commonly used for Cluster B disorders?

Back: Dramatic, emotional, or erratic.

Front: Which three personality disorders comprise Cluster C?

Back: Avoidant, Dependent, and Obsessive-Compulsive personality disorders.

Front: What is the overarching descriptor commonly used for Cluster C disorders?

Back: Anxious or fearful.

Front: What diagnostic category is used when a patient meets general criteria for a personality disorder but not for a specific cluster?

Back: Other Specified or Unspecified Personality Disorder.

Front: What is the central feature of Paranoid Personality Disorder?

Back: A pervasive, unjustified distrust and suspiciousness of others such that their motives are interpreted as malevolent.

Front: How does a patient with Paranoid Personality Disorder view benign remarks or events?

Back: They hidden demeaning or threatening meanings into benign remarks or events.

Front: How do patients with Paranoid Personality Disorder typically handle perceived slights or injuries?

Back: They persistently bear grudges and are unforgiving of insults or injuries.

Front: What is the PPD patient's typical attitude regarding the fidelity of their spouse or sexual partner?

Back: They harbor recurrent, unjustified suspicions regarding the fidelity of their partner.

Front: Why do individuals with Paranoid Personality Disorder reluctantly confide in others?

Back: They fear the information will be used maliciously against them.

Front: What distinguishes Paranoid Personality Disorder from Schizophrenia?

Back: PPD lacks fixed, unshakeable delusions, hallucinations, and formal thought disorder.

Front: Which defense mechanism is most prominently utilized by individuals with Paranoid Personality Disorder?

Back: Projection, where they attribute their own unacknowledged hostile feelings to others.

Front: What is the primary psychological barrier to treating a patient with Paranoid Personality Disorder?

Back: Their profound mistrust makes establishing a therapeutic alliance exceptionally difficult.

Front: What is the core characteristic of Schizoid Personality Disorder?

Back: A pervasive pattern of detachment from social relationships and a restricted range of emotional expression.

Front: How do individuals with Schizoid Personality Disorder view close relationships, including being part of a family?

Back: They neither desire nor enjoy close relationships.

Front: What type of activities do individuals with Schizoid Personality Disorder almost always choose?

Back: Solitary activities.

Front: How do individuals with Schizoid Personality Disorder typically respond to praise or criticism?

Back: They appear indifferent to both praise and criticism from others.

Front: What is the typical sexual history or interest level of a person with Schizoid Personality Disorder?

Back: They have little, if any, interest in having sexual experiences with another person.

Front: How is the affect of a patient with Schizoid Personality Disorder usually described?

Back: Emotional coldness, detachment, or flattened affectivity.

Front: What distinguishes Schizoid Personality Disorder from Avoidant Personality Disorder?

Back: Schizoid individuals prefer isolation, whereas Avoidant individuals desire relationships but fear rejection.

Front: What distinguishes Schizoid Personality Disorder from Schizotypal Personality Disorder?

Back: Schizoid lacks the cognitive, perceptual distortions, and eccentricities seen in Schizotypal.

Front: What is the definitive presentation of Schizotypal Personality Disorder?

Back: A pattern of acute discomfort in close relationships, cognitive or perceptual distortions, and eccentricities of behavior.

Front: What are "ideas of reference" as frequently seen in Schizotypal Personality Disorder?

Back: Incorrect interpretations that innocuous, casual external events have highly personally significant meanings.

Front: What kind of odd beliefs or magical thinking influence the behavior of an STPD patient?

Back: Beliefs inconsistent with subcultural norms, such as clairvoyance, telepathy, or a "sixth sense."

Front: What type of unusual perceptual experiences might a Schizotypal patient report?

Back: Bodily illusions or sensing the presence of a person or force that is not actually there.

Front: How is the speech of a Schizotypal patient typically characterized?

Back: Odd, vague, circumstantial, metaphorical, overelaborate, or stereotyped speech.

Front: What type of anxiety do Schizotypal individuals experience in social situations that does not diminish with familiarity?

Back: Severe social anxiety that tends to be associated with paranoid fears rather than negative judgments about self.

Front: Schizotypal Personality Disorder is genetically linked to which major psychiatric axis I disorder?

Back: Schizophrenia.

Front: What medication class can be used in low doses to treat transient psychotic episodes or severe anxiety in STPD patients?

Back: Low-dose atypical antipsychotics.

Front: What is the essential feature of Antisocial Personality Disorder?

Back: A pervasive pattern of disregard for, and violation of, the rights of others occurring since age 15.

Front: What childhood diagnosis is a strict prerequisite for diagnosing Antisocial Personality Disorder?

Back: Conduct Disorder, with onset before age 15.

Front: What is the minimum age at which Antisocial Personality Disorder can be officially diagnosed?

Back: 18 years old.

Front: How do individuals with Antisocial Personality Disorder typically behave regarding social norms and laws?

Back: They repeatedly perform acts that are grounds for arrest due to a failure to conform to lawful behaviors.

Front: What is the characteristic trait of ASPD involving a complete lack of remorse?

Back: They are indifferent to, or rationalize, having hurt, mistreated, or stolen from another person.

Front: How does impulsivity manifest behaviorally in individuals with Antisocial Personality Disorder?

Back: A failure to plan ahead, frequent job changes, and sudden, unprovoked relocations.

Front: What physical behavior is frequently exhibited by individuals with Antisocial Personality Disorder?

Back: Irritability and aggressiveness, as indicated by repeated physical fights or assaults.

Front: What is the primary secondary gain sought by patients with Antisocial Personality Disorder when interacting with healthcare systems?

Back: Obtaining substances, avoiding legal prosecution, or securing disability benefits.

Front: What happens to the severity of criminal or antisocial behaviors as an ASPD patient reaches middle age?

Back: The overt, aggressive antisocial behaviors often decrease or remit as they grow older.

Front: What is the core definition of Borderline Personality Disorder?

Back: A pervasive pattern of instability in interpersonal relationships, self-image, affects, and marked impulsivity.

Front: What frantic efforts do individuals with Borderline Personality Disorder make?

Back: Frantic efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment.

Front: What is the characteristic pattern of interpersonal relationships in BPD?

Back: Unstable and intense relationships alternating between extremes of idealization and devaluation.

Front: What defense mechanism is classically defined by alternating between total idealization and total devaluation?

Back: Splitting.

Front: What is the nature of identity disturbance in Borderline Personality Disorder?

Back: A markedly and persistently unstable self-image or sense of self.

Front: In what areas must a BPD patient show potentially self-damaging impulsivity?

Back: Spending, sex, substance abuse, reckless driving, or binge eating.

Front: What recurrent behaviors are highly characteristic of crisis presentations in BPD patients?

Back: Recurrent suicidal behavior, gestures, threats, or self-mutilating acts like cutting.

Front: What chronic internal feeling is frequently voiced by individuals with Borderline Personality Disorder?

Back: A chronic feeling of emptiness.

Front: What happens to a BPD patient's cognitive state during periods of extreme, acute stress?

Back: They may experience transient, stress-related paranoid ideation or severe dissociative symptoms.

Front: What is the gold-standard, evidence-based psychotherapy modality developed specifically for BPD?

Back: Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT).

Front: What are the four core skill modules taught to patients in Dialectical Behavior Therapy?

Back: Mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness.

Front: Why should long-term benzodiazapine prescriptions be strictly avoided in BPD patients?

Back: They can cause behavioral disinhibition and carry a high risk for lethal overdose during impulsive gestures.

Front: What is the primary characteristic of Histrionic Personality Disorder?

Back: A pervasive pattern of excessive emotionality and attention-seeking behavior.

Front: How do individuals with Histrionic Personality Disorder feel when they are not the center of attention?

Back: Uncomfortable, unappreciated, or completely unfulfilled.

Front: What is the nature of the interpersonal interactions initiated by individuals with Histrionic Personality Disorder?

Back: Interactions are frequently characterized by inappropriate sexually seductive or provocative behavior.

Front: How is the expression of emotion described in a patient with Histrionic Personality Disorder?

Back: Rapidly shifting and shallow.

Front: What physical means do Histrionic individuals consistently use to draw attention to themselves?

Back: They consistently use their physical appearance to attract attention.

Front: How is the speech style of a patient with Histrionic Personality Disorder best described?

Back: Excessively impressionistic and strikingly lacking in detail.

Front: What is the nature of the emotional expression of someone with Histrionic Personality Disorder?

Back: Highly dramatic, theatrical, and exaggerated.

Front: How suggestible are individuals with Histrionic Personality Disorder?

Back: They are highly suggestible and easily influenced by others or by current fads.

Front: How do Histrionic individuals typically misjudge the depth of their relationships?

Back: They consider relationships to be much more intimate than they actually are.

Front: What is the essential feature of Narcissistic Personality Disorder?

Back: A pervasive pattern of grandiosity, a constant need for admiration, and a profound lack of empathy.

Front: With what types of fantasies are individuals with Narcissistic Personality Disorder preoccupied?

Back: Fantasies of unlimited success, power, brilliance, beauty, or ideal love.

Front: Who do individuals with Narcissistic Personality Disorder believe they should associate with?

Back: Other special, unique, or high-status people and institutions.

Front: What baseline entitlement do individuals with Narcissistic Personality Disorder possess?

Back: Unreasonable expectations of especially favorable treatment or automatic compliance with their expectations.

Front: How do individuals with Narcissistic Personality Disorder utilize other people in relationships?

Back: They are interpersonally exploitative, taking advantage of others to achieve their own ends.

Front: What core human capacity is notably absent or deficient in Narcissistic Personality Disorder?

Back: Empathy, leading to an unwillingness to recognize or identify with the feelings and needs of others.

Front: What emotion do individuals with NPD frequently experience toward others, or believe others experience toward them?

Back: Intense envy.

Front: What underlying psychological vulnerability exists beneath the grandiose exterior of an NPD patient?

Back: Fragile self-esteem that renders them highly vulnerable to "narcissistic injury" from criticism.

Front: What is the core presentation of Avoidant Personality Disorder?

Back: A pervasive pattern of social inhibition, feelings of inadequacy, and hypersensitivity to negative evaluation.

Front: Why do individuals with Avoidant Personality Disorder avoid occupational activities that involve significant interpersonal contact?

Back: Because of profound fears of criticism, disapproval, or rejection.

Front: What is the strict prerequisite for an avoidant individual to get involved with other people?

Back: They must be completely certain of being liked before entering a relationship.

Front: What holds individuals with Avoidant Personality Disorder back in intimate relationships?

Back: A fear of being shamed, ridiculed, or rejected.

Front: How do individuals with Avoidant Personality Disorder view themselves socially?

Back: As socially inept, personally unappealing, or inherently inferior to others.

Front: Why are avoidant individuals unusually reluctant to take personal risks or engage in new activities?

Back: Because such activities may prove embarrassing or confirm their feelings of inadequacy.

Front: What major Axis I anxiety disorder shares a massive clinical and genetic overlap with Avoidant Personality Disorder?

Back: Social Anxiety Disorder (Generalized Type).

Front: What medication class can be used to treat the severe physiological symptoms of social anxiety in AVPD?

Back: Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs) or beta-blockers.

Front: What is the definitive feature of Dependent Personality Disorder?

Back: A pervasive and excessive need to be taken care of that leads to submissive and clinging behavior and fears of separation.

Front: Why do individuals with Dependent Personality Disorder struggle to make everyday decisions?

Back: They require an excessive amount of advice and reassurance from others to make choices.

Front: What do dependent individuals need others to do regarding major areas of their life?

Back: They need others to assume responsibility for most major areas of their life.

Front: Why do individuals with Dependent Personality Disorder rarely express disagreement with others?

Back: Because of a profound fear of losing support, approval, or relationship connection.

Front: Why do dependent individuals struggle to initiate projects or do things on their own?

Back: Due to a lack of self-confidence in their judgment or abilities rather than a lack of motivation or energy.

Front: To what lengths will a person with Dependent Personality Disorder go to obtain nurture and support?

Back: They will go to excessive lengths, including volunteering to do things that are unpleasant or degrading.

Front: How do individuals with Dependent Personality Disorder react when a close relationship ends?

Back: They urgently seek another relationship as a source of care and support.

Front: With what terrifying internal belief are dependent individuals preoccupied?

Back: Unrealistic fears of being left to take care of themselves.

Front: What is the core definition of Obsessive-Compulsive Personality Disorder?

Back: A pervasive pattern of preoccupation with orderliness, perfectionism, and mental and interpersonal control, at the expense of flexibility, openness, and efficiency.

Front: What is the paradoxical result of an OCPD patient's extreme preoccupation with details, rules, lists, and schedules?

Back: The major point of the activity is frequently lost, and efficiency is severely compromised.

Front: How does perfectionism specifically interfere with task completion in OCPD patients?

Back: Tasks are left unfinished because their own overly strict standards are not met.

Front: To what extent do individuals with OCPD devote themselves to work and productivity?

Back: They dedicate themselves excessively to work to the total exclusion of leisure activities and friendships.

Front: What is the OCPD patient's attitude regarding morality, ethics, or values?

Back: They are overly conscientious, scrupulous, and completely inflexible.

Front: Why are individuals with OCPD highly reluctant to delegate tasks or work with others?

Back: Unless others agree to submit exactly to their specific way of doing things, they prefer to do it all themselves.

Front: What is the OCPD patient's spending style regarding both self and others?

Back: A miserly spending style where money is viewed as something to be hoarded for future catastrophes.

Front: What physical items do individuals with OCPD typically struggle to discard?

Back: Worn-out or worthless objects, even when they have absolutely no sentimental value.

Front: What is the critical clinical difference between OCD and OCPD?

Back: OCD is characterized by true, ego-dystonic obsessions and compulsions, whereas OCPD is an ego-syntonic lifestyle of perfectionism.

Front: Is there any FDA-approved medication specifically indicated for the primary treatment of any personality disorder?

Back: No medication is FDA-approved for treating personality disorders; pharmacotherapy targets comorbid symptoms or clusters.

Front: According to Stahl’s Essential Psychopharmacology, what symptom dimension responds best to low-dose antipsychotics across all clusters?

Back: Cognitive-perceptual symptoms, such as transient paranoia, suspiciousness, or illusions.

Front: Which medication class is preferred for managing affective dysregulation and impulsivity in Cluster B disorders?

Back: SSRIs or other mood stabilizers like lamotrigine and valproate.

Front: What is the therapeutic risk of using tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) in patients with Borderline Personality Disorder?

Back: High toxicity and lethality in acute, impulsive overdose scenarios.

Front: What specific form of cognitive-behavioral therapy focuses on changing deep-seated, lifelong core beliefs in personality disorders?

Back: Schema Therapy.

Front: What psychodynamic therapy framework focuses on the internal representations of self and others to treat Borderline Personality Disorder?

Back: Transference-Focused Psychotherapy (TFP).

Front: What treatment complication occurs when a patient unconsciously replicates their dysfunctional relationship patterns onto the clinician?

Back: Transference.

Front: What occurs when a clinician develops unconscious emotional reactions toward a personality disorder patient based on the patient's behavior?

Back: Countertransference.

Front: What steps should a clinical team take to counter a Borderline patient's attempts at splitting the staff?

Back: Establish regular, open communication and maintain strictly unified, consistent treatment boundaries.

Front: What must a physician prioritize when establishing a treatment plan for a severely self-harming Cluster B patient?

Back: Clear behavioral contracts, clear safety planning, and firm boundary setting.

Front: What features distinguish Schizotypal Personality Disorder from a Delusional Disorder?

Back: Schizotypal beliefs are odd and magical but lack the fixed, non-bizarre, unshakeable nature of a true delusion.

Front: How do you differentiate a Social Anxiety Disorder flare from Avoidant Personality Disorder?

Back: Avoidant Personality Disorder is broader, globally pervasive, starts earlier, and is completely integrated into the identity.

Front: What is the key differentiator between Narcissistic Personality Disorder and a manic episode?

Back: Mania features a distinct change from baseline mood, flight of ideas, decreased need for sleep, and episodic psychosis.

Front: What separates the mood swings of Borderline Personality Disorder from Bipolar II disorder?

Back: BPD mood shifts are highly reactive to interpersonal triggers and last hours, whereas Bipolar II hypomania lasts days and is independent of triggers.

Front: Individuals with Antisocial Personality Disorder have a markedly elevated comorbid lifetime risk for what class of psychiatric disorders?

Back: Substance Use Disorders.

Front: What major personality disorder is a frequent precursor to or comorbidity of Somatization Disorders (Somatic Symptom Disorder)?

Back: Histrionic Personality Disorder.

Front: What Axis I eating disorder has a strong, perfectionistic clinical overlap with Obsessive-Compulsive Personality Disorder?

Back: Anorexia Nervosa (especially the restricting type).

Front: What personality disorder is highly comorbid with Major Depressive Disorder and carries a massive risk for completed suicide?

Back: Borderline Personality Disorder.

Front: What personality disorder cluster is most strongly associated with a family history of substance abuse and mood disorders?

Back: Cluster B.

Front: What personality disorder cluster is most closely associated with a family history of anxiety disorders?

Back: Cluster C.

Front: If a patient displays sudden, profound personality shifts later in life (e.g., age 50), what must be ruled out before assigning a personality disorder?

Front: What is the estimated total prevalence of any personality disorder in the general United States population?

Back: Approximately 10% to 15% of the general population.

Front: Which personality disorder is diagnosed significantly more frequently in clinical settings in females than in males?

Back: Borderline Personality Disorder.

Front: Which personality disorder is diagnosed significantly more frequently in males than in females within forensic settings?

Back: Antisocial Personality Disorder.

Front: Which personality disorder shows a strong familial and genetic aggregation with Schizophrenia spectrum disorders?

Front: What differentiates the manipulative behavior of a Borderline patient from that of an Antisocial patient?

Back: Borderline patients manipulate to secure nurturance and avoid abandonment, whereas Antisocial patients manipulate to gain material power, sex, or money.

Front: What distinguishes the perfectionism of a patient with Anorexia Nervosa from a patient with pure Obsessive-Compulsive Personality Disorder?

Back: The anorexic patient’s rigid control is primarily confined to food intake and body image, whereas the OCPD patient's rigidity spans across all occupational and personal domains.

Front: How does Paranoid Personality Disorder differ from the paranoid flavor of a major depressive episode with psychotic features?

Back: In PPD, the suspiciousness is chronic and baseline, while in depression with psychosis, it occurs exclusively during the discrete depressive episode.

Front: What differentiates the social withdrawal of a Schizoid individual from that of a severely depressed patient?

Back: The Schizoid individual exhibits a lifelong, stable baseline of isolation, whereas the depressed patient experiences a distinct change from a previous level of functioning.

Front: Why do patients with Borderline Personality Disorder often experience rapid, severe adverse drug reactions or paradoxical responses to psychotropic medications?

Back: Their intense affective instability and splitting can cause them to report extreme, fluctuating somatic complaints and subjective side effects.

Front: What distinguishes Narcissistic Personality Disorder from Histrionic Personality Disorder regarding interpersonal exploitation?

Back: Narcissistic individuals exploit others to feed their grandiosity, while Histrionic individuals do so primarily to gain attention and emotional reassurance.

Front: What childhood history component acts as a highly negative prognostic factor for the adulthood course of Antisocial Personality Disorder?

Back: A severe, early-onset history of physical cruelty to animals and fire-setting during the Conduct Disorder phase.

Front: Which personality disorder cluster is most strongly correlated with a high risk of developing an accidental medication or illicit substance overdose?

Back: Cluster B, due to severe impulsivity, emotional reactivity, and high rates of comorbid substance abuse.

Front: What is the typical longitudinal outcome for patients with Borderline Personality Disorder who complete a full course of Dialectical Behavior Therapy?

Back: They demonstrate high rates of symptom remission and significant reductions in self-harm and emergency department utilization.

Front: How does the response to psychotherapy generally differ between Cluster C and Cluster A personality disorders?

Back: Cluster C patients generally demonstrate much higher insight and a more favorable response to psychotherapy than Cluster A patients.

Front: What long-term socioeconomic trend is commonly observed in individuals with undiagnosed or untreated Obsessive-Compulsive Personality Disorder?

Back: They may achieve moderate professional success due to diligence, but their career ceiling is often limited by their inability to compromise or delegate tasks.

Section: Psychiatry
Topic: Eating disorders


Front: Core diagnostic criteria for Anorexia Nervosa (AN)?

Back: Restriction of energy intake leading to significantly low body weight, intense fear of gaining weight, and distorted body image.

Front: Weight threshold separating Anorexia Nervosa from Bulimia Nervosa?

Back: Anorexia Nervosa requires a significantly low body weight (BMI generally less than 18.5 kg/m² in adults).

Front: Two main subtypes of Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Restricting type and Binge-eating/purging type.

Front: Definition of Restricting type Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Weight loss is accomplished primarily through dieting, fasting, and/or excessive exercise over the last 3 months.

Front: Definition of Binge-eating/purging type Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: The individual has engaged in recurrent episodes of binge eating or purging behavior over the last 3 months.

Front: Primary psychological distinction between OCD and Anorexia Nervosa obsessions?

Back: OCD obsessions are ego-dystonic, whereas Anorexia Nervosa obsessions regarding weight and food are ego-syntonic.

Front: Severity specifiers for adult Anorexia Nervosa based on BMI?

Back: Mild (BMI 17–18.4), Moderate (BMI 16–16.99), Severe (BMI 15–15.99), Extreme (BMI less than 15).

Front: DSM-5-TR status of amenorrhea in Anorexia Nervosa diagnosis?

Back: Amenorrhea was removed as a required criterion to allow diagnosis in males, pre-menarcheal females, and those on hormonal contraceptives.

Front: Most common vital sign abnormalities in severe Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Bradycardia, hypotension, and hypothermia.

Front: Cutaneous finding unique to severe starvation in Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Lanugo (fine, downy body hair).

Front: Cardiovascular adaptation to chronic starvation in Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Myocardial atrophy resulting in decreased cardiac output and a prolonged QTc interval.

Front: Hematological manifestations of severe Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Leukopenia, anemia, and occasionally thrombocytopenia due to bone marrow hypoplasia.

Front: Endocrine abnormalities in female Anorexia Nervosa patients?

Back: Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, leading to low estrogen and amenorrhea.

Front: Bone health complication secondary to chronic estrogen deficiency in Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Osteopenia and osteoporosis, which are often irreversible.

Front: Expected thyroid function test panel findings in Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Normal TSH with low-normal T4 and low T3, characteristic of Euthyroid Sick Syndrome.

Front: Gastrointestinal complications associated with severe starvation in Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Delayed gastric emptying and gastroparesis, leading to early satiety and bloating.

Front: Salivary gland finding seen primarily in the purging subtype of Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Parotid gland hypertrophy (sialadenosis).

Front: Dermatological sign associated with self-induced vomiting?

Back: Russell’s sign (calluses or scars on the knuckles/dorsal hand).

Front: Dental manifestation characteristic of chronic purging?

Back: Perimylolysis (erosion of dental enamel, particularly on the lingual surfaces).

Front: First-line medical priority in the stabilization of severe Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Nutritional rehabilitation and restoration of a safe body weight.

Front: Target weekly weight gain for an Anorexia Nervosa inpatient?

Back: 2 to 3 pounds (0.9 to 1.4 kg) per week.

Front: Target weekly weight gain for an Anorexia Nervosa outpatient?

Back: 0.5 to 1 pound (0.2 to 0.5 kg) per week.

Front: First-line psychotherapeutic modality for adolescents with Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Family-Based Therapy (FBT), also known as the Maudsley Method.

Front: Role of medications in the acute treatment of Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Medications generally lack efficacy for core symptoms during acute starvation and are not first-line.

Front: Atypical antipsychotic occasionally used to promote weight gain and reduce obsessive thinking in Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Olanzapine.

Front: Antidepressant completely contraindicated in Anorexia Nervosa and Bulimia Nervosa?

Back: Bupropion, due to an increased risk of generalized seizures.

Front: Indication for inpatient hospitalization in Anorexia Nervosa based on heart rate?

Back: Bradycardia with a heart rate below 40 beats per minute.

Front: Indication for inpatient hospitalization in Anorexia Nervosa based on blood pressure?

Back: Hypotension with a blood pressure below 80/50 mmHg or orthostatic changes.

Front: Electrolyte indication for immediate inpatient admission in eating disorders?

Back: Severe hypokalemia, hypophosphatemia, or hypomagnesemia that cannot be corrected out-of-hospital.

Front: Definition of Refeeding Syndrome?

Back: Severe fluid and electrolyte shifts that occur when nutritional rehabilitation is initiated in chronically starved patients.

Front: Primary driving hormone behind Refeeding Syndrome?

Back: Insulin, which surges in response to carbohydrate introduction.

Front: Intracellular electrolyte shift triggered by insulin during refeeding?

Back: Rapid intracellular shift of phosphate, magnesium, and potassium.

Front: Pathognomonic laboratory hallmark of Refeeding Syndrome?

Back: Hypophosphatemia.

Front: Clinical complications resulting from profound hypophosphatemia in Refeeding Syndrome?

Back: Cardiac arrhythmias, rhabdomyolysis, respiratory failure, and seizures.

Front: Vitamin deficiency that must be corrected during refeeding to prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy?

Back: Thiamine (Vitamin B1).

Front: Initial approach to caloric prescription to prevent Refeeding Syndrome?

Back: Start caloric intake low (e.g., 30–40 kcal/kg/day or lower in extreme cases) and advance slowly while monitoring electrolytes.

Front: Acid-base disturbance typically caused by chronic self-induced vomiting?

Back: Hypokalemic, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis.

Front: Acid-base disturbance typically caused by chronic laxative abuse?

Back: Metabolic acidosis due to bicarbonate loss in stool.

Front: Electrolyte abnormality most responsible for cardiac arrhythmias in Bulimia Nervosa?

Back: Hypokalemia.

Front: Diagnostic utility of serum amylase in purging disorders?

Back: Serum amylase is often elevated due to hypersecretion from hyperplastic salivary glands.

Front: Life-threatening gastrointestinal emergency associated with forceful vomiting?

Back: Mallory-Weiss tears or esophageal rupture (Boerhaave syndrome).

Front: Cardiac complication linked to the chronic abuse of Ipecac syrup?

Back: Ipecac-induced cardiomyopathy, caused by the accumulation of emetine.

Front: First-line psychotherapeutic treatment for Bulimia Nervosa?

Back: Cognitive Behavioral Therapy adapted for eating disorders (CBT-E).

Front: Only FDA-approved medication for the treatment of Bulimia Nervosa?

Back: Fluoxetine.

Front: Dosage requirement for Fluoxetine in Bulimia Nervosa compared to major depressive disorder?

Back: Higher doses are required, typically 60 mg daily instead of the standard 20 mg.

Front: Primary therapeutic goal of CBT-E in Bulimia Nervosa?

Back: Normalizing eating patterns, restructuring cognitive distortions regarding weight, and developing alternative coping mechanisms.

Front: Core diagnostic criteria for Binge-Eating Disorder (BED)?

Back: Recurrent episodes of binge eating associated with marked distress, occurring in the absence of regular compensatory behaviors.

Front: Frequency and duration requirements for Binge-Eating Disorder diagnosis?

Back: At least once a week for at least 3 months.

Front: Three behavioral indicators required during a binge episode for BED diagnosis?

Back: Eating rapidly, eating until uncomfortably full, eating large amounts when not hungry, eating alone out of embarrassment, or feeling disgusted/guilty afterward.

Front: Major clinical feature separating Binge-Eating Disorder from Bulimia Nervosa?

Back: Binge-Eating Disorder lacks the regular use of inappropriate compensatory behaviors.

Front: Most common physical health comorbidity associated with Binge-Eating Disorder?

Back: Obesity and its related metabolic complications (e.g., type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia).

Front: First-line psychotherapeutic intervention for Binge-Eating Disorder?

Back: Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) or Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT).

Front: Only FDA-approved medication for the treatment of moderate-to-severe Binge-Eating Disorder?

Back: Lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse).

Front: Mechanism of action by which Lisdexamfetamine treats Binge-Eating Disorder?

Back: It acts as a central nervous system stimulant, modulating dopamine and norepinephrine to reduce impulsive binging urges.

Front: Off-label antiepileptic medication sometimes used to reduce binge frequency and promote weight loss?

Back: Topiramate.

Front: Role of SSRIs in the management of Binge-Eating Disorder?

Back: Used as a second-line or adjunct treatment to reduce binge frequency and treat comorbid depressive or anxious symptoms.

Front: Core definition of Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID)?

Back: An eating or feeding disturbance characterized by a persistent failure to meet appropriate nutritional and/or energy needs.

Front: Three primary presentations/motivations behind food avoidance in ARFID?

Back: Apparent lack of interest in food, avoidance based on sensory characteristics, or concern about aversive consequences of eating (e.g., choking).

Front: Key clinical feature distinguishing ARFID from Anorexia Nervosa and Bulimia Nervosa?

Back: ARFID lacks any distortion in the perception of body weight or shape, and no fear of gaining weight.

Front: Required clinical consequences to diagnose ARFID?

Back: Significant weight loss (or failure to achieve expected growth), significant nutritional deficiency, dependence on enteral feeding/supplements, or marked psychosocial impairment.

Front: Most common pediatric psychiatric comorbidity overlapping with sensory-based ARFID?

Back: Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD).

Front: Primary therapeutic modality used to treat ARFID?

Back: Behavioral interventions, systemic desensitization, and family-supported nutritional rehabilitation.

Front: Core diagnostic criteria for Pica?

Back: Persistent eating of nonnutritive, nonfood substances over a period of at least 1 month.

Front: Minimum developmental age required to legally diagnose Pica?

Back: At least 2 years of age, to ensure the behavior is developmentally inappropriate.

Front: Common micronutrient deficiencies closely associated with Pica?

Back: Iron deficiency anemia and zinc deficiency.

Front: Important medical risks associated with severe Pica?

Back: Lead poisoning, intestinal obstruction, intestinal perforation, and parasitic infections.

Front: Core diagnostic criteria for Rumination Disorder?

Back: Repeated regurgitation of food over a period of at least 1 month, where food may be re-chewed, re-swallowed, or spit out.

Front: Key behavioral distinction for Rumination Disorder?

Back: The behavior must not be due to an associated gastrointestinal or medical condition (e.g., GERD, pyloric stenosis).

Front: First-line behavioral intervention for Rumination Disorder?

Back: Diaphragmatic breathing techniques practiced immediately after eating.

Front: Purpose of the "Other Specified Feeding or Eating Disorder" (OSFED) category?

Back: To classify presentations where symptoms cause clinically significant distress but do not meet the full diagnostic criteria for any specific eating disorder.

Front: Definition of Atypical Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: All criteria for Anorexia Nervosa are met, except that despite significant weight loss, the individual's weight is within or above the normal range.

Front: Definition of Bulimia Nervosa of low frequency and/or limited duration?

Back: All criteria for Bulimia Nervosa are met, except the binge eating and inappropriate compensatory behaviors occur less than once a week and/or for less than 3 months.

Front: Definition of Purging Disorder?

Back: Recurrent purging behavior to influence weight or shape in the absence of binge eating episodes.

Front: Definition of Night Eating Syndrome?

Back: Recurrent episodes of night eating, manifested by eating after awakening from sleep or by excessive food consumption after the evening meal, causing distress.

Front: Mechanism by which Fluoxetine diminishes binging and purging behaviors?

Back: Downregulation of presynaptic 5-HT1A receptors and enhancement of serotonin neurotransmission in the hypothalamus.

Front: Rationale behind avoiding Bupropion in purging patients based on neurotransmitter dynamics?

Back: Purging induces rapid electrolyte shifts that lower the seizure threshold, which compounding with Bupropion's inhibition of norepinephrine/dopamine reuptake triggers status epilepticus.

Front: Clinical monitoring protocol required when using high-dose Fluoxetine in eating disorder clinics?

Back: Baseline and periodic ECGs to monitor for QTc prolongation, especially if electrolyte fluctuations are present.

Front: Indication for using Topiramate adjunctively in Binge-Eating Disorder?

Back: It modulates GABA-A receptors and inhibits AMPA/kainate pathways, reducing the reward-seeking urgency of binge behaviors.

Front: Metabolic side effects of Olanzapine that require tracking when used for Anorexia Nervosa weight restoration?

Back: Fasting blood glucose, lipid panels, and baseline HbA1c to monitor for drug-induced metabolic syndrome.

Front: Mechanism of action of Orlistat and its status in eating disorder management?

Back: Gastric and pancreatic lipase inhibitor; generally avoided or highly restricted due to high potential for abuse as a purging mechanism.

Front: Psychiatric disorder with the highest mortality rate?

Back: Anorexia Nervosa.

Front: Two primary causes of death in patients diagnosed with Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Medical complications of starvation (primarily cardiac arrest) and suicide.

Front: Peak age of onset for Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Early adolescence, typically between 14 and 18 years of age.

Front: Peak age of onset for Bulimia Nervosa?

Back: Late adolescence to early adulthood, typically between 18 and 22 years of age.

Front: Gender distribution ratio for Anorexia Nervosa and Bulimia Nervosa in clinical populations?

Back: Approximately 10:1 female-to-male ratio.

Front: Gender distribution of Binge-Eating Disorder compared to Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Much more symmetric, with females representing roughly 60% and males representing 40% of cases.

Front: Most common lifetime psychiatric comorbidities across all eating disorders?

Back: Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), Anxiety disorders (Social Anxiety, OCD), and Substance Use Disorders.

Front: General long-term recovery rate for patients treated for Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Approximately 50% achieve full recovery, 30% show partial improvement, and 20% develop a chronic, unremitting illness.

Front: Diagnostic differentiation when a patient displays low weight, fear of fatness, and obsessive handwashing?

Back: Dual diagnosis of Anorexia Nervosa and OCD is warranted if the OCD obsessions extend completely outside of food and body image.

Front: Distinguishing features between Body Dysmorphic Disorder (BDD) and Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: BDD involves preoccupation with specific, localized flaws in physical appearance, whereas Anorexia involves a global distortion of weight and body shape.

Front: Medical conditions that mimic the profound weight loss of Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Hyperthyroidism, Addison’s disease, Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD), and occult malignancies.

Front: Lab profile finding that helps differentiate true fasting starvation from surreptitious purging?

Back: Purging presents with low urinary chloride levels, while fasting starvation maintains normal urinary electrolytes.

Front: Diagnosis for a patient who limits dietary intake to "pure" or "healthy" foods, causing nutritional failure, but has no concern with weight or body shape?

Back: Orthorexia Nervosa (not a formal DSM-5-TR diagnosis; categorized under ARFID or OSFED).

Front: Diagnostic outcome when an individual meets all criteria for both Schizophrenia and Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Both disorders should be diagnosed concurrently if the eating disturbance is not entirely subordinate to persecutory delusions about food.

Front: Diagnostic outcome when binge eating episodes occur exclusively during episodes of Major Depressive Disorder?

Back: Both MDD and the appropriate eating disorder (e.g., BED or Bulimia) should be diagnosed if criteria for both are met independently.

Front: Expected serum bicarbonate level in an active laxative-abusing patient?

Back: Low serum bicarbonate (metabolic acidosis).

Front: Expected serum bicarbonate level in an active vomiting patient?

Back: High serum bicarbonate (contraction metabolic alkalosis).

Front: Why does hypokalemia persist in purging patients despite oral potassium supplementation?

Back: Concurrent hypomagnesemia promotes renal potassium wasting, preventing correction until magnesium is replenished.

Front: Mechanism of aldosterone elevation in chronic purging behaviors?

Back: Intravascular volume depletion triggers the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), leading to secondary hyperaldosteronism.

Front: Effect of secondary hyperaldosteronism on renal excretion in purging patients?

Back: It forces the kidneys to retain sodium and water at the expense of exacerbating urinary potassium and hydrogen excretion.

Front: Phenomenon of rapid fluid retention and peripheral edema upon stopping chronic purging behaviors?

Back: "Pseudo-Bartter syndrome," driven by the sudden withdrawal of purging while RAAS remains highly upregulated.

Front: Immediate management strategy for severe pedal edema secondary to purging cessation?

Back: Avoid rapid diuretic use; treat with mild sodium restriction, leg elevation, and time to let the RAAS downregulate naturally.

Front: Mechanism causing hypothalamic amenorrhea in starvation states?

Back: Suppression of the pulsatile secretion of Gonadotropin-Releasing Hormone (GnRH) from the hypothalamus.

Front: Status of growth hormone (GH) levels in severe Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Paradoxically elevated, but peripheral insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) production is profoundly suppressed due to hepatic GH resistance.

Front: Cortisol levels typically found in patients with acute, severe Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Elevated, due to starvation-induced activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and prolonged clearance rates.

Front: Structural brain changes observable on neuroimaging in severe Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Pseudo-atrophy of the brain, characterized by increased ventricular size and prominent sulci, which largely reverses with weight restoration.

Front: Changes in cholesterol panels frequently seen in starvation states?

Back: Paradoxical hypercholesterolemia, driven by decreased biliary excretion and altered cholesterol metabolism.

Front: Pathophysiology behind delayed gastric emptying in starvation?

Back: Atrophy of the gastrointestinal smooth muscle coat combined with autonomic dysregulation.

Front: Primary therapeutic goal during Phase 1 of Family-Based Therapy (Maudsley)?

Back: Parents are given complete, non-punitive control over the patient's eating habits and refeeding schedule.

Front: Primary focus of Phase 2 of Family-Based Therapy (Maudsley)?

Back: Gradually returning control over eating back to the adolescent once weight stabilization is nearly achieved.

Front: Primary target of Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT) when utilized for Bulimia Nervosa?

Back: Resolving current interpersonal problems, grief, or role transitions that trigger the emotional distress driving binge/purge cycles.

Front: Cognitive distortion characterized by an eating disorder patient viewing a single bite of forbidden food as total failure?

Back: All-or-nothing (dichotomous) thinking.

Front: Cognitive restructuring technique used in CBT-E to handle catastrophic thoughts about weight gain?

Back: Decatastrophizing, via logical challenging and behavioral experiments.

Front: Transdiagnostic model concept regarding eating disorders?

Back: The theory that Anorexia, Bulimia, and BED share identical core psychopathology—over-evaluation of shape and weight—manifesting in different behavioral patterns.

Front: Standard titration strategy for Lisdexamfetamine in Binge-Eating Disorder?

Back: Initiate at 30 mg orally once daily in the morning, and titrate weekly to a target therapeutic dose of 50 to 70 mg.

Front: Black box warning consideration when initiating stimulants for Binge-Eating Disorder?

Back: High potential for abuse and dependence; careful screening for personal or family history of substance use disorder is required.

Front: Cardiovascular absolute contraindications for initiating Lisdexamfetamine?

Back: Advanced arteriosclerosis, symptomatic cardiovascular disease, moderate-to-severe hypertension, or known structural cardiac abnormalities.

Front: Primary side effect profile of Topiramate used off-label for binge behaviors?

Back: Paresthesias, cognitive slowing ("word-finding difficulties"), somnolence, and an increased risk of nephrolithiasis (kidney stones).

Front: Mechanism by which Topiramate induces metabolic acidosis?

Back: It acts as a weak inhibitor of carbonic anhydrase enzymes, increasing renal bicarbonate loss.

Front: Most common side effect of high-dose Fluoxetine (60–80mg) during the first two weeks of treatment?

Back: Transiently increased anxiety, nausea, insomnia, and tremors.

Front: Clinical monitoring required prior to initiating Olanzapine in an eating disorder patient?

Back: Baseline body weight, waist circumference, blood pressure, fasting plasma glucose, and fasting lipid profile.

Front: Most likely eating disorder presentation in a patient with Type 1 Diabetes who consistently presents with unexplained DKA?

Back: Diabulimia, characterized by the deliberate withholding of insulin to induce glycosuria and rapid weight loss.

Front: Expected serum chloride level in a patient who secondary-abuses diuretics versus a patient who purges via vomiting?

Back: Vomiting causes low urinary and serum chloride, whereas diuretic abuse causes high urinary chloride despite low serum chloride.

Front: Diagnostic significance of a normal or elevated body weight in a patient who purges daily?

Back: It rules out Anorexia Nervosa and confirms a diagnosis of Bulimia Nervosa or Purging Disorder.

Front: The "Female Athlete Triad" components frequently tested on Step 2 CK?

Back: Low energy availability (with or without an eating disorder), menstrual dysfunction (amenorrhea), and low bone mineral density.

Front: Most sensitive screening tool utilized in primary care clinics to detect eating disorders?

Back: The SCOFF questionnaire.

Front: Ethical justification for treating an adult Anorexia Nervosa patient against their will?

Back: Civil commitment or involuntary medical treatment is legally justified if severe malnutrition impairs cognitive capacity to the point of imminent self-harm.

Front: Primary immediate cause of death during the first 48 hours of aggressive nutritional rehabilitation?

Back: Acute congestive heart failure secondary to fluid overload and severe hypophosphatemia.

Front: Indication for cardiac telemetry in an eating disorder inpatient admission?

Back: A prolonged QTc interval greater than 500 milliseconds or a history of syncopal episodes.

Front: Preferred route of nutritional replacement in a severely malnourished patient who refuses oral intake?

Back: Nasogastric tube feeding is preferred over total parenteral nutrition (TPN) to maintain gut mucosal integrity and minimize infection risk.

Front: Expected changes in liver function tests (LFTs) during the first two weeks of refeeding therapy?

Back: Transient elevation of transaminases (AST and ALT) due to hepatic steatosis or rapid glycogen deposition.

Front: Primary clinical presentation of Anorexia Nervosa in pre-pubertal children?

Back: Failure to make expected developmental height gains or a complete stagnation of the growth curve rather than overt weight loss.

Front: Major obstetric complication associated with active Bulimia Nervosa during pregnancy?

Back: Increased risk of intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), low birth weight, and postpartum depression.

Front: Influence of maternal Anorexia Nervosa on the risk of miscarriage?

Back: It significantly increases the risk of spontaneous abortion due to an unfavorable uterine environment and hormonal instability.

Front: Typical presentation of "Muscle Dysmorphia" (a variant of Body Dysmorphic Disorder often overlapping with eating disorders)?

Back: Core preoccupation that one's body is too small or insufficiently muscular, leading to excessive weightlifting and anabolic steroid abuse.

Front: Primary neurochemical abnormality implicated in the altered satiety signals of Bulimia Nervosa?

Back: Blunted postprandial cholecystokinin (CCK) secretion and altered central serotonin receptor sensitivity.

Front: Changes in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) 5-HIAA levels in long-term recovered Anorexia Nervosa patients?

Back: Paradoxically elevated 5-HIAA levels, which may correlate with baseline trait anxiety and perfectionism.

Front: Structural brain alteration on MRI that persists even after long-term weight restoration in Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Persistent reductions in gray matter volume in specific regions of the anterior cingulate cortex have been observed in some chronic cases.

Front: Heritability estimate for Anorexia Nervosa based on twin studies?

Back: High genetic liability, with heritability estimated between 50% and 60%.

Front: Why are typical (first-generation) antipsychotics avoided in the treatment of Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: They carry a high risk of lowering the seizure threshold and exacerbating prolonged QTc intervals without providing weight-restoration benefits.

Front: Management strategy for severe SSRI-induced gastrointestinal distress in a Bulimia Nervosa patient?

Back: Take the medication with a meal or split the daily dose, as the distress is driven by peripheral serotonin receptor activation.

Front: Why is Mirtazapine considered an attractive off-label choice for some Anorexia Nervosa outpatients with comorbid depression?

Back: Its antagonism of 5-HT2C and H1 receptors robustly stimulates appetite and promotes weight gain while simultaneously reducing insomnia.

Front: Drug-drug interaction risk when combining high-dose Fluoxetine with certain atypical antipsychotics like Ziprasidone?

Back: Severe additive QTc prolongation, which increases the risk of Torsades de Pointes.

Front: Diagnostic criteria adjustments in the DSM-5-TR regarding the definition of a "Binge"?

Back: The DSM-5-TR clarifies that the "sense of lack of control" during a binge can be present even if the individual chooses to stop eating mid-episode.

Front: How does DSM-5-TR classify a patient who presents with regular purging after eating normal amounts of food but does not binge?

Back: Purging Disorder, which is coded under Other Specified Feeding or Eating Disorder (OSFED).

Front: Diagnostic classification for "Kline-Levin Syndrome" to differentiate it from Binge-Eating Disorder?

Back: It is classified as a sleep-wake disorder (hypersomnolence), characterized by periodic episodes of hyperphagia and hypersexuality, not an eating disorder.

Front: Differentiation between ARFID and selective eating based on religious or cultural practices?

Back: If the food restriction is entirely explained by organized religious fasting or cultural norms, an ARFID diagnosis cannot be made.

Front: True or False: Leptin levels are characteristically elevated in patients with active Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: False; leptin levels are profoundly low due to the loss of adipose tissue, which removes a key stimulus for GnRH secretion.

Front: Why do patients with severe Anorexia Nervosa exhibit cold intolerance beyond simple lack of insulation?

Back: Starvation induces a functional down-regulation of peripheral cellular metabolism to conserve energy, mimicking systemic hypothyroidism.

Front: Expected effect of severe malnutrition on the secretion of Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH) and urine concentration?

Back: It can cause a mild, reversible central Diabetes Insipidus variant, leading to polyuria and an inability to maximally concentrate urine.

Front: What happens to ghrelin levels in patients with acute Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Ghrelin levels are significantly elevated as the body attempts to signal starvation and stimulate food intake, though the brain remains resistant to it.

Front: Serum calcium status in chronic Anorexia Nervosa without active purging?

Back: Typically normal due to compensatory parathyroid hormone (PTH) elevation drawing calcium from bone stores, accelerating osteoporosis.

Front: What is the "Squat-and-Stand" test used for in an eating disorder physical evaluation?

Back: It assesses proximal muscle wasting and motor weakness caused by myofibrillar catabolism due to severe protein-calorie malnutrition.

Front: Definition of "Melanosis Coli" in a patient presenting with an unclassified eating disorder?

Back: A benign, dark pigmentation of the colorectal mucosa visible on colonoscopy, indicating chronic abuse of anthraquinone stimulant laxatives (e.g., senna).

Front: Diagnostic significance of a sudden, dramatic drop in blood urea nitrogen (BUN) in a severely emaciated patient?

Back: It reflects near-total depletion of protein stores and hepatic synthetic failure, a sign of terminal starvation.

Front: Why does severe starvation cause non-inflammatory, bilateral enlargement of the parotid glands?

Back: Compensatory hyper-salivation and acinar hypertrophy occur as the body prepares to process scarce food efficiently.

Front: What is "Acrocyanosis" in the context of severe eating disorders?

Back: A bluish or purple discoloration of the hands and feet caused by peripheral vasoconstriction to maintain core body temperature.

Front: How do you differentiate the weight loss of a Major Depressive Episode from Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Depressed patients lose appetite and weight without a distorted body image, drive for thinness, or intense fear of weight gain.

Front: Core differentiator between Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID) and Somatic Symptom Disorder?

Back: Somatic Symptom Disorder focuses on the distressing nature of a physical symptom (e.g., abdominal pain), whereas ARFID focuses purely on the aversive consequences of eating itself (e.g., choking).

Front: Can an individual be diagnosed with both Pica and Rumination Disorder simultaneously?

Back: Yes, if the criteria for both disorders are fully met independently, they should be co-diagnosed.

Front: Diagnostic outcome if a patient meets criteria for Bulimia Nervosa but only binges and purges once every two weeks for six months?

Back: Coded as Low Frequency Bulimia Nervosa under the Other Specified Feeding or Eating Disorder (OSFED) category.

Front: Diagnostic boundary between Binge-Eating Disorder and Night Eating Syndrome?

Back: Night Eating Syndrome involves conscious awareness of eating a large amount of food post-dinner or mid-sleep, but lacks the specific "loss of control" binge episodes.

Front: Immediate medical intervention for a patient with an eating disorder who presents with symptomatic hypoglycemia?

Back: Intravenous infusion of dextrose, preceded immediately by or paired with intravenous thiamine to prevent acute Wernicke’s encephalopathy.

Front: Fluid resuscitation fluid of choice for a severely dehydrated, purging patient with hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis?

Back: Normal saline (0.9% NaCl) with potassium added, because chloride replacement is required to halt renal bicarbonate retention.

Front: Why is lactated Ringer’s solution avoided in the initial resuscitation of a purging patient with metabolic alkalosis?

Back: It contains lactate, which is metabolized into bicarbonate and can worsen the pre-existing metabolic alkalosis.

Front: Target inpatient heart rate during sleep that triggers an immediate medical intervention?

Back: A nocturnal heart rate falling consistently below 30 to 35 beats per minute.

Front: Legal requirement for discharging a voluntary adult eating disorder patient who is medically unstable but demands to leave against medical advice (AMA)?

Back: The clinician must hold the patient under an emergency psychiatric/medical hold if the patient’s severe cognitive impairment from starvation constitutes an imminent risk to life.

Back: It is a prodrug requiring rate-limited enzymatic cleavage by red blood cells to become active, preventing rapid euphoric spikes.

Front: Rationale behind using low-dose atypical antipsychotics to manage acute mealtime distress in Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: They block 5-HT2A and D2 receptors, which transiently reduces the acute, severe delusional anxiety associated with food ingestion.

Front: Why can SSRIs fail to treat comorbid depressive symptoms during the acute, starvation phase of Anorexia Nervosa?

Back: Severe malnutrition depletes tryptophan, the essential amino acid precursor required for the synthesis of serotonin.

Front: Cardiovascular monitoring parameter that must be evaluated before prescribing Topiramate off-label?

Back: None; unlike stimulants, Topiramate does not increase blood pressure or heart rate, making it safer for patients with mild cardiovascular concerns.

Front: Main reason why tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) are avoided in purging patients?

Back: They lower the seizure threshold and have profound cardiotoxicity, which multiplies the danger of underlying electrolyte imbalances.

Front: Core diagnostic criteria for Bulimia Nervosa?

Back: Recurrent episodes of binge eating followed by inappropriate compensatory behaviors to prevent weight gain, occurring at least weekly for 3 months.

Front: Definition of a binge eating episode?

Back: Eating an amount of food definitely larger than what most individuals would eat, accompanied by a sense of lack of control.

Front: Examples of inappropriate compensatory behaviors in Bulimia Nervosa?

Back: Self-induced vomiting, misuse of laxatives, diuretics, enemas, fasting, or excessive exercise.

Front: Main clinical feature distinguishing Bulimia Nervosa from Anorexia Nervosa, binge-eating/purging type?

Back: Bulimia Nervosa patients maintain a normal or overweight body mass index (BMI $\ge$ 18.5).

Front: DSM-5-TR frequency and duration requirements for Bulimia Nervosa?

Back: At least once a week for at least 3 months.

Front: Personality trait frequently comorbid with Bulimia Nervosa compared to Restricting Anorexia?

Back: High impulsivity and emotional dysregulation (often overlapping with Borderline Personality Disorder).

Front: Severity categorization for Bulimia Nervosa?

Back: Based on the frequency of compensatory behaviors per week: Mild (1–3), Moderate (4–7), Severe (8–13), Extreme ($\ge$14).

Back: Alpha-2 adrenergic receptor antagonist that increases release of serotonin and norepinephrine.

Front: Mechanism of action of Trazodone?

Front: Definition and timeline of Acute Dystonia?
Back: Sudden, painful muscle spasms of the neck, face, or tongue occurring within hours to days of starting antipsychotics.

Front: First-line treatment for an acute dystonic reaction?
Back: Intramuscular Benztropine or Diphenhydramine.

Front: Definition and clinical presentation of Akathisia?
Back: Subjective feeling of inner restlessness and the physical compulsion to move, typically occurring within days to weeks.

Front: First-line pharmacological options for the treatment of Akathisia?
Back: Propranolol or Benzodiazepines.

Front: Presentation and timeline of Antipsychotic-Induced Parkinsonism?
Back: Bradykinesia, rigidity, and a resting tremor occurring within weeks to months of treatment initiation.

Front: Preferred management for drug-induced parkinsonism if the antipsychotic cannot be lowered?
Back: Benztropine or Amantadine.

Front: Definition and timeline of Tardive Dyskinesia?
Back: Involuntary, choreoathetotic movements of the mouth, tongue, and face following months to years of antipsychotic use.

Front: Mechanism of action of medications approved to treat Tardive Dyskinesia (e.g., Valbenazine, Deutetrabenazine)?
Back: Vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2) inhibitors.

Front: Tetrad of symptoms defining Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS)?
Back: Severe "lead-pipe" muscle rigidity, hyperthermia, autonomic instability, and altered mental status.

Front: Laboratory marker characteristically elevated in Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome?
Back: Creatine kinase (CK).

Front: Two primary pharmacological agents used to treat severe Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome?
Back: Dantrolene and Bromocriptine.

Front: Primary therapeutic mechanism of Second-Generation Antipsychotics (SGAs)?

Back: Olanzapine and Clozapine.

Front: Two Second-Generation Antipsychotics with the lowest risk for metabolic syndrome and weight gain?
Back: Aripiprazole and Ziprasidone.

Front: Second-Generation Antipsychotic requiring administration with food to ensure adequate absorption?
Back: Ziprasidone (or Lurasidone).

Front: Specific mechanism of action that differentiates Aripiprazole from most other SGAs?
Back: Partial D2 receptor agonism.

Front: Second-Generation Antipsychotic with the highest propensity to elevate prolactin levels?
Back: Risperidone.

Front: Antipsychotic preferred for managing psychosis in patients with Parkinson's disease due to low D2 affinity?
Back: Quetiapine.

Front: First-line maintenance medication for Bipolar I disorder with proven anti-suicidal properties?
Back: Lithium.

Front: Maintenance laboratory monitoring parameters required for a patient on Lithium?
Back: Thyroid function tests (TSH), renal function tests (BUN/Creatinine), and serum Lithium levels.

Back: 0.6 to 1.2 mEq/L.

Back: T-wave flattening or inversion.

Back: Nephrogenic Diabetes Insipidus.

Front: Congenital cardiac anomaly associated with Lithium use during the first trimester of pregnancy?
Back: Ebstein's anomaly.

Front: Mechanism of action of Valproic Acid (Valproate)?
Back: Blocks voltage-gated sodium channels and increases GABA concentrations in the brain.

Front: Two major organ systems at risk for acute toxicity from Valproic Acid?
Back: Hepatic (hepatotoxicity) and Pancreatic (acute pancreatitis).

Front: Congenital malformation associated with maternal Valproic Acid use during pregnancy?
Back: Neural tube defects (e.g., spina bifida).

Front: Mechanism of action of Carbamazepine?
Back: Blocks voltage-gated sodium channels, inhibiting repetitive neuronal firing.

Front: Serious hematologic adverse effect associated with Carbamazepine use?
Back: Agranulocytosis and aplastic anemia.

Front: Genetic allele strongly associated with Stevens-Johnson Syndrome in Asian patients taking Carbamazepine?
Back: HLA-B*1502.

Back: Autoinduction of its own metabolism via the CYP3A4 enzyme system.

Front: Mood stabilizer primarily used for bipolar depression maintenance that requires slow titration?
Back: Lamotrigine.

Back: Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) / Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN).

Front: Interaction effect when Valproate and Lamotrigine are co-administered?
Back: Valproate doubles Lamotrigine levels, drastically increasing SJS risk.

Front: Mechanism of action of Benzodiazepines?
Back: Positive allosteric modulators that increase the frequency of GABA-A channel opening.

Front: Three Benzodiazepines that bypass phase I hepatic oxidation and undergo only phase II glucuronidation?
Back: Oxazepam, Temazepam, and Lorazepam (mnemonic: OTL / Outside The Liver).

Front: Benzodiazepine preferred for the management of acute alcohol withdrawal in a patient with severe liver cirrhosis?
Back: Lorazepam (or Oxazepam).

Front: Specific competitive antagonist used to reverse benzodiazepine-induced respiratory depression?
Back: Flumazenil.

Front: Major clinical danger of administering Flumazenil to a patient with chronic benzodiazepine dependence?
Back: Precipitation of acute withdrawal seizures.

Front: Mechanism of action of Buspirone?
Back: Partial agonist at the Serotonin 5-HT1A receptor.

Front: Clinical timeline limitation of Buspirone for the treatment of generalized anxiety disorder?
Back: It takes 2 to 4 weeks to become clinically effective and cannot be used prn (as needed).

Front: Mechanism of action of "Z-drugs" (Zolpidem, Zaleplon, Eszopiclone)?
Back: Selective agonists at the alpha-1 subunit of the GABA-A receptor complex.

Front: Main clinical advantage of Z-drugs over standard Benzodiazepines for insomnia?
Back: Minimal disruption of normal sleep architecture and lower risk of dependence.

Front: Mechanism of action of Ramelteon?
Back: Melatonin receptor (MT1 and MT2) agonist.

Front: Mechanism of action of Suvorexant?
Back: Orexin receptor antagonist that turns off wakefulness signals.

Front: First-line medication for opioid overdose causing respiratory depression?
Back: Naloxone.

Front: Long-acting opioid agonist used for maintenance therapy in opioid use disorder?
Back: Methadone.

Front: EKG abnormality that requires strict monitoring in patients on high-dose Methadone maintenance?

Front: Mechanism of action of Buprenorphine?
Back: Partial mu-opioid receptor agonist with a high binding affinity.

Front: Purpose of adding Naloxone to Buprenorphine in sublingual combination formulations (Suboxone)?

Front: Competitive opioid antagonist used to prevent relapse in both alcohol and opioid use disorders?
Back: Naltrexone.

Back: Liver function tests (due to risk of hepatotoxicity).

Front: Mechanism of action of Disulfiram?

Front: Mechanism of action of Acamprosate?

Front: Contraindication for the use of Acamprosate in patients with alcohol use disorder?

Back: Alpha-4-beta-2 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor partial agonist.

Front: First-line pharmacological class for Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) in pediatric and adult populations?
Back: Stimulants (Methylphenidate and Amphetamines).

Front: Mechanism of action of Methylphenidate?
Back: Blocks the reuptake of dopamine and norepinephrine via transporter inhibition.

Front: Mechanism of action of Amphetamines?
Back: Stimulates the presynaptic release of dopamine and norepinephrine into the synaptic cleft.

Front: Non-stimulant medication approved for ADHD that acts as a selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (NRI)?
Back: Atomoxetine.

Front: Mechanism of action of Guanfacine and Clonidine when used for ADHD?
Back: Alpha-2 adrenergic receptor agonists that strengthen prefrontal cortical networks.

Front: Mechanism of action of Donepezil, Rivastigmine, and Galantamine?
