Section: Pediatrics (Introductory)
Topic: Neonatal conditions — jaundice, RDS, sepsis

Front: Definition of physiological jaundice peak timeline in a term infant?

Back: Total serum bilirubin (TSM) peaks at 3 to 5 days of life.

Front: Definition of physiological jaundice peak timeline in a preterm infant?

Back: Total serum bilirubin peaks at 5 to 7 days of life.

Front: Maximum normal velocity of bilirubin rise in physiological jaundice?

Back: Less than 5 mg/dL per 24 hours (or <0.2 mg/dL per hour).

Front: Definition of pathological jaundice based on onset timing?

Back: Any visible jaundice presenting within the first 24 hours of life.

Front: Definition of pathological jaundice based on direct (conjugated) bilirubin levels?

Back: Direct bilirubin greater than 1.0 mg/dL if TSB is <5 mg/dL, or >20% of the TSB if TSB is >5 mg/dL.

Front: Mechanism of Breast Milk Jaundice?

Back: High levels of beta-glucuronidase in breast milk deconjugate bilirubin in the intestine, increasing enterohepatic circulation.

Front: Typical onset timing for Breast Milk Jaundice?

Back: Starts after the first week of life, peaking at 2 to 3 weeks.

Front: Mechanism of Breastfeeding Jaundice (Breastfeeding Failure Jaundice)?

Back: Inadequate milk intake leads to dehydration, slow gut motility, and increased enterohepatic bilirubin reabsorption.

Front: Typical onset timing for Breastfeeding Jaundice?

Back: Occurs within the first 3 to 5 days of life.

Front: Cephalocaudal progression of jaundice means what clinically?

Back: Dermal icterus progresses from head to toe as serum bilirubin levels rise.

Front: Primary enzyme deficient in Crigler-Najjar Syndrome Type I?

Back: Complete absence of Uridine Diphosphate Glucuronosyltransferase (UGT1A1).

Front: Primary enzyme deficiency in Gilbert Syndrome?

Back: Mildly decreased activity (around 10-30% of normal) of UGT1A1.

Front: Diagnostic response to phenobarbital in Crigler-Najjar Type II (Arias Syndrome)?

Back: Phenobarbital induces UGT1A1 activity, causing a significant decrease in serum bilirubin.

Front: Most common hemolytic cause of severe neonatal unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia?

Back: ABO incompatibility (usually Mother type O, Infant type A or B).

Front: Key peripheral blood smear finding in ABO incompatibility?

Back: Microspherocytes.

Front: Why does Rh incompatibility cause more severe hydrops fetalis than ABO incompatibility?

Back: Maternal anti-D antibodies (IgG) are highly destructive, whereas anti-A/B antibodies are often IgM or neutralized by tissue antigens.

Front: What does a positive Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT/Coombs) indicate?

Back: Antibody-coated red blood cells are present in the newborn's circulation.

Front: Most common red cell enzyme deficiency causing neonatal jaundice?

Back: Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency.

Front: Trigger factors for hemolysis in a G6PD-deficient neonate?

Back: Oxidative stress from maternal infection, naphthalene (mothballs), or certain medications.

Front: Classic triad of Encephalopathy due to acute bilirubin toxicity?

Back: Hypotonia/hypertonia (retrocollis/opisthotonos), high-pitched cry, and poor feeding.

Front: Chronic, irreversible brain damage from bilirubin deposition is called?

Back: Kernicterus.

Front: Specific neuroanatomical site targeted by unconjugated bilirubin toxicity?

Back: Basal ganglia (specifically the globus pallidus) and subthalamic nuclei.

Front: Long-term neurologic sequelae of Kernicterus?

Back: Choreoathetoid cerebral palsy, sensorineural hearing loss, and upward gaze palsy.

Front: Mechanism of action of phototherapy for neonatal jaundice?

Back: Converts unconjugated bilirubin into structural isomers (lumirubin) via photoisomerization, which are excreted in bile and urine without conjugation.

Front: Wavelength of light most effective for neonatal phototherapy?

Back: Blue-green light in the 460 to 490 nm spectrum.

Front: Crucial physical protection needed for a neonate under phototherapy?

Back: Eye shields to prevent retinal damage.

Front: Benign skin complication seen in infants with conjugated hyperbilirubinemia undergoing phototherapy?

Back: Bronze Baby Syndrome.

Front: Definitive immediate therapy for neonates with TSB at or near exchange levels despite intensive phototherapy?

Back: Double-volume exchange transfusion.

Front: Major immediate risk/complication of exchange transfusion?

Back: Hypocalcemia (due to citrate anticoagulant), thrombocytopenia, and portal vein thrombosis.

Front: First-line screening test to differentiate biliary atresia from neonatal hepatitis?

Back: Abdominal ultrasound (looking for the triangular cord sign or absent gallbladder).

Front: Gold standard diagnostic test for Biliary Atresia?

Back: Intraoperative cholangiogram.

Front: Definitive temporary surgical management for Biliary Atresia?

Back: Kasai hepatoportoenterostomy.

Front: Ideal timeframe for performing a Kasai procedure to ensure optimal prognosis?

Back: Before 45 to 60 days of life.

Front: Direct hyperbilirubinemia accompanied by cataracts and hepatomegaly suggests?

Back: Galactosemia (Galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase deficiency).

Front: Direct hyperbilirubinemia with a lack of interlobular bile ducts on liver biopsy describes?

Back: Alagille Syndrome (associated with JAG1 mutation).

Front: Cardinal facial features of Alagille Syndrome?

Back: Broad forehead, pointed chin, and deep-set, hypertoric eyes.

Front: Cardiac anomaly most highly associated with Alagille Syndrome?

Back: Peripheral pulmonary artery stenosis.

Front: Skeletal abnormality visible on X-ray in Alagille Syndrome?

Back: Butterfly vertebrae.

Front: Most common cause of conjugated hyperbilirubinemia in a preterm infant on long-term parental nutrition?

Back: Intravenous Lipid/PN-associated cholestasis (PNAC).

Front: Alternative medical term for Respiratory Distress Syndrome?

Back: Hyaline Membrane Disease (HMD).

Front: Primary biochemical composition of pulmonary surfactant?

Back: Dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (lecithin) and surfactant proteins (A, B, C, D).

Front: Primary cell type responsible for producing and secreting surfactant?

Back: Type II alveolar pneumocytes.

Front: Gestational age when surfactant production begins to rise significantly?

Back: Between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation.

Front: Gestational age when surfactant production reaches mature levels?

Back: 35 weeks of gestation.

Front: Pathophysiology of atelectasis in RDS?

Back: High alveolar surface tension leads to alveolar collapse at end-expiration.

Front: Consequence of progressive atelectasis on ventilation-perfusion matching?

Back: Intrapulmonary right-to-left shunting leading to severe hypoxemia.

Front: Key maternal metabolic condition that delays fetal lung maturation?

Back: Maternal diabetes mellitus (hyperinsulinemia inhibits surfactant synthesis).

Front: Physical stress factor that accelerates fetal lung maturation?

Back: Chronic intrauterine stress (e.g., maternal hypertension, PPROM) via endogenous corticosteroid release.

Front: Classical triad of physical signs of neonatal respiratory distress?

Back: Tachypnea, intercostal/subcostal retractions, and expiratory grunting.

Front: Pathophysiology behind the expiratory grunt in RDS?

Back: Partial closure of the glottis during expiration to maintain positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP).

Front: Pathognomonic chest X-ray findings in severe RDS?

Back: Diffuse, uniform "ground-glass" reticulogranular appearance with air bronchograms.

Front: Reason for air bronchograms on an RDS chest X-ray?

Back: Aerated bronchioles are silhouetted against surrounding collapsed, fluid-filled alveoli.

Front: Classic arterial blood gas (ABG) findings in untreated RDS?

Back: Hypoxemia, hypercapnia, and mixed respiratory-metabolic acidosis.

Front: Preferred pharmacological strategy for a mother at 30 weeks gestation in active preterm labor?

Back: Antenatal corticosteroids (Betamethasone or Dexamethasone).

Front: Standard timeframe for maximum benefit of antenatal corticosteroids?

Back: At least 24 hours and up to 7 days before delivery.

Front: First-line non-invasive respiratory support for a preterm infant with mild-to-moderate RDS?

Back: Nasal Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (nCPAP).

Front: Primary physiological benefit of CPAP in RDS?

Back: Recruits collapsed alveoli and stabilizes Functional Residual Capacity (FRC).

Front: What does the "INSURE" strategy stand for in RDS management?

Back: Intubate, Surfactant administration, and Extubate to CPAP immediately.

Front: Route of administration for exogenous surfactant?

Back: Endotracheal instillation directly into the lungs.

Front: Major acute risk during the rapid administration of exogenous surfactant?

Back: Transient airway obstruction and bradycardia.

Front: Physiological change after surfactant that necessitates rapid weaning of ventilator pressures?

Back: A sharp, rapid increase in pulmonary compliance.

Front: Failure to properly wean ventilator settings after surfactant can cause?

Back: Pulmonary barotrauma/volutrauma leading to pneumothorax.

Front: Chronic lung disease condition resulting from prolonged mechanical ventilation and oxygen toxicity?

Back: Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia (BPD).

Front: Diagnostic definition of Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia (BPD)?

Back: Requirement for supplemental oxygen at 36 weeks postmenstrual age (for infants born <32 weeks).

Front: Diagnostic alternative for BPD if the infant was born at or after 32 weeks gestation?

Back: Requirement for supplemental oxygen beyond 28 days of life.

Front: Characteristic chest X-ray finding in chronic Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia?

Back: "Coarse lucencies" or a "sponge-like" cystic appearance with areas of atelectasis and hyperinflation.

Front: Major cardiovascular complication of severe Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia?

Back: Pulmonary hypertension leading to cor pulmonale.

Front: Prophylactic medication recommended to prevent RSV infection in infants with severe BPD?

Back: Palivizumab (monoclonal antibody against RSV F glycoprotein).

Front: Acute neurological complication associated with severe hypoxia and fluctuations in blood pressure in RDS?

Back: Intraventricular Hemorrhage (IVH).

Front: Screen tool used to detect Intraventricular Hemorrhage in preterm infants?

Back: Cranial ultrasound through the anterior fontanelle.

Front: Origin site of bleeding in neonatal Intraventricular Hemorrhage?

Back: The fragile subependymal germinal matrix.

Front: Main differential diagnosis for a term infant with respiratory distress and a "wet lung" chest X-ray?

Back: Transient Tachypnea of the Newborn (TTN).

Front: Pathophysiology of Transient Tachypnea of the Newborn (TTN)?

Back: Delayed clearance of fetal lung fluid by lung epithelial sodium channels ($ENaC$).

Front: Classic chest X-ray findings in Transient Tachypnea of the Newborn (TTN)?

Back: Prominent perihilar vascular markings, fluid in the interlobar fissures, and hyperinflation.

Front: Major risk factor for the development of Transient Tachypnea of the Newborn?

Back: Elective Cesarean delivery without a trial of labor.



Front: Definition of Early-Onset Neonatal Sepsis (EOS) by timing?

Back: Onset of systemic infection within the first 72 hours of life.

Front: Definition of Late-Onset Neonatal Sepsis (LOS) by timing?

Back: Onset of systemic infection after 72 hours of life up to 28 days.

Front: Primary mode of transmission for Early-Onset Sepsis?

Back: Vertical transmission from the maternal genital tract during labor or delivery.

Front: Primary mode of transmission for Late-Onset Sepsis?

Back: Horizontal transmission from the environment, caregivers, or nosocomial sources.

Front: Most common bacterial pathogen responsible for Early-Onset Neonatal Sepsis?

Back: Group B Streptococcus (Streptococcus agalactiae).

Front: Second most common pathogen causing EOS, particularly in preterm infants?

Back: Escherichia coli.

Front: Gram-positive rod associated with neonatal sepsis via maternal ingestion of unpasteurized dairy?

Back: Listeria monocytogenes.

Front: Most common pathogen group responsible for Late-Onset Sepsis in NICU patients with central lines?

Back: Coagulase-negative Staphylococci (Staphylococcus epidermidis).

Front: Major maternal risk factor for Early-Onset Sepsis involving amniotic fluid?

Back: Maternal Chorioamnionitis (intra-amniotic infection).

Front: Clinical diagnostic criteria for Maternal Chorioamnionitis?

Back: Maternal fever plus maternal/fetal tachycardia, uterine tenderness, or foul-smelling amniotic fluid.

Front: Time cutoff defining Prolonged Rupture of Membranes (PROM) as a sepsis risk factor?

Back: Rupture of membranes greater than or equal to 18 hours prior to delivery.

Front: Standard screening protocol timing for maternal Group B Streptococcus carriage?

Back: Rectovaginal swab culture performed at 36 0/7 to 37 6/7 weeks of gestation.

Front: Preferred agent for maternal intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis against GBS?

Back: Intravenous Penicillin G (or Ampicillin).

Front: Alternative intrapartum antibiotic for GBS prophylaxis if the mother has a non-severe penicillin allergy?

Back: Cefazolin.

Front: Alternative intrapartum antibiotic for GBS prophylaxis if the mother has a severe penicillin allergy and resistant isolates?

Back: Vancomycin.

Front: Minimum duration of intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis prior to delivery to be considered "adequate"?

Back: 4 hours or more before birth.

Front: Most common, yet non-specific, clinical sign of neonatal sepsis?

Back: Temperature instability (hypothermia or hyperthermia).

Front: Why is hypothermia more common than hyperthermia in sick preterm neonates?

Back: Immature thermoregulation and lack of brown fat stores to generate metabolic heat.

Front: Cardiovascular indicator of early neonatal septic shock?

Back: Prolonged capillary refill time (>3 seconds), tachycardia, and mottled skin.

Front: Standard, absolute diagnostic test required prior to starting empiric antibiotics for suspected sepsis?

Back: Blood culture.

Front: Minimum adequate volume of blood required for a neonatal blood culture bottles?

Back: 1 mL of blood.

Front: Why must a Lumbar Puncture be strongly considered in neonatal sepsis workups?

Back: Up to 30% of neonates with bacteremia have concurrent meningitis without focal meningeal signs.

Front: Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) findings indicative of bacterial neonatal meningitis?

Back: Elevated WBC count (with polymorphonuclear predominance), elevated protein, and low glucose.

Front: First-line empiric antibiotic regimen for suspected Early-Onset Neonatal Sepsis?

Back: Ampicillin plus an Aminoglycoside (typically Gentamicin).

Front: Why is Ampicillin explicitly included in the early-onset empiric regimen?

Back: To provide specific, empirical coverage for Listeria monocytogenes.

Front: Empiric antibiotic regimen for suspected Late-Onset Sepsis/Nosocomial NICU infections?

Back: Vancomycin (for MRSA/CoNS) plus an antipseudomonal beta-lactam (like Cefepime or Piperacillin-Tazobactam).

Front: Why is Ceftriaxone avoided in neonates requiring empiric therapy?

Back: It displaces bilirubin from albumin (increasing kernicterus risk) and can precipitate with calcium in biliary sludge.

Front: Preferred alternative third-generation cephalosporin used for neonatal bacterial meningitis?

Back: Cefotaxime.

Front: Target trough level timing for monitoring Gentamicin toxicity in a neonate?

Back: Measured immediately before the 3rd or 4th dose.

Front: Primary toxicities associated with Aminoglycoside (Gentamicin) use?

Back: Ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity.

Front: Primary toxicity associated with Vancomycin use if infused too rapidly?

Back: Red Man Syndrome (Histamine release).

Front: Complete Blood Count (CBC) finding that has a high negative predictive value for neonatal sepsis?

Back: A normal absolute neutrophil count (ANC) and low immature-to-total (I:T) neutrophil ratio.

Front: Formula to calculate the Immature-to-Total (I:T) neutrophil ratio?

Back: (Bands + Metamyelocytes) divided by (Segmented Neutrophils + Bands + Metamyelocytes).

Front: An I:T ratio greater than what value suggests acute neonatal infection?

Back: Greater than or equal to 0.2.

Front: Acute hematologic sign of severe sepsis due to platelet consumption?

Back: Thrombocytopenia (platelets <150,000/uL).

Front: Systemic inflammatory biomarker commonly used to track the treatment response of neonatal sepsis?

Back: C-Reactive Protein (CRP).

Front: Why is a single normal CRP at onset insufficient to rule out neonatal sepsis?

Back: CRP synthesis lags and can take 12 to 24 hours from the onset of infection to elevate significantly.

Front: Serious gastrointestinal complication of prematurity linked to severe systemic sepsis?

Back: Necrotizing Enterocolitis (NEC).

Front: Pathognomonic radiographic sign seen on abdominal X-ray in Necrotizing Enterocolitis?

Back: Pneumatosis intestinalis (gas within the bowel wall).

Front: Metabolic complication of sepsis caused by increased peripheral glucose consumption and poor reserves?

Back: Hypoglycemia.

Front: Target serum glucose level for immediate intervention in a symptomatic neonate?

Back: Less than 40 to 45 mg/dL.

Front: Standard management for a completely asymptomatic well-appearing infant born to a mother with inadequately treated GBS prophylaxis?

Back: Routine clinical observation in the hospital for at least 48 hours without diagnostic tests.

Front: Neonatal condition characterized by a generalized petechial rash ("blueberry muffin spots") and hepatosplenomegaly?

Back: Congenital TORCH infection.

Front: Classic triad of congenital Toxoplasmosis?

Back: Chorioretinitis, hydrocephalus, and intracranial calcifications.

Front: Pattern of intracranial calcifications in congenital Toxoplasmosis?

Back: Diffuse throughout the brain parenchyma.

Front: Pattern of intracranial calcifications in congenital Cytomegalovirus (CMV)?

Back: Periventricular calcifications.

Front: Classic long-term neurological sequela of congenital CMV infection?

Back: Sensorineural hearing loss.

Front: Classic triad of congenital Rubella Syndrome?

Back: Cataracts, sensorineural deafness, and congenital heart disease (Patent Ductus Arteriosus).

Front: Primary physical finding in early congenital Syphilis involving the nose?

Back: Snuffles (copious, highly infectious mucoid or bloody nasal discharge).

Front: Definitive diagnostic test for neonatal Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) central nervous system infection?

Back: CSF HSV Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR).

Front: Standard antiviral therapy for documented or highly suspected neonatal HSV infection?

Back: High-dose intravenous Acyclovir ($60 \text{ mg/kg/day}$ divided every 8 hours).



Front: What is the primary hazard of rapid, aggressive correction of profound chronic hyponatremia during exchange transfusion?

Back: Central pontine myelinolysis (Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome).

Front: Why does an infant with a large cephalohematoma have an increased risk of severe hyperbilirubinemia?

Back: Extravascular RBC destruction and breakdown of trapped hemoglobin increases the systemic bilirubin load.

Front: Which congenital cardiac lesion is classically associated with an increased incidence of neonatal hyperbilirubinemia due to polycythemia?

Back: Infants of diabetic mothers or transposition of the great arteries.

Front: What is the rule of thumb for when to initiate phototherapy based on a healthy term infant's age in hours?

Back: Use the AAP (American Academy of Pediatrics) nomogram, tracking risk zones based on hour-specific bilirubin levels.

Front: What is the effect of maternal epidural anesthesia on neonatal jaundice?

Back: It can sometimes cause poor initial feeding, exacerbating breastfeeding jaundice.

Front: What underlying structural liver anomaly must be ruled out in an infant presenting with clay-colored (acholic) stools?

Back: Biliary atresia.

Front: What nutritional deficiency must be proactively managed in infants with chronic cholestatic jaundice?

Back: Deficiencies in fat-soluble vitamins (Vitamins A, D, E, K) due to poor bile acid-mediated micelle formation.

Front: What is the significance of a "double bubble" sign on abdominal X-ray in a neonate with conjugated hyperbilirubinemia?

Back: Suggests Duodenal Atresia, classically associated with Down Syndrome, which also carries an increased risk of neonatal transient myeloproliferative disorder and cholestasis.

Front: Why does hypothyroidism cause prolonged unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia?

Back: Hepatic glucuronide conjugation is delayed because thyroid hormone is required for UGT enzyme maturation.

Front: What is the inheritance pattern of Crigler-Najjar Syndrome Type I?

Back: Autosomal recessive.

Front: What is the inheritance pattern of Gilbert Syndrome?

Back: Autosomal recessive (most commonly a promoter mutation variant).



Front: What is the target pre-ductal oxygen saturation range for a preterm infant at 5 minutes of life?

Back: 80% to 85% (per Neonatal Resuscitation Program/NRP guidelines).

Front: Where should a pulse oximeter probe be placed on a neonate to measure pre-ductal oxygen saturation?

Back: On the right wrist or palmar surface of the right hand.

Front: What causes the difference between pre-ductal and post-ductal oxygen saturations in severe respiratory distress?

Back: Persistent Pulmonary Hypertension of the Newborn (PPHN) causing a right-to-left shunt across the patent ductus arteriosus (PDA).

Front: What is the definitive echocardiographic finding that confirms Persistent Pulmonary Hypertension of the Newborn (PPHN)?

Back: Right-to-left shunting through the PDA or foramen ovale, and flattened interventricular septum from high right ventricular pressures.

Front: What is the first-line inhaled vasodilator therapy used for PPHN?

Back: Inhaled Nitric Oxide (iNO).

Front: What is the primary mechanism of action of Inhaled Nitric Oxide?

Back: Selectively relaxes pulmonary vascular smooth muscle without causing systemic hypotension.

Front: What respiratory condition is characterized by a "patchy, asymmetric infiltrates with rope-like density" and hyperinflation on chest X-ray?

Back: Meconium Aspiration Syndrome (MAS).

Front: What is the major mechanical complication of Meconium Aspiration Syndrome in the airway?

Back: A "ball-valve" effect leading to air trapping, subpleural emphysema, and high risk of pneumothorax.

Front: What is the current standard management for a non-vigorous infant born through meconium-stained amniotic fluid?

Back: Direct routine resuscitation at the warmer under NRP guidelines (routine endotracheal suctioning is no longer recommended).

Front: What defines Apnea of Prematurity?

Back: Cessation of breathing for more than 20 seconds, or any pause accompanied by bradycardia or cyanosis in an infant <37 weeks.

Front: What is the first-line pharmacological treatment for Apnea of Prematurity?

Back: Caffeine citrate.

Front: What is the mechanism of action of Caffeine Citrate in treating Apnea of Prematurity?

Back: Stimulates the central respiratory center in the medulla and increases medullary sensitivity to $CO_2$.

Front: At what postmenstrual age does Apnea of Prematurity typically resolve spontaneously?

Back: Usually by 36 to 37 weeks postmenstrual age.

Front: What underlying vascular complication of extreme prematurity is exacerbated by high, fluctuating oxygen saturations?

Back: Retinopathy of Prematurity (ROP).

Front: What is the pathophysiology of Retinopathy of Prematurity?

Back: Hyperoxia causes initial vessel vasoconstriction and cessation of growth, followed by hypoxia-driven abnormal neovascularization.

Front: What is the screening timeline for Retinopathy of Prematurity?

Back: Infants born $\le 30$ weeks gestation or weighing $<1500\text{ g}$ should be screened at 4 weeks chronologic age or 31 weeks postmenstrual age (whichever is later).

Front: What cardiac lesion classically remains open due to chronic hypoxia and respiratory distress in preterm infants?

Back: Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA).

Front: What is the classic physical exam finding of a hemodynamically significant PDA?

Back: A continuous, machine-like murmur heard best at the left upper sternal border, accompanied by bounding peripheral pulses.

Front: What is the standard first-line medical therapy to induce closure of a symptomatic PDA in a preterm infant?

Back: Cyclooxygenase (COX) inhibitors such as Indomethacin or Ibuprofen.

Front: What is a major absolute contraindication to administering Indomethacin for PDA closure?

Back: Active necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), significant renal impairment, or active bleeding.



Front: What clinical tool is now widely recommended to determine the need for antibiotics in early-onset sepsis instead of empirical treatment based on risk factors alone?

Back: The Kaiser Permanente Neonatal Sepsis Risk Calculator.

Front: What are the three main clinical categories evaluated by the Neonatal Sepsis Risk Calculator?

Back: Maternal risk factors, the infant's clinical status (well-appearing, equivocal, or critically ill), and hour-specific risk.

Front: What is the significance of a high direct or band count on a neonatal differential without a change in total WBC?

Back: Left shift, indicating early bone marrow release of immature neutrophils in response to acute infection.

Front: Why can severe neonatal sepsis cause a normal or even low total white blood cell count?

Back: Depletion of the bone marrow's limited neutrophil storage pool in the face of overwhelming infection.

Front: What is the significance of a high serum lactate level in a neonate with suspected sepsis?

Back: Indicates tissue hypoperfusion and anaerobic metabolism, pointing toward septic shock.

Front: What fluid volume bolus is recommended for a neonate in septic shock?

Back: $10\text{ to }20 \text{ mL/kg}$ of isotonic crystalloid (Normal Saline) administered slowly over 10-20 minutes.

Front: Why should fluid boluses be delivered carefully in preterm neonates compared to older children?

Back: Rapid volume expansion can cause sudden shifts in cranial blood pressure, increasing the risk of Intraventricular Hemorrhage (IVH).

Front: What is the first-choice inotrope/vasopressor for neonatal fluid-refractory septic shock with low systemic vascular resistance?

Back: Dopamine or Epinephrine.

Front: What viral infection should be suspected in a neonate with sepsis-like symptoms, negative bacterial cultures, and markedly elevated transaminases (AST/ALT)?

Back: Neonatal Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) or Enterovirus.

Front: What is the most common presentation of late-onset GBS infection?

Back: Meningitis (often presenting with irritability, fever, and focal neurological signs after week 1).

Front: Does maternal intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis effectively prevent Late-Onset GBS disease?

Back: No, it only significantly reduces the incidence of Early-Onset GBS disease.

Front: What metabolic disturbance can mimic the lethargy and poor feeding of neonatal sepsis in a small-for-gestational-age (SGA) infant?

Back: Neonatal polycythemia (hematocrit $>65\%$) leading to hyperviscosity syndrome.

Front: What is the definitive treatment for symptomatic neonatal polycythemia hyperviscosity syndrome?

Back: Partial exchange transfusion with normal saline.

Front: What classic electrolyte abnormality is seen in neonatal sepsis due to temporary adrenal insufficiency or tissue damage?

Back: Hyponatremia or hyperkalemia.

Front: What complication of neonatal central venous access lines increases the risk of fungal late-onset sepsis?

Back: Biofilm formation, most commonly by Candida albicans or Candida parapsilosis.

Front: What is the preferred empiric treatment for documented systemic neonatal candidiasis?

Back: Amphotericin B deoxycholate.

Front: What does an elevated cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) protein level without an elevated WBC count suggest in a neonate with neurological symptoms?

Back: Normal variant (neonates naturally have higher baseline CSF protein, up to $150\text{ mg/dL}$ in preterms).

Front: What is the next diagnostic step if a neonate has persistently positive blood cultures 48 hours after starting appropriate antibiotics?

Back: A dark-brown/black pigmented liver due to epinephrine metabolite accumulation is present in Dubin-Johnson but completely absent in Rotor Syndrome.

Front: What genetic mutation confirms Alagille Syndrome in a neonate presenting with cholestasis, butterfly vertebrae, and a harsh systolic murmur?

Back: Mutation in the JAG1 or NOTCH2 gene resulting in paucity of interlobular bile ducts.

Front: What is the primary diagnostic utility of a Technetium-99m hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid (HIDA) scan in a 3-week-old with direct hyperbilirubinemia?

Back: Failure of the radiotracer to excrete into the duodenum after 24 hours strongly supports a diagnosis of Biliary Atresia over neonatal hepatitis.

Front: What medical therapy can be used to stimulate bile flow in infants experiencing Total Parenteral Nutrition (TPN)-induced cholestasis?

Back: Ursodeoxycholic acid (Ursodiol).

Front: Why does a Coombs-negative hemolytic anemia with Heinz bodies and bite cells on a peripheral smear point to G6PD deficiency during a jaundice workup?

Back: Heinz bodies represent denatured hemoglobin from oxidative stress, which are subsequently "bitten" out by splenic macrophages.

Front: What is the critical total serum bilirubin threshold level above which a term infant is at immediate risk for acute bilirubin encephalopathy?

Back: Typically greater than or equal to 25 to 30 mg/dL, depending on the infant’s hourly age and risk factors.

Front: What specific ocular manifestation is seen in neonates with severe Type 1 Tyrosinemia causing conjugated hyperbilirubinemia?

Back: Corneal ulcers or crystalline corneal plaques (due to tyrosine crystal deposition).

Front: What structural component of the alveolus collapses first when there is a genetic deficiency of Surfactant Protein B (SP-B)?

Back: The terminal alveolar air-spaces, causing fatal, refractory respiratory failure at birth despite exogenous surfactant treatment.

Front: What rare congenital structural anomaly mimics severe RDS but presents with a scaphoid abdomen and bowel sounds heard in the chest?

Back: Congenital Diaphragmatic Hernia (CDH).

Front: What is the immediate, non-negotiable delivery room management for an infant born with a known Congenital Diaphragmatic Hernia?

Back: Immediate endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation (avoid bag-valve-mask ventilation to prevent gastric distension and lung compression).

Front: What respiratory syndrome presents identically to RDS on day one but occurs in a term infant born via scheduled Cesarean delivery?

Back: Transient Tachypnea of the Newborn (TTN).

Front: What specific fluid-clearance mechanism fails in Transient Tachypnea of the Newborn (TTN)?

Back: Delayed activation of epithelial sodium channels ($ENaC$) which normally reabsorb fetal lung fluid during labor.

Front: What chest X-ray finding definitively differentiates Meconium Aspiration Syndrome (MAS) from classic RDS?

Back: MAS shows asymmetric, patchy infiltrates with lung hyperinflation and flattened diaphragms, whereas RDS shows symmetric, diffuse ground-glass opacities with low lung volumes.

Front: What is the primary pulmonary vascular consequence of severe, chronic tissue hypoxia in an infant with Meconium Aspiration Syndrome?

Back: Persistent Pulmonary Hypertension of the Newborn (PPHN) due to reactive pulmonary vasoconstriction.

Front: What diagnostic test confirms a right-to-left shunt in a neonate suspected of having PPHN?

Back: Simultanous pre-ductal (right hand) and post-ductal (either foot) arterial blood gas or pulse oximetry showing a $PaO_2$ gradient $>10-15\text{ mmHg}$ or an $SpO_2$ difference $>5-10\%$.

Front: What chronic structural remodeling occurs in the pulmonary vasculature during prolonged, unresolved PPHN?

Back: Hypertrophy and hyperplasia of the smooth muscle layer of pulmonary arterioles.

Front: Why does maternal use of Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs) late in pregnancy increase the risk of neonatal PPHN?

Back: Accumulation of serotonin in the fetal lung can act as a direct pulmonary vasoconstrictor and alter vascular remodeling.

Front: What is the primary mechanism of action of Sildenafil when used as an adjunctive treatment for neonatal pulmonary hypertension?

Back: Inhibits phosphodiesterase-5 (PDE-5), preventing the breakdown of cyclic GMP ($cGMP$) to promote pulmonary smooth muscle relaxation.

Front: What mechanical ventilator strategy is optimized to minimize barotrauma in an infant with severe, non-compliant RDS failing conventional ventilation?

Back: High-Frequency Oscillatory Ventilation (HFOV) or High-Frequency Jet Ventilation (HFJV).

Front: What is the primary mechanism of gas exchange during High-Frequency Oscillatory Ventilation (HFOV)?

Back: Continuous alveolar recruitment via a constant mean airway pressure, paired with rapid, sub-anatomical dead-space tidal volume oscillations.



Front: Why do neonates have an impaired ability to localize infections, making them highly susceptible to rapid systemic bacteremia?

Back: They possess deficient chemotaxis of neutrophils and low levels of circulating complement proteins (C3 and C4).

Front: What specific bacterial virulence factor allows neonatal Escherichia coli strains to selectively cross the blood-brain barrier to cause meningitis?

Back: The K1 capsular polysaccharide antigen.

Front: Why is the absolute neutrophil count (ANC) often completely unreliable in the first 4 hours of life when screening for Early-Onset Sepsis?

Back: Normal physiological delivery-stress causes a transient, dramatic surge in the ANC immediately after birth.

Front: What does a persistently low or dropping platelet count in a neonate being treated for sepsis indicate?

Back: Ongoing consumptive coagulopathy (Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation/DIC) or persistent endotoxemia.

Front: What is the definitive, standard criteria for a positive cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) culture to rule out sample contamination in neonatal meningitis?

Back: Growth of a single pathogenic organism within 24 to 48 hours of incubation.

Front: Why is a lumbar puncture mandatory in any neonate with a positive blood culture, even if they look clinically stable?

Back: Up to 15-30% of neonates with bacteremia have concurrent meningitis, and treating meningitis requires significantly higher doses and a longer duration of antibiotics.

Front: What is the standard duration of antibiotic therapy for uncomplicated neonatal bacteremia versus confirmed GBS meningitis?

Back: 7 to 10 days for uncomplicated bacteremia; 14 to 21 days for bacterial meningitis.

Front: Why must Aminoglycoside (Gentamicin) serum levels be carefully tracked via peak and trough monitoring in neonates?

Back: High troughs increase the risk of nephrotoxicity (acute tubular necrosis), while inadequate peaks result in treatment failure due to insufficient bactericidal activity.

Front: What is the first-line empiric antibiotic choice for a neonate presenting with late-onset sepsis who has an indwelling central venous catheter?

Back: Vancomycin (to cover Coagulase-Negative Staphylococci and MRSA) combined with an antipseudomonal aminoglycoside or cephalosporin.

Front: What severe systemic complication arises if a neonate with underlying septic shock develops profound, bilateral adrenal hemorrhage?

Back: Waterhouse-Friderichsen Syndrome, leading to acute adrenal crisis and refractory hypotension.

Front: What stress-dose hormone therapy is indicated for a neonate with septic shock that remains completely refractory to both volume resuscitation and high-dose vasopressors?

Back: Intravenous Hydrocortisone (to treat relative adrenal insufficiency).

Front: What specific clinical criteria from the Neonatal Sepsis Risk Calculator automatically mandates immediate empiric antibiotic therapy, regardless of maternal risk history?

Back: A clinical status classification of "Clinically Ill" (defined as persistent respiratory distress, hypotonia, lethargy, or poor perfusion).

Front: What classic, benign skin condition can be mistaken for a septic vesicular rash but is characterized by eosinophils on a Wright-stained pustule smear?

Back: Erythema Toxicum Neonatorum.

Front: What dermatological finding in a neonate with sepsis suggests systemic Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection?

Back: Ecthyma gangrenosum (pustules that rapidly progress to necrotic, ulcerated lesions with central black eschars).

Front: What classic intracranial ultrasound finding is seen in a neonate who survived severe, late-onset bacterial meningitis?

Back: Encephalomalacia, porencephaly, or ventricular septation (leading to obstructive hydrocephalus).

Front: What is the most common long-term neurodevelopmental disability seen in infants who survive neonatal sepsis combined with severe RDS?

Back: Cerebral palsy, often accompanied by varying degrees of cognitive impairment or sensorineural hearing loss.

Front: Why does profound metabolic acidosis ($pH < 7.1$) in a septic neonate drastically impair cardiac contractility?

Back: Severe acidemia alters intracellular calcium availability and blunts myocardial responsiveness to endogenous and exogenous catecholamines.

Front: Why does a neonate with Congenital Hypothyroidism display prolonged unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia?

Back: Hepatic glucuronide conjugation is delayed because thyroid hormone is required for the structural maturation of hepatic UGT1A1.

Front: What rare, life-threatening metabolic cause of conjugated hyperbilirubinemia presents with vomiting, hypoglycemia, and cataracts after introduction of milk?

Back: Galactosemia (Galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase deficiency).

Front: Why are infants with Galactosemia at a vastly increased risk for Early-Onset Neonatal Sepsis?

Back: High systemic galactose concentrations inhibit leukocyte bactericidal activity, predisposing them specifically to Escherichia coli sepsis.

Front: What feature on a liver biopsy distinguishes Dubin-Johnson Syndrome from Rotor Syndrome in a neonate with direct hyperbilirubinemia?

Back: A dark-brown/black pigmented liver due to epinephrine metabolite accumulation is present in Dubin-Johnson but completely absent in Rotor Syndrome.

Section: Pediatrics (Introductory)
Topic: Common pediatric infections — otitis, RSV, meningitis


Front: Most common bacterial pathogen isolated in Acute Otitis Media (AOM)?
Back: Streptococcus pneumoniae.

Front: Two most common non-typeable bacterial pathogens in AOM after S. pneumoniae?
Back: Non-typeable Haemophilus influenzae and Moraxella catarrhalis.

Front: Bacterial pathogen most likely to cause bilateral AOM concurrent with purulent conjunctivitis?
Back: Non-typeable Haemophilus influenzae (Otitis-Conjunctivitis Syndrome).

Front: Primary viral pathogens predisposed to causing secondary AOM?
Back: Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) and Influenza virus.

Front: Gold standard physical exam finding required to diagnose AOM?
Back: Bulging of the tympanic membrane with impaired mobility on pneumatic otoscopy.

Front: Color change of the tympanic membrane most indicative of acute infection in AOM?
Back: Distinct erythema or pale yellow/cloudy discoloration behind the membrane.

Front: Meaning of a flat, non-mobile tympanogram (Type B) on tympanometry?
Back: Presence of fluid in the middle ear space (effusion).

Front: Defining clinical feature that differentiates Otitis Media with Effusion (OME) from AOM?
Back: OME has middle ear effusion without signs of acute inflammation or systemic illness.

Front: Standard first-line antibiotic choice for pediatric AOM without penicillin allergy?
Back: High-dose Amoxicillin (80 to 90 mg/kg/day).

Front: Rationale for using high-dose Amoxicillin in treating pediatric AOM?
Back: To overcome penicillin-binding protein resistance in Streptococcus pneumoniae.

Front: Preferred initial antibiotic for AOM if the child has concurrent purulent conjunctivitis?
Back: Amoxicillin-Clavulanate (Augmentin) to cover beta-lactamase producing H. influenzae.

Front: Preferred initial antibiotic for AOM if the patient received amoxicillin in the past 30 days?
Back: Amoxicillin-Clavulanate.

Front: Alternative oral antibiotic choice for AOM in a patient with a non-anaphylactic penicillin allergy?
Back: Cefdinir, Cefuroxime, or Cefpodoxime (second or third-generation cephalosporins).

Front: Alternative antibiotic therapy for AOM in a patient with an anaphylactic penicillin allergy?
Back: Azithromycin or Clindamycin.

Front: First-line parenteral treatment for AOM in a child unable to tolerate oral medications?
Back: Intramuscular Ceftriaxone (1 to 3 daily doses).

Front: Strict criteria for "watchful waiting" (observation without antibiotics) in pediatric AOM?
Back: Age 6 to 23 months with unilateral, mild symptoms, or age over 2 years with mild symptoms.

Front: Minimum age below which all patients with certain or suspected AOM must receive immediate antibiotics?
Back: Six months of age.

Front: Recommended duration of antibiotic therapy for AOM in children under two years of age?
Back: 10 days.

Front: Recommended duration of antibiotic therapy for AOM in children aged six years or older with mild symptoms?
Back: 5 to 7 days.

Front: Most common intracranial complication of poorly treated Acute Otitis Media?
Back: Meningitis.

Front: Most common extracranial, bony complication of Acute Otitis Media?
Back: Acute Mastoiditis.

Front: Classic physical exam finding in a child presenting with Acute Mastoiditis?
Back: Displacement of the pinna downward and outward with postauricular erythema and tenderness.

Front: Initial diagnostic imaging modality of choice for suspected mastoiditis or intracranial extension?
Back: CT scan of the temporal bones with contrast.

Front: Defining feature of Chronic Otitis Media with Effusion?
Back: Middle ear effusion persisting for 3 months or longer.

Front: Standard management for asymptomatic Otitis Media with Effusion noted after an episode of AOM?
Back: Clinical observation and reassurance, as most cases resolve spontaneously within 3 months.

Front: Absolute indication for tympanostomy tube insertion in chronic OME?
Back: Effusion persisting for 3 or more months with documented structural damage or bilateral hearing loss.

Front: Clinical criteria for tympanostomy tube placement due to recurrent AOM?
Back: 3 or more distinct episodes in 6 months, or 4 or more episodes in 12 months with at least one in the preceding 6 months.

Front: First-line treatment for uncomplicated otorrhea in a child with functional tympanostomy tubes?
Back: Topical antibiotic eardrops (e.g., Fluoroquinolones) rather than systemic oral antibiotics.

Front: Pathophysiologic mechanism underlying Otitis Media with Effusion in children with cleft palate?
Back: Tensor veli palatini muscle dysfunction leading to chronic Eustachian tube obstruction.

Front: Classic otoscopic finding associated with Bullous Myringitis?
Back: Fluid-filled blisters or vesicles visible on the surface of the tympanic membrane.

Front: Most common clinical manifestation of primary Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) infection in infants under one year old?
Back: Bronchiolitis.

Front: Peak age range for severe RSV bronchiolitis manifestations in infants?
Back: 2 to 6 months of age.

Front: Primary pathophysiologic mechanism of airway obstruction in RSV bronchiolitis?
Back: Necrosis of airway epithelium, inflammatory mucus plugging, and submucosal edema in the bronchioles.

Front: Classic initial upper respiratory symptoms of RSV infection before lower tract involvement?
Back: Clear rhinorrhea, sneezing, and a low-grade fever.

Front: Standard auscultatory findings on lung examination during the peak of RSV bronchiolitis?
Back: Diffuse, high-pitched expiratory wheezing and scattered fine crackles throughout both lung fields.

Front: Visual physical exam signs indicative of severe respiratory distress in an infant with RSV?
Back: Intercostal, subcostal, and suprasternal retractions, along with nasal flaring and expiratory grunting.

Front: Life-threatening respiratory symptom that can be the presenting sign of RSV in premature infants?
Back: Apnea.

Front: Standard method of confirming RSV diagnosis in a hospitalized patient?
Back: Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) assay or rapid antigen detection from a nasopharyngeal aspirate.

Front: Typical chest radiograph findings in an infant with classic RSV bronchiolitis?
Back: Bilateral hyperinflation, flattened diaphragms, peribronchial cuffing, and patchy areas of atelectasis.

Front: Role of routine chest radiography in the evaluation of mild, uncomplicated pediatric bronchiolitis?
Back: Not routinely recommended, as it often leads to inappropriate antibiotic use for atelectasis misidentified as pneumonia.

Front: Cornerstone of management for an infant admitted with typical RSV bronchiolitis?
Back: Supportive care, including frequent nasopharyngeal suctioning, supplemental oxygen, and hydration.

Front: Primary indication for hospital admission in an infant with RSV bronchiolitis?
Back: Persistent hypoxemia (oxygen saturation below 90 to 92%), respiratory distress, or inadequate oral hydration.

Front: Role of systemic or inhaled corticosteroids in the routine management of RSV bronchiolitis?
Back: Not recommended, as clinical trials show no benefit in reducing hospitalization or improving clinical scores.

Front: Role of bronchodilators (e.g., Albuterol) in the routine treatment of a first episode of bronchiolitis?
Back: Not recommended routinely due to lack of impact on disease course or hospital stay duration.

Front: Only definitive clinical scenario where a trial of nebulized epinephrine might be evaluated in bronchiolitis?
Back: As a rescue trial in a hospital setting, discontinued immediately if no objective clinical improvement is documented.

Front: Preferred method of hydration for a tachypneic infant with bronchiolitis and a respiratory rate over 60 to 70 breaths per minute?
Back: Intravenous fluids or nasogastric tube feeding to minimize the high risk of aspiration.

Front: Target oxygen saturation parameter for discontinuing supplemental oxygen in a recovering bronchiolitis patient?
Back: Stable oxygen saturation at or above 90% on room air during periods of awake activity and sleep.

Front: Monoclonal antibody used for immunoprophylaxis against RSV in high-risk infants?
Back: Palivizumab.

Front: Absolute candidate group for Palivizumab prophylaxis during their first RSV season?
Back: Infants born before 29 weeks of gestation.

Front: Chronic medical condition qualifying an infant under 12 months for Palivizumab prophylaxis?
Back: Chronic Lung Disease of prematurity requiring medical intervention within six months of the RSV season.

Front: Maximum number of monthly doses of Palivizumab recommended during a single RSV season?
Back: Five monthly doses.

Front: Preventive immunization option for pregnant individuals to protect newborns from severe RSV?
Back: RSV prefusion F protein vaccine (Abrysvo) administered between 32 and 36 weeks of gestation.

Front: Long-acting monoclonal antibody recommended for all infants under 8 months entering their first RSV season?
Back: Nirsevimab.

Front: Most common long-term pulmonary sequela observed in children who had severe RSV bronchiolitis in infancy?
Back: Recurrent wheezing episodes and an increased risk of childhood asthma.

Front: Primary mode of transmission for Respiratory Syncytial Virus in healthcare settings?
Back: Large-particle droplets and fomites via direct contact with contaminated surfaces or hands.

Front: Essential infection control precautions required for a patient hospitalized with RSV?
Back: Contact and Droplet precautions.

Front: Acid-base disturbance typically seen on a blood gas in an infant with impending respiratory failure from RSV?
Back: Respiratory acidosis due to profound alveolar hypoventilation and carbon dioxide retention.

Front: Definition of a positive response to a trial of hypertonic saline nebulization in bronchiolitis?
Back: Reduced length of hospital stay in infants, though its routine emergency department use remains controversial.

Front: Specific antiviral agent reserved exclusively for severe, life-threatening RSV in highly immunocompromised patients?
Back: Ribavirin (administered via aerosol).

Front: Typical incubation period for Respiratory Syncytial Virus after exposure?
Back: 4 to 6 days.

Front: Most common bacterial pathogen causing meningitis in neonates (0 to 28 days old)?
Back: Group B Streptococcus (Streptococcus agalactiae).

Front: Second and third most common bacterial pathogens causing neonatal meningitis?
Back: Escherichia coli (and other Gram-negative bacilli) and Listeria monocytogenes.

Front: Most common bacterial pathogen causing meningitis in infants and children over 1 month of age?
Back: Streptococcus pneumoniae.

Front: Bacterial pathogen responsible for meningitis outbreaks in college dorms or military barracks, characterized by a rapid purpuric rash?
Back: Neisseria meningitidis.

Front: Classic triad of symptoms seen in older children and adults with bacterial meningitis?
Back: Fever, headache, and nuchal rigidity (stiff neck).

Front: Definition of Kernig sign?
Back: Resistance or pain upon extension of the patient's knee when the hip is flexed at a 90-degree angle.

Front: Definition of Brudzinski sign?
Back: Involuntary flexion of the patient's hips and knees when the neck is passively flexed forward.

Front: Why are Kernig and Brudzinski signs frequently absent in infants under 12 months with meningitis?
Back: Due to the immature development of neuromuscular reflexes and lack of meningeal stretch response at that age.

Front: Most reliable clinical signs of meningitis in a young infant under 3 months?
Back: Hypothermia or fever, paradoxically increased irritability when held, lethargy, poor feeding, and a bulging fontanelle.

Front: Definitive diagnostic procedure required to confirm or rule out suspected pediatric meningitis?
Back: Lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis.

Front: Absolute clinical contraindications to performing an immediate lumbar puncture in a suspected meningitis patient?
Back: Signs of increased intracranial pressure (papilledema, unequal pupils), focal neurologic deficits, or cardiopulmonary instability.

Front: Immediate course of action if a lumbar puncture must be delayed due to clinical instability or focal deficits?
Back: Obtain blood cultures and immediately administer empiric intravenous antibiotics.

Front: Typical CSF opening pressure finding in acute bacterial meningitis?
Back: Elevated (usually greater than 200 mm H2O).

Front: Typical CSF white blood cell count and differential in bacterial meningitis?
Back: Markedly elevated WBC count (often >1000/microL) with a strong predominance of polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs).

Front: Typical CSF glucose concentration relative to serum glucose in bacterial meningitis?
Back: Significantly decreased, with a CSF-to-serum glucose ratio of less than 0.4.

Front: Typical CSF protein concentration finding in bacterial meningitis?
Back: Markedly elevated (often >100 mg/dL) due to increased blood-brain barrier permeability.

Front: Typical CSF findings in Viral (Aseptic) Meningitis?
Back: Normal glucose, normal to mildly elevated protein, and mildly elevated WBCs with lymphocytic predominance.

Front: Most common viral etiology responsible for aseptic meningitis in pediatric patients?
Back: Enteroviruses (such as Coxsackievirus and Echovirus).

Front: Diagnostic utility of adding a CSF Gram stain during workup?
Back: Rapidly identifies the bacterial morphology and guides tailored antibiotic choice ahead of culture results.

Front: Standard empiric antibiotic regimen for a neonate (under 28 days) with suspected bacterial meningitis?
Back: Ampicillin plus Cefotaxime (or Ampicillin plus an Aminoglycoside like Gentamicin).

Front: Why is Ceftriaxone strictly avoided in neonates requiring empiric treatment for meningitis?
Back: It displaces bilirubin from albumin, increasing the risk of kernicterus, and can precipitate with IV calcium.

Front: Standard empiric antibiotic regimen for a child over 1 month of age with suspected bacterial meningitis?
Back: Vancomycin plus a third-generation cephalosporin (Ceftriaxone or Cefotaxime).

Front: Purpose of adding Vancomycin to the empiric meningitis regimen for children over 1 month?
Back: To provide coverage for highly penicillin-resistant strains of Streptococcus pneumoniae.

Front: Purpose of adding Ampicillin specifically to the neonatal empiric meningitis regimen?
Back: To provide mandatory coverage for Listeria monocytogenes.

Front: Specific adjunctive medication shown to reduce the risk of sensorineural hearing loss in children with H. influenzae type b meningitis?
Back: Dexamethasone.

Front: Optimal timing for administering the first dose of adjunctive Dexamethasone in pediatric bacterial meningitis?
Back: Prior to or concurrently with the first dose of intravenous antibiotics.

Front: Most common long-term sensorineural complication resulting from childhood bacterial meningitis?
Back: Sensorineural hearing loss (due to labyrinthitis ossificans).

Front: Electrolyte abnormality frequently caused by inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) during bacterial meningitis?
Back: Hyponatremia.

Front: Immediate management step required for close household contacts of a patient diagnosed with Neisseria meningitidis?
Back: Chemoprophylaxis with Rifampin, Ciprofloxacin, or Ceftriaxone.

Front: Indication for cranial imaging (CT or MRI) during the course of treating bacterial meningitis?
Back: Persistent fever after 72 hours of appropriate therapy, new focal neurologic deficits, or enlarging head circumference.

Front: First step in the PALS pediatric assessment triangle?

Back: Appearance (evaluating muscle tone, interactiveness, and consolability).

Front: Second step in the PALS pediatric assessment triangle?

Back: Work of Breathing (evaluating retractions, stridor, wheezing, or nasal flaring).

Front: Third step in the PALS pediatric assessment triangle?

Back: Circulation to Skin (evaluating pallor, mottling, or cyanosis).

Front: Definition of a normal capillary refill time in a well-perfused pediatric patient?

Back: Two seconds or less.

Front: Definition of bradycardia requiring chest compressions in an infant or child with compromised perfusion despite oxygenation?

Back: Heart rate less than 60 beats per minute.

Front: Standard compression-to-ventilation ratio for two-rescuer pediatric CPR?

Back: 15 compressions to 2 ventilations.

Front: Standard compression-to-ventilation ratio for single-rescuer pediatric CPR?

Back: 30 compressions to 2 ventilations.

Front: Preferred location for checking a pulse in an unresponsive infant under one year of age?

Back: Brachial artery.

Front: Preferred location for checking a pulse in an unresponsive child over one year of age?

Back: Carotid or femoral artery.

Front: Recommended depth of chest compressions during CPR for an infant?

Back: At least one-third the anterior-posterior diameter of the chest, or approximately 1.5 inches (4 cm).

Front: Recommended depth of chest compressions during CPR for a child?

Back: At least one-third the anterior-posterior diameter of the chest, or approximately 2 inches (5 cm).

Front: Correct energy dose for the first shock during pediatric defibrillation for a shockable rhythm?

Back: 2 Joules per kilogram ($2\text{ J/kg}$).

Front: Correct energy dose for the second and subsequent shocks during pediatric defibrillation?

Back: 4 Joules per kilogram ($4\text{ J/kg}$).

Front: Maximum recommended single energy dose for pediatric defibrillation?

Back: 10 Joules per kilogram ($10\text{ J/kg}$) or the standard adult dose.

Front: First-line medication and dose administered for pediatric cardiac arrest or symptomatic bradycardia?

Back: Epinephrine at a dose of 0.01 mg/kg ($0.1\text{ mL/kg}$ of the 0.1 mg/mL concentration) intravenously or intraosseously.

Front: Preferred fluid type and initial volume bolus size recommended for a pediatric patient in hypovolemic or septic shock?

Back: 20 mL/kg of an isotonic crystalloid fluid (e.g., Normal Saline or Lactated Ringer's).

Front: Primary alternative vascular access method if intravenous access cannot be rapidly established in an unstable child?

Back: Intraosseous (IO) access.

Front: Classic dermatologic description of the rash seen in meningococcemia according to the Zitelli Atlas?

Back: Non-blanching, petechial or purpuric lesions that can rapidly coalesce into purpura fulminans.

Front: Distinction between a blanching rash and a non-blanching rash on physical exam?

Back: A non-blanching rash (petechiae/purpura) does not fade or turn white when pressure is applied, indicating extravasated blood.

Front: Classic otoscopic appearance of Tympanosclerosis in the Zitelli Atlas?

Back: Chalky white plaques or patches embedded within the tympanic membrane, representing organized collagen and calcium deposits.

Front: Significance of finding isolated tympanosclerosis patches on an asymptomatic child's ear exam?

Back: Benign finding usually indicating a history of recurrent otitis media or prior tympanostomy tube placement.

Front: Physical sign on a child's face indicating chronic mouth breathing secondary to adenoid hypertrophy?

Back: "Adenoid facies," characterized by an elongated face, open mouth, prominent upper incisors, and a pinched nose.

Front: Visual appearance of the tympanic membrane in a patient with a Cholesteatoma?

Back: A pearly white, keratinous mass typically located in the posterosuperior quadrant of the tympanic membrane or pars flaccida.

Front: Primary clinical danger associated with an untreated middle ear Cholesteatoma?

Back: Progressive bone destruction of the ossicles and mastoid, leading to permanent hearing loss or intracranial entry.

Front: Classic chest X-ray sign seen in children with croup (laryngotracheobronchitis) as a differential for respiratory distress?

Back: The "Steeple Sign," representing subglottic narrowing of the airway.

Front: Defining physical exam feature that distinguishes a simple febrile seizure from a complex febrile seizure?

Back: Simple febrile seizures are generalized, last less than 15 minutes, and do not recur within 24 hours.

Front: Defining physical exam feature of a complex febrile seizure?

Back: Focal onset, duration greater than 15 minutes, or multiple occurrences within a single 24-hour period.

Front: Age range in which febrile seizures typically occur?

Back: 6 months to 5 years of age.

Front: Primary management strategy for a child presenting with a brief, self-limiting, simple febrile seizure?

Back: Standard antipyretics for comfort and thorough reassurance of the parents, without routine neuroimaging or antiepileptic drugs.

Front: Pathogen responsible for Ramsay Hunt Syndrome affecting the ear and facial nerves?

Back: Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV) reactivation.

Front: Ear finding that distinguishes a traumatic tympanic membrane perforation from an infectious perforation?
Back: Traumatic perforations show ragged, linear edges with minimal inflammation, whereas infectious perforations show a hyperemic, inflamed membrane with purulent discharge.

Front: Why is high-dose Amoxicillin ineffective against Mycoplasma pneumoniae lower respiratory infections?
Back: Mycoplasma species lack a peptidoglycan cell wall, making them inherently resistant to all beta-lactam antibiotics.

Front: Preferred class of antibiotics for treating atypical pneumonia caused by Mycoplasma pneumoniae?
Back: Macrolides (e.g., Azithromycin).

Front: Diagnostic criteria for recurrent respiratory papillomatosis of the larynx, an uncommon differential for chronic stridor?
Back: Visualization of raspberry-like, exophytic growths on the vocal cords via laryngoscopy, caused by HPV types 6 or 11.

Front: Most accurate clinical indicator of proper endotracheal tube placement in a pediatric resuscitation setting?
Back: Presence of persistent waveform capnography showing bilateral chest expansion and equal breath sounds.

Front: Why is the respiratory rate faster in neonates and infants compared to older children?
Back: Infants have high metabolic demands and low pulmonary compliance, making them dependent on respiratory rate rather than tidal volume to alter minute ventilation.

Front: Primary mechanism of hypoxemia in a child with severe RSV bronchiolitis?
Back: Ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) mismatch caused by widespread mucous plugging and atelectasis.

Front: CSF parameter that remains most useful for diagnosing bacterial meningitis if antibiotics were given shortly before the lumbar puncture?
Back: Multiplex PCR testing or CSF cultures (though cultures are less sensitive post-antibiotics, PCR often remains positive).

Front: Characteristic feature of a "traumatic tap" during a lumbar puncture compared to true bacterial meningitis?
Back: Clearance of red blood cells from the first to the last tube collected, and a normal CSF glucose ratio.

Front: Preferred treatment for a child with symptomatic, culture-confirmed H. influenzae type b meningitis?
Back: Intravenous Ceftriaxone or Cefotaxime for 7 to 10 days.

Front: Recommended duration of antibiotic treatment for uncomplicated Streptococcus pneumoniae meningitis?
Back: 10 to 14 days.

Front: Recommended duration of antibiotic treatment for uncomplicated Neisseria meningitidis meningitis?
Back: 5 to 7 days.

Front: Standard duration of antibiotic treatment for Gram-negative bacillary meningitis in neonates?
Back: Minimum of 21 days, or 14 days beyond the first negative CSF culture.

Front: Most likely diagnosis for an infant presenting with high fever for 3 days that drops abruptly, followed by the appearance of a maculopapular rash?
Back: Roseola Infantum (Exanthem Subitum), caused by Human Herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6).

Front: Serious complication of Neisseria meningitidis infection involving acute adrenal gland failure?
Back: Waterhouse-Friderichsen Syndrome (hemorrhagic adrenal infarction).

Front: Expected appearance of a normal tympanic membrane on pneumatic otoscopy?
Back: Translucent, pearly gray membrane that moves briskly inward with positive pressure and outward with negative pressure.

Front: Recommended approach for managing a localized serous fluid level behind a non-inflamed tympanic membrane 2 weeks after an AOM episode?
Back: Watchful waiting, as post-AOM effusion takes up to 4 to 12 weeks to clear completely in many children.

Front: Cardinal sign of Gradenigo Syndrome, a rare complication of otitis media?
Back: Triad of otorrhea, deep facial pain in the distribution of the trigeminal nerve, and abducens nerve (CN VI) palsy.

Front: Initial empirical treatment for an infant with suspected bacterial meningitis who has an absolute contraindication to cephalosporins?
Back: Intravenous Meropenem plus Vancomycin.

Front: Anatomical difference in the pediatric Eustachian tube that predisposes young children to recurrent AOM?
Back: The tube is shorter, wider, and oriented more horizontally than in adults, facilitating the reflux of nasopharyngeal pathogens into the middle ear.

Front: Diagnostic criteria for Chronic Suppurative Otitis Media (CSOM)?
Back: Persistent purulent otorrhea through a perforated tympanic membrane lasting longer than 6 to 12 weeks.

Front: Primary bacterial pathogens isolated in Chronic Suppurative Otitis Media?
Back: Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus.

Front: Recommended initial treatment for uncomplicated Chronic Suppurative Otitis Media?
Back: Topical fluoroquinolone drops combined with regular aural toilet (suctioning/cleaning).

Front: Distinctive clinical presentation of Otitis Media with ANCA-associated vasculitis or granulomatosis with polyangiitis?
Back: Intractable otitis media unresponsive to standard antibiotics, accompanied by progressive sensorineural hearing loss and facial nerve palsy.

Front: Pathophysiology of Bezold abscess as a complication of acute mastoiditis?
Back: Perforation of the mastoid cortex allowing pus to track into the sheath of the sternocleidomastoid muscle.

Front: Most common cranial nerve palsy associated with acute mastoiditis or severe AOM?
Back: Facial nerve (Cranial Nerve VII) palsy due to inflammation within the Fallopian canal.

Front: Definitive management for an AOM patient presenting with acute facial nerve palsy?
Back: Urgent myringotomy with or without tympanostomy tube insertion, along with intravenous antibiotics.

Front: Condition characterized by bluish discoloration behind an intact tympanic membrane without acute infection signs?
Back: Hemotympanum, typically secondary to temporal bone fracture or head trauma.

Front: Visual presentation of a retraction pocket on otoscopy according to the Zitelli Atlas?
Back: The tympanic membrane is sucked inward, collapsing onto the ossicles, most frequently in the posterosuperior quadrant.

Front: Primary environmental risk factors that significantly increase a child's susceptibility to recurrent AOM?
Back: Exposure to secondhand tobacco smoke, daycare attendance, lack of breastfeeding, and pacifier use after six months of age.

Front: Most common cardiac condition predisposing an infant to high-risk, severe RSV bronchiolitis?
Back: Hemodynamically significant congenital heart disease, particularly acyanotic lesions with pulmonary hypertension.

Front: Rare, severe complication of RSV in infants involving sudden, profound neurological changes?
Back: RSV-associated encephalopathy, presenting with seizures or altered mental status.

Front: Primary reason routine chest physiotherapy (percussion and postural drainage) is avoided in RSV bronchiolitis?
Back: It has been shown to increase distress and chest wall pain without improving clinical outcomes or shortening hospital stays.

Front: Expected laboratory finding on a complete blood count (CBC) during a typical RSV infection?
Back: Normal white blood cell count or mild leukocytosis with a lymphocytic predominance, matching standard viral patterns.

Front: Most common bacterial pathogen isolated when secondary bacterial pneumonia complicates RSV bronchiolitis?
Back: Streptococcus pneumoniae or Staphylococcus aureus.

Front: Pathogen causing "Swelling of the epiglottis" which must be differentiated from severe RSV or croup in an unimmunized child?
Back: Haemophilus influenzae type b (causing acute Epiglottitis).

Front: Classic lateral neck X-ray finding in a child with acute epiglottitis?
Back: The "Thumbprint Sign," representing a swollen, enlarged epiglottis.

Front: Initial step in the management of a child with suspected epiglottitis?
Back: Maintain the child in a position of comfort and urgently transfer to the operating room for controlled intubation.

Front: Diagnostic differentiation between RSV bronchiolitis and Chlamydia trachomatis pneumonia in an 8-week-old infant?
Back: Chlamydia presents with a staccato cough, eosinophilia, and absence of fever, often with a history of neonatal conjunctivitis.

Front: Rationale for avoiding routine use of humidified mist tents (cool mist therapy) in RSV bronchiolitis management?
Back: Clinical studies have shown no objective improvement in respiratory scores or oxygenation parameters.

Front: Most common cause of recurrent bacterial meningitis in children with a history of head trauma?

Back: Anatomical CSF leak or dural defect, most frequently tracking Streptococcus pneumoniae.

Front: Rare condition involving a congenital dermal sinus tract extending into the spinal cord that predisposes to recurrent meningitis?

Back: Spina Bifida Occulta or spinal dysraphism.

Front: CSF profile characteristic of Tuberculous Meningitis?

Back: Markedly elevated protein, significantly low glucose, and moderate pleocytosis with a distinct lymphocytic predominance.

Front: Anatomical predilection of Tuberculous Meningitis exudates inside the brain?

Back: Thick exudates concentrate around the base of the brain (basilar meningitis), frequently causing cranial nerve palsies.

Front: Diagnosis for a child with fever, headache, altered mental status, and focal neurological signs with temporal lobe localized lesions on MRI?

Back: Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) Encephalitis.

Front: Preferred first-line empirical antiviral treatment for suspected neonatal HSV meningitis or encephalitis?

Back: High-dose intravenous Acyclovir ($20\text{ mg/kg}$ every 8 hours).

Front: Most common vector-borne viral etiology of pediatric meningoencephalitis in the United States?

Back: West Nile Virus (or other Arboviruses like La Crosse Encephalitis).

Front: Pathophysiologic mechanism driving cerebral edema during acute bacterial meningitis?

Back: Combination of cytotoxic edema (cell swelling), vasogenic edema (leaky blood-brain barrier), and interstitial edema (obstructed CSF outflow).

Front: Long-term ophthalmic complication that can occur secondary to untreated elevated intracranial pressure in bacterial meningitis?

Back: Optic atrophy or cortical blindness.

Front: Definition of "Waterhouse-Friderichsen Syndrome" associated with meningococcemia?

Back: Severe hemorrhagic necrosis of the adrenal glands leading to acute adrenal insufficiency and profound refractory shock.

Front: Defining difference between "compensated shock" and "hypotensive (decompensated) shock" in a pediatric patient?

Back: Compensated shock maintains a normal systolic blood pressure via tachycardia and vasoconstriction, whereas hypotensive shock shows a blood pressure below the 5th percentile for age.

Front: Formula used to calculate the lower limit of acceptable systolic blood pressure (5th percentile) for a child aged 1 to 10 years?

Back: $70\text{ mmHg} + (2 \times \text{age in years})$.

Front: Formula used to calculate the lower limit of acceptable systolic blood pressure for a term neonate (0 to 28 days)?

Back: Less than $60\text{ mmHg}$.

Front: Preferred initial intraosseous (IO) access insertion site in a pediatric resuscitation setting?

Back: Proximal tibia, approximately 1 to 2 cm distal to the tibial tuberosity on the flat medial surface.

Front: Primary sign of successful fluid resuscitation in a child presenting with hypovolemic shock?

Back: Decrease in heart rate, improvement in mental status, normal capillary refill time, and an increase in urine output to greater than $1\text{ mL/kg/hour}$.

Front: First-line medication and intervention strategy for a pediatric patient with documented Torsades de Pointes?

Back: Intravenous Magnesium Sulfate ($25\text{ to }50\text{ mg/kg}$).

Front: Target dose of Amiodarone for refractory Pulseless Ventricular Fibrillation or Ventricular Tachycardia in PALS?

Back: $5\text{ mg/kg}$ intravenous or intraosseous bolus (may repeat up to 3 times).

Front: Most common non-traumatic cause of cardiac arrest in the pediatric population?

Back: Progressive respiratory failure or asphyxia leading to bradycardia and asystole (hypoxic-ischemic arrest).

Front: Primary physiologic hazard associated with hyperventilating a pediatric patient during CPR?

Back: Increased intrathoracic pressure, which decreases venous return to the heart, dropping cardiac output and cerebral perfusion.

Front: Action taken in PALS if a pediatric patient displays organized electrical activity on the monitor but lacks a palpable pulse?

Back: Diagnose Pulseless Electrical Activity (PEA) and immediately resume chest compressions while administering Epinephrine every 3 to 5 minutes.

Front: Diagnosis for a 4-year-old child with an painful, swollen, erythematous earlobe following an insect bite, with an intact, normal tympanic membrane?
Back: Cellulitis of the external ear (perichondritis if sparing the lobule).

Front: Key physical exam feature distinguishing perichondritis from generalized pinna cellulitis?
Back: Perichondritis strictly spares the earlobe, as the lobe lacks underlying cartilage.

Front: Rationale behind performing an urgent ophthalmologic exam (fundoscopy) in a stable child with chronic papilledema post-meningitis?
Back: To evaluate for chronic increased intracranial pressure and rule out early optic nerve atrophy.

Front: Most likely diagnosis for a child who presents with sudden onset of unilateral wheezing and decreased breath sounds, without viral prodrome or fever?
Back: Foreign body aspiration.

Front: Why are systemic aminoglycosides (e.g., Gentamicin monotherapy) inappropriate for treating localized bacterial meningitis?
Back: Aminoglycosides have poor penetration through the blood-brain barrier into the cerebrospinal fluid, even when meninges are inflamed.

Front: Preferred diagnostic test to screen for localized hearing loss in an infant under 6 months of age post-meningitis?
Back: Auditory Brainstem Response (ABR) testing.

Front: Recommended screening test for hearing loss in a child older than 6 months of age who is developmentally appropriate?
Back: Visual Reinforcement Audiometry (VRA) or conditioned play audiometry.

Front: Diagnosis for an infant presenting with typical signs of meningitis who also exhibits a direct Coombs-positive hemolytic anemia and hepatosplenomegaly?
Back: Congenital Syphilis complicating neonatal presentation.

Front: Primary reason why the influenza vaccine is highly recommended as a preventative strategy against AOM in young children?
Back: Preventing the primary viral upper respiratory tract infection significantly reduces secondary bacterial Eustachian tube dysfunction and subsequent AOM.

Front: Expected CSF profile in a patient with Guillain-Barré Syndrome, a common neurological differential for weakness post-viral illness?
Back: Albuminocytologic dissociation (markedly elevated protein concentration with a completely normal white blood cell count).

Front: Pathogen responsible for Malignant Otitis Externa, a severe differential for ear pain in immunocompromised or diabetic pediatric patients?
Back: Pseudomonas aeruginosa.

Front: Distinctive physical exam finding in Malignant Otitis Externa according to the Zitelli Atlas?
Back: Granulation tissue visible on the floor of the external auditory canal at the osteocartilaginous junction.

Front: Preferred initial antibiotic treatment for a child diagnosed with Malignant Otitis Externa?
Back: Intravenous antipseudomonal antibiotics (e.g., Cefepime or Piperacillin-Tazobactam).

Front: Diagnostic criteria for Acute Otitis Media failure, prompting a switch to second-line antibiotics?
Back: Lack of clinical improvement (persistent fever, ear pain, or worsening bulging) after 48 to 72 hours of initial antibiotic therapy.

Front: Standard second-line oral antibiotic choice for AOM after failure of initial high-dose Amoxicillin?
Back: High-dose Amoxicillin-Clavulanate (Augmentin formatted at a 14:1 ratio to minimize diarrhea).

Front: Definition of a "dry perforation" of the tympanic membrane?
Back: A persistent hole in the tympanic membrane without current signs of active middle ear infection or otorrhea.

Front: Standard management strategy for an isolated, asymptomatic, small traumatic tympanic membrane perforation?
Back: Watchful waiting, as the majority of small traumatic perforations heal spontaneously within 4 to 6 weeks.

Front: Primary surgical procedure performed concurrently with tympanostomy tube placement if a child has chronic adenoiditis or nasal obstruction?
Back: Adenoidectomy.

Front: Specific cranial nerve examined to evaluate for suspected Gradenigo Syndrome?
Back: Abducens Nerve (Cranial Nerve VI), assessed by checking for lateral rectus palsy and diplopia on lateral gaze.

Front: Most accurate method to clinically distinguish severe Otitis Externa (Swimmer's Ear) from Acute Otitis Media on physical exam?
Back: Extreme pain elicited by manipulating the tragus or pulling the pinna upward, which is absent in isolated AOM.

Front: Primary physiological reason infants are obligate nasal breathers, making RSV-induced nasal congestion life-threatening?

Back: The infant tongue is large relative to the oral cavity, and the epiglottis sits higher in the pharynx, making oral breathing highly inefficient.

Front: Consequence of severe intercostal and subcostal retractions on an infant's work of breathing over time?

Back: Progressive respiratory muscle fatigue leading to respiratory failure and cardiac arrest.

Front: Underlying reason why an infant with severe RSV bronchiolitis might present with a normal $PaCO_2$ on an initial blood gas but still be in danger?

Back: A normal $PaCO_2$ in a severely tachypneic, tiring infant indicates impending respiratory failure, as they can no longer sustain the work required to hyperventilate.

Front: Most common non-infectious condition that mimics the recurrent wheezing patterns seen after an initial RSV infection?

Back: Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD) causing microaspiration.

Front: Specific laboratory test used to differentiate between a viral flare of bronchiolitis and a secondary bacterial infection in a toxic-appearing infant?

Back: Serum Procalcitonin or C-Reactive Protein (CRP) levels, which elevate significantly in bacterial infections compared to viral infections.

Front: Recommended isolation protocol variant required if an RSV patient is also co-infected with Adenovirus?

Back: Addition of strict Droplet and Eye protection precautions, as Adenovirus is highly contagious via conjunctival secretions.

Front: Why is the use of dynamic chest physiotherapy strictly discouraged in the PALS guidelines for acute bronchiolitis?

Back: It does not improve oxygenation or clear peripheral airway secretions, and it increases the risk of rib fractures and bronchial spasms.

Front: Long-term pulmonary function abnormality characteristically found on spirometry years after a severe RSV infection?

Back: Obstructive lung pattern with reduced $FEV_1/FVC$ ratio and air trapping.

Front: Indication for introducing Non-Invasive Positive Pressure Ventilation (NIPPV/CPAP) in an infant with worsening RSV bronchiolitis?

Back: Persistent hypoxemia despite high-flow supplemental oxygen, severe retractions, or a rising $PaCO_2$ with increased work of breathing.

Front: Only specific patient population where aerosolized Ribavirin should actively be considered for RSV lower respiratory tract disease?

Back: Severe, life-threatening RSV infection in highly immunocompromised hosts, such as recent bone marrow transplant recipients.

Front: Most common source of transmission for early-onset neonatal Group B Streptococcus meningitis?
Back: Vertical transmission from the maternal genital tract during labor and delivery.

Front: Most common source of transmission for late-onset neonatal Group B Streptococcus meningitis (occurring 7 to 90 days postpartum)?
Back: Horizontal transmission from healthcare workers, community contacts, or maternal skin.

Front: Specific Gram stain morphology characteristic of Streptococcus pneumoniae isolated from CSF?
Back: Gram-positive, lancet-shaped diplococci.

Front: Specific Gram stain morphology characteristic of Neisseria meningitidis isolated from CSF?
Back: Gram-negative, kidney bean-shaped diplococci.

Front: Specific Gram stain morphology characteristic of Haemophilus influenzae isolated from CSF?
Back: Gram-negative coccobacilli.

Front: Specific Gram stain morphology characteristic of Listeria monocytogenes isolated from CSF?
Back: Gram-positive rods or coccobacilli exhibiting characteristic tumbling motility in fluid cultures.

Front: Primary rationale for performing an urgent head ultrasound in a neonate with confirmed bacterial meningitis?
Back: To screen for early intraventricular hemorrhage, ventriculitis, or widening of the extra-axial spaces before fontanelle closure.

Front: Classic neuroimaging finding on a contrast-enhanced brain MRI during active bacterial meningitis?
Back: Diffuse leptomeningeal enhancement tracking along the cerebral sulci and basilar cisterns.

Front: Definitive management step for a child who develops a localized brain abscess as a complication of Citrobacter koseri neonatal meningitis?
Back: Prolonged intravenous antibiotic therapy (6 to 8 weeks) combined with neurosurgical aspiration or drainage.

Front: Expected CSF profile changes in a patient with a partially treated bacterial meningitis (antibiotics given 2 hours before lumbar puncture)?
Back: Neutrophilic pleocytosis and low glucose persist, but CSF Gram stain and culture sensitivity decrease significantly.

Front: Minimum acceptable systolic blood pressure (lower limit) for a child aged 1 month to 1 year?

Back: Less than $70\text{ mmHg}$.

Front: Target heart rate range for an infant under 1 year old experiencing symptomatic sinus tachycardia during a stress response?

Back: Typically less than $220\text{ beats per minute}$ (SVT is suspected if greater than $220\text{ bpm}$).

Front: Target heart rate range for a child over 1 year old experiencing symptomatic sinus tachycardia?

Back: Typically less than $180\text{ beats per minute}$ (SVT is suspected if greater than $180\text{ bpm}$).

Front: Definitive PALS intervention for a pediatric patient with Supraventricular Tachycardia (SVT) who is hemodynamically unstable?

Back: Immediate synchronized cardioversion at an initial dose of $0.5\text{ to }1\text{ J/kg}$.

Front: First-line pharmacological treatment for a hemodynamically stable pediatric patient with confirmed SVT?

Back: Adenosine administered as a rapid intravenous push ($0.1\text{ mg/kg}$, maximum first dose $6\text{ mg}$).

Front: Primary hazard of utilizing a standard uncuffed endotracheal tube in a pediatric patient requiring high airway pressures?

Back: Large air leaks around the larynx, resulting in hypoventilation and inaccurate tidal volume monitoring.

Front: Recommended method to calculate the appropriate internal diameter size for a cuffed endotracheal tube in a child aged 2 to 10 years?

Back: $(\text{Age in years} / 4) + 3.5$.

Front: Recommended method to calculate the appropriate internal diameter size for an uncuffed endotracheal tube in a child aged 2 to 10 years?

Back: $(\text{Age in years} / 4) + 4$.

Front: Preferred initial vasoactive medication infusion for a pediatric patient in warm (hyperdynamic) septic shock?

Back: Norepinephrine infusion (to correct profound systemic vasodilation).

Front: Preferred initial vasoactive medication infusion for a pediatric patient in cold (hypodynamic) septic shock with low cardiac output?

Back: Epinephrine infusion (to leverage potent inotropic and chronotropic effects).

Front: Visual presentation of Koplik spots according to the Zitelli Atlas, which must be differentiated from oral lesions of enteroviral meningitis?
Back: Small, bluish-white spots on an erythematous background located on the buccal mucosa opposite the lower molars (pathognomonic for Measles).

Front: Condition characterized by a high fever, severe inspiratory stridor, and a preference to sit leaning forward with the neck extended (tripod position)?
Back: Acute Epiglottitis.

Front: Visual description of "shagreen patches" and "ash-leaf spots" on a child's skin according to the Zitelli Atlas, pointing to a disease that causes infantile spasms mimicking meningitis?
Back: Tuberous Sclerosis.

Front: Most common cause of structural conductive hearing loss identified in children with a history of recurrent, severe AOM episodes?
Back: Ossicular chain discontinuity or fixation secondary to chronic inflammation.

Front: Expected physical exam finding when performing the Rinne test on a child with significant conductive hearing loss from chronic OME?
Back: Bone conduction is heard longer than or equal to air conduction (Negative Rinne test).

Front: Expected physical exam finding when performing the Weber test on a child with unilateral conductive hearing loss in the right ear?
Back: The sound lateralizes specifically to the right ear.

Front: Expected physical exam finding when performing the Weber test on a child with unilateral sensorineural hearing loss in the right ear?
Back: The sound lateralizes specifically to the left ear (the unaffected ear).

Front: Diagnostic criteria for a toxic-appearing child with fever, a barking cough, and thick, purulent tracheal secretions unresponsive to nebulized epinephrine?
Back: Bacterial Tracheitis (most commonly caused by Staphylococcus aureus).

Front: Primary treatment approach for a child diagnosed with confirmed Bacterial Tracheitis?
Back: Urgent airway stabilization under anesthesia combined with intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics.

Front: Condition characterized by a bulging, erythematous tympanic membrane with a distinct dark purple or black hue behind it in a newborn?
Back: Neonatal vascular anomaly or vascular tumor of the middle ear (e.g., glomus tympanicum), which must be differentiated from acute infectious otitis.

Section: Pediatrics (Introductory)
Topic: Pediatric vaccines & preventive care


Front: Minimum age for the first dose of the Hepatitis B vaccine?
Back: Birth (ideally within 24 hours of delivery).

Front: Minimum age required to administer the Rotavirus vaccine?
Back: 6 weeks.

Front: Maximum age for the first dose of the Rotavirus vaccine?
Back: 14 weeks and 6 days.

Front: Maximum age for the final dose of the Rotavirus vaccine?
Back: 8 months and 0 days.

Front: Routine ages for the 5-dose DTaP vaccine series?
Back: 2 months, 4 months, 6 months, 15–18 months, and 4–6 years.

Front: Age at which the Tdap booster is routinely recommended?
Back: 11–12 years.

Front: Routine ages for the 4-dose Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) series?
Back: 2 months, 4 months, 6 months, and 12–15 months.

Front: Routine ages for the 4-dose Pneumococcal Conjugate (PCV15/PCV20) series?
Back: 2 months, 4 months, 6 months, and 12–15 months.

Front: Routine ages for the 4-dose Inactivated Poliovirus (IPV) series?
Back: 2 months, 4 months, 6–18 months, and 4–6 years.

Front: Minimum age to initiate the annual Influenza vaccine?
Back: 6 months.

Front: Requirement for children aged 6 months to 8 years receiving the Influenza vaccine for the first time?
Back: Two doses separated by at least 4 weeks.

Front: Routine ages for the 2-dose Measles, Mumps, and Rubella (MMR) series?
Back: 12–15 months and 4–6 years.

Front: Minimum interval between the first and second doses of the MMR vaccine?
Back: 4 weeks.

Front: Routine ages for the 2-dose Varicella series?
Back: 12–15 months and 4–6 years.

Front: Routine ages for the 2-dose Hepatitis A series?
Back: 12–23 months, with the two doses separated by at least 6 months.

Front: Routine age to initiate the 2-dose Human Papillomavirus (HPV) series?
Back: 11–12 years (can start as early as age 9).

Front: Dosing schedule for HPV vaccine if initiated at or after age 15?
Back: 3 doses at 0, 1–2, and 6 months.

Front: Routine ages for the 2-dose Meningococcal ACWY (MenACWY) series?
Back: 11–12 years and a booster at age 16.

Front: Route of administration for MMR, Varicella, and MMRV vaccines?
Back: Subcutaneous (SQ).

Front: Route of administration for DTaP, Hib, HepA, HepB, PCV, and MenACWY?
Back: Intramuscular (IM).

Front: Route of administration for Rotavirus vaccines?
Back: Oral (PO).

Front: Preferred IM injection site for infants and toddlers (<2 years old)?
Back: Anterolateral aspect of the thigh (Vastus lateralis muscle).

Front: Preferred IM injection site for older children and adolescents (≥3 years old)?
Back: Deltoid muscle.

Front: Only absolute contraindication to all vaccines?
Back: Severe allergic reaction (e.g., anaphylaxis) after a previous dose or to a vaccine component.

Front: General rule for vaccinating a child with a mild acute illness (e.g., low-grade fever, mild otitis media, cold)?
Back: Vaccinate as scheduled; mild illness is not a contraindication.

Front: General contraindication for all live-attenuated vaccines (MMR, Varicella, Rotavirus, LAIV)?
Back: Severe immunodeficiency (e.g., advanced HIV, chemotherapy, high-dose systemic steroids).

Front: Absolute contraindication specific to the Rotavirus vaccine?
Back: History of intussusception or severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID).

Front: Specific contraindication to the DTaP vaccine regarding the central nervous system?
Back: Encephalopathy within 7 days of a previous pertussis-containing vaccine dose.

Front: Precaution for DTaP concerning neurological stability?
Back: Progressive or unstable neurological disorders (e.g., uncontrolled epilepsy) until the condition stabilizes.

Front: Length of time to delay live vaccines after receiving intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) or packed red blood cells?
Back: 3 to 11 months, depending on the specific blood product and dose.

Front: Maximum daily dose and duration of systemic prednisone that allows for immediate administration of live vaccines?
Back: Less than 2 mg/kg/day (or <20 mg/day total) for less than 14 days.

Front: Precaution for the Live Attenuated Influenza Vaccine (LAIV) regarding underlying chronic conditions?
Back: Asthma or reactive airway disease in children aged 2–4 years.

Front: Is a family history of seizures a contraindication to the MMRV vaccine?
Back: No, but it is a precaution due to a slightly higher risk of febrile seizures compared to separate MMR and Varicella injections.

Front: Management of a newborn whose mother is HBsAg-positive?
Back: Administer HepB vaccine and Hepatitis B Immune Globulin (HBIG) within 12 hours of birth.

Front: Post-vaccination serologic testing protocol for an infant born to an HBsAg-positive mother?
Back: Test for HBsAg and anti-HBs at age 9–12 months (or 1–2 months after the final HepB dose).

Front: Management of a newborn weighing <2000g whose mother is HBsAg-negative?
Back: Delay the first HepB vaccine dose until 1 month of age or at hospital discharge.

Front: Management of a newborn weighing <2000g whose mother is HBsAg-positive?
Back: Give HepB vaccine and HBIG within 12 hours of birth, but do not count the birth dose toward the 3-dose series.

Front: Target population for the Pneumococcal Polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23)?
Back: Children aged 2–18 years with underlying chronic medical conditions (e.g., asplenia, sickle cell, HIV, chronic heart/lung disease) after completing the PCV series.

Front: Indication for Meningococcal Serogroup B (MenB) vaccination?
Back: Adolescents aged 16–23 years (preferred 16–18) and high-risk patients aged ≥10 years (e.g., anatomic/functional asplenia, complement deficiency).

Front: Indication for early MMR vaccination at 6–11 months of age?
Back: International travel outside the United States.

Front: Does an early MMR dose given at 6–11 months count toward the 2-dose routine pediatric series?
Back: No, the child still requires 2 routine doses at ≥12 months of age.

Front: Routine recommendation for Tdap vaccination during pregnancy?
Back: 1 dose during every pregnancy, ideally between 27 and 36 weeks gestation, regardless of prior vaccination history.

Front: Frequency of well-child visits recommended by the AAP during the first year of life?
Back: At 3–5 days, and 1, 2, 4, 6, and 9 months of age.

Front: Frequency of well-child visits recommended by the AAP during the second year of life?
Back: At 12, 15, 18, and 24 months of age.

Front: Universal age recommendations for autism spectrum disorder (ASD) screening?
Back: 18 months and 24 months.

Front: Age at which universal developmental screening using a validated tool is recommended?
Back: 9, 18, and 30 months.

Front: Universal age for maternal depression screening during well-child visits?
Back: 1, 2, 4, and 6 months.

Front: Universal age for initial visual acuity screening using standard charts?
Back: 4 years (can attempt at 3 years if cooperative).

Front: Age at which formal, objective audiometry hearing screening begins?
Back: 4 years.

Front: Universal screening age for dyslipidemia using a fasting or non-fasting lipid panel?
Back: Once between 9 and 11 years, and once between 17 and 21 years.

Front: Indication for targeted lead screening in pediatric patients?
Back: Medicaid enrollment, living in high-prevalence zip codes, or housing built before 1978.

Front: Routine ages for universal hemoglobin/hematocrit screening for anemia?
Back: 12 months of age.

Front: Age at which blood pressure measurement should begin routinely at every well-child visit?
Back: 3 years.

Front: Initial screening method for scoliosis in adolescents?
Back: Adams Forward Bend Test.

Front: Recommended daily Vitamin D supplementation for exclusively breastfed infants?
Back: 400 IU daily starting within the first few days of life.

Front: When should Vitamin D supplementation stop for formula-fed infants?
Back: When they consume at least 1 liter (approx. 32 oz) per day of Vitamin D-fortified formula or whole milk.

Front: Age at which iron supplementation should begin for exclusively breastfed, term infants?
Back: 4 months of age (at 1 mg/kg/day of elemental iron) until iron-containing foods are introduced.

Front: Iron supplementation rule for preterm infants?
Back: 2 mg/kg/day of elemental iron starting at 2 weeks of age until 12 months of age.

Front: Age at which solid foods should be introduced into an infant's diet?
Back: Around 6 months of age, when developmentally ready.

Front: Recommended age to introduce allergenic foods (e.g., peanut products) to prevent allergies?
Back: Around 4–6 months of age, after other solids are tolerated, especially in infants with severe eczema or egg allergy.

Front: Maximum recommended intake of whole cow's milk per day for toddlers aged 1–5 years?
Back: 16 to 24 ounces (approx. 500–700 mL) per day to prevent iron-deficiency anemia.

Front: Age at which low-fat (1% or skim) milk can be introduced instead of whole milk?
Back: 2 years of age (can consider at 12 months if overweight or family history of obesity/cardiovascular disease).

Front: Age before which raw honey is completely contraindicated?
Back: 12 months (due to the risk of infant botulism from Clostridium botulinum spores).

Front: Most sensitive indicator of a child's nutritional status or acute illness?
Back: Weight changes (monitored via standardized growth charts).

Front: Definition of failure to thrive (FTT) based on growth chart percentiles?
Back: Weight below the 3rd to 5th percentile, or a drop across two major percentile lines.

Front: Recommended safe sleep position to prevent Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS)?
Back: Supine (flat on the back) for every sleep period.

Front: Essential components of a safe infant sleep environment?
Back: Firm, flat sleep surface, in a crib/bassinette, with no loose blankets, pillows, bumper pads, or soft toys.

Front: Recommended duration for infants to remain in a rear-facing car safety seat?
Back: Until they reach the highest weight or height limit allowed by their car seat manufacturer.

Front: Criteria for switching a child from a belt-positioning booster seat to a standard vehicle lap-and-shoulder belt?
Back: Height of 4 feet 9 inches (143 cm) and typically between 8 and 12 years of age.

Front: Age before which children should always sit in the back seat of a vehicle equipped with passenger airbags?
Back: 13 years.

Front: Primary mechanism of cardiac arrest in pediatric patients according to PALS?
Back: Asphyxial/respiratory failure or progressive shock (rarely primary cardiac events).

Front: Definition of pediatric tachycardia requiring immediate evaluation based on age groups?
Back: Infant heart rate >220/min or child heart rate >180/min with narrow QRS complexes.

Front: First-line intervention for a pediatric patient with symptomatic bradycardia and compromised cardiopulmonary status despite oxygenation/ventilation?
Back: Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) if the heart rate remains <60/min.

Front: Classic skin finding in Zitelli's Atlas for a child with congenital dermal melanocytosis (Mongolian spots)?
Back: Flat, blue-grey patches with indefinite borders, most commonly located over the sacrum or buttocks.

Front: Physical exam finding that distinguishes erythema toxicum neonatorum from neonatal herpes?
Back: Erythema toxicum presents with eosinophils on a Wright-stained smear; herpes presents with multinucleated giant cells on a Tzanck smear.

Front: Anticipatory guidance topic regarding water safety for toddlers?
Back: Continuous, touch-supervision by an adult whenever the child is in or near water (including bathtubs and swimming pools).

Front: Water temperature threshold recommended for home water heaters to prevent scald burns?
Back: Below 120°F (49°C).

Front: Universal age recommendation for the first dental visit?
Back: By 12 months of age, or within 6 months of the eruption of the first tooth.


Front: Minimum interval between doses 1 and 2 of the HepB series in a catch-up schedule?
Back: 4 weeks.

Front: Minimum age to receive the Tdap vaccine if a child missed the childhood DTaP series?
Back: 7 years.

Front: Number of doses required for the HPV vaccine series if the first dose is given at age 16?
Back: 3 doses.

Front: Complication of Haemophilus influenzae type b that Hib vaccination effectively prevents?
Back: Epiglottitis, meningitis, and septic arthritis.

Front: Primary reason for administering the Rubella vaccine component to children?
Back: To prevent Congenital Rubella Syndrome (CRS) in the offspring of pregnant women exposed to infected children.

Front: Major neurological complication associated with Measles infection that the MMR vaccine prevents?
Back: Subacute Sclerosing Panencephalitis (SSPE).

Front: Serious complication of Mumps infection prevented by vaccination in post-pubertal males?
Back: Orchitis (which can cause testicular atrophy and, rarely, infertility).

Front: Pneumococcal serotype coverage type utilized in PCV15/PCV20?
Back: Capsular polysaccharide conjugated to a nontoxic diphtheria toxin protein carrier (T-cell dependent response).

Front: Pneumococcal vaccine type that induces a T-cell independent response?
Back: PPSV23 (unconjugated polysaccharide vaccine).

Front: Why is PPSV23 ineffective in children under 2 years of age?
Back: Immature splenic function and inability to mount an immune response to T-cell independent polysaccharide antigens.

Front: Diagnostic significance of a white pupillary reflex (leukocoria) on a red reflex exam?

Back: Urgent referral to an ophthalmologist for suspected retinoblastoma or congenital cataracts.

Front: Physical signs of developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) during newborn screening?

Back: Positive Barlow (dislocation) or Ortolani (reduction) maneuvers, or asymmetric thigh skin folds.

Front: Standard imaging modality used to screen for DDH in infants under 4 months of age?

Back: Hip ultrasonography.

Front: Standard imaging modality used to screen for DDH in infants 4 months and older?

Back: AP and frog-leg lateral radiographs of the pelvis.

Front: Screening recommendation for cryptorchidism (undescended testes)?

Back: Palpate at every well-child visit; refer to a specialist if undescended by 6 months of age.

Front: Anticipatory guidance regarding screen time limits for children under 18 months of age?

Back: Avoid screen time entirely, except for video chatting.

Front: Recommended limit for recreational screen time for children aged 2 to 5 years?

Back: No more than 1 hour per day of high-quality programming.

Front: Best preventive counseling regarding secondhand smoke exposure in infants?

Back: Strict elimination of smoking in the home and car to prevent lower respiratory tract infections and SIDS.

Front: Initial management strategy for a toddler presenting with physiological bowed legs (genu varum) under 2 years of age?

Back: Reassurance and observation, as it typically resolves spontaneously by age 2.

Front: Diagnostic criteria on a growth chart that suggests constitutional delay of growth?

Back: Growth velocity is normal, but height and weight trail parallel to and below the 3rd percentile, with a delayed bone age.

Front: Expected age by which an infant should double their birth weight?

Back: 4 to 5 months of age.

Front: Expected age by which an infant should triple their birth weight?

Back: 12 months of age.

Front: Expected age by which an infant should quadruple their birth weight?

Back: 24 months of age.

Front: Average increase in head circumference during the first year of life?

Back: Approximately 12 centimeters.

Front: Manifestation of congenital hypothyroidism on a newborn preventative screening panel if left untreated?

Back: Intellectual disability, macroglossia, umbilical hernia, hypotonia, and prolonged jaundice.

Front: Manifestation of phenylketonuria (PKU) on a newborn preventative screening panel if left untreated?

Back: Intellectual disability, musty/mousy body odor, microcephaly, and hypopigmentation.

Front: Immediate screening step required for an infant with a suspected critical congenital heart defect (CCHD)?

Back: Pulse oximetry screening in the right hand and either foot after 24 hours of life.

Front: Positive screening result for Critical Congenital Heart Disease (CCHD) via pulse oximetry?

Back: Any $\text{SpO}_2 < 90\%$, $\text{SpO}_2 < 95\%$ in both extremities on 3 measures separated by 1 hour, or a $>3\%$ difference between hand and foot.

Front: Clinical significance of a dynamic, musical, vibratory systolic murmur at the lower left sternal border in an asymptomatic 4-year-old child?

Back: Still's murmur, which is benign/innocent and requires reassurance.

Front: First physical indicator of female puberty (thelarche) on a preventative exam?

Back: Breast budding under the areola, typically occurring between 8 and 13 years of age.

Front: First physical indicator of male puberty (gonadarche) on a preventative exam?

Back: Testicular enlargement ($\ge 4 \text{ mL}$ volume or $\ge 2.5 \text{ cm}$ length), typically occurring between 9 and 14 years of age.

Front: Primary preventative screening done for adolescents regarding mental health?

Back: Annual screening for depression using the PHQ-2 or PHQ-A questionnaires starting at age 12.

Front: Age at which universal screening for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) is recommended at least once?

Back: Between 15 and 18 years of age.

Front: Preventative strategy for dental caries in communities without fluoridated tap water?

Back: Oral fluoride supplementation starting at 6 months of age.

Front: What finding on a pediatric skin exam should trigger an evaluation for Neurofibromatosis Type 1 (NF1)?

Back: Six or more café-au-lait macules greater than 5 mm in diameter in prepubertal children.

Front: True or false: The MMR vaccine causes autism due to the thimerosal preservative.
Back: False; multiple large-scale studies have disproven any link, and MMR has never contained thimerosal.

Front: Expected, benign local physical exam findings within 48 hours of a DTaP injection?
Back: Erythema, mild swelling, and localized tenderness at the injection site.

Front: Definition of a hypotonic-hyporesponsive episode (HHE) following vaccination?
Back: Sudden onset of pallor, hypotonia, and unresponsiveness within 48 hours of vaccination (most commonly DTaP).

Front: Is a history of a hypotonic-hyporesponsive episode (HHE) an absolute contraindication to future DTaP doses?
Back: No, it is a precaution; the benefits of pertussis coverage usually outweigh the risks.

Front: What rare, self-limiting neurological condition is historically monitored after the Influenza vaccine?
Back: Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS) within 6 weeks of administration.

Front: Can a child with a known, non-anaphylactic egg allergy receive the routine Influenza vaccine?
Back: Yes, any age-appropriate Influenza vaccine (inactivated or live) can be given safely without special precautions.

Front: Can a child living with a pregnant mother safely receive live vaccines like MMR and Varicella?
Back: Yes, transmission of the vaccine viruses to contacts is extremely rare.

Front: Under what specific circumstance should a child avoid a vaccine due to a household contact?
Back: Avoid Live Attenuated Influenza Vaccine (LAIV) if a household contact is severely immunocompromised and in a protected environment (e.g., bone marrow transplant unit).

Front: Most common benign side effect noted 7 to 12 days after receiving the MMR vaccine?
Back: Transient low-grade fever and a mild, non-contagious maculopapular rash.

Front: Management of a child who develops a small, non-painful subcutaneous nodule at a DTaP injection site?
Back: Reassurance; it is a common granulomatous reaction to the aluminum adjuvant and resolves spontaneously.

Front: Post-exposure prophylaxis for an unvaccinated 10-month-old infant exposed to Measles within 72 hours?

Back: Administer the MMR vaccine.

Front: Post-exposure prophylaxis for an immunocompromised child exposed to Measles within 6 days?

Back: Administer Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG).

Front: Post-exposure prophylaxis for an unimmunized 8-month-old infant exposed to Varicella within 96 hours?

Back: Administer Varicella Zoster Immune Globulin (VarZIG).

Front: Post-exposure prophylaxis for an unimmunized 15-month-old child exposed to Varicella within 3 to 5 days?

Back: Administer the Varicella vaccine.

Front: Management of a clean, minor wound in a child with an unknown or <3 dose DTaP history?

Back: Give the DTaP/Tdap vaccine only; no Tetanus Immune Globulin (TIG) is required.

Front: Management of a deep, contaminated wound in a child with an unknown or <3 dose DTaP history?

Back: Give both the DTaP/Tdap vaccine and Tetanus Immune Globulin (TIG).

Front: Management of a clean, minor wound in a child with $\ge 3$ documented DTaP doses, with the last dose 7 years ago?

Back: Reassurance and wound care; no vaccine or TIG is needed because the last dose was $<10$ years ago.

Front: Management of a contaminated wound in a child with $\ge 3$ documented DTaP doses, with the last dose 7 years ago?

Back: Give a DTaP/Tdap booster because the last dose was $>5$ years ago for a high-risk wound.

Front: What additional vaccine is recommended for pediatric travelers visiting areas of sub-Saharan Africa known as the "Meningitis Belt"?

Back: Meningococcal ACWY (MenACWY) vaccine booster or early dose.

Front: Minimum age to receive the Yellow Fever vaccine for pediatric international travelers?

Back: 9 months (due to the risk of vaccine-associated encephalitis in younger infants).

Front: Physical exam finding on the Hirschberg (corneal light reflex) test that indicates strabismus?
Back: Asymmetrical placement of the light reflex point on the pupil or iris.

Front: Gold-standard management for a child diagnosed with amblyopia due to strabismus?
Back: Patching the unaffected (stronger) eye or using atropine drops to blur the stronger eye, forcing use of the weaker eye.

Front: Objective screening method used for newborn hearing screening in the nursery?
Back: Otoacoustic Emissions (OAE) or Automated Auditory Brainstem Response (AABR).

Front: Interpretation of an infant who "fails" the initial newborn hearing screening?
Back: Repeat screening before 1 month of age; if still failed, comprehensive audiologic evaluation by 3 months.

Front: Primary dental advice given to parents to prevent Early Childhood Caries (baby bottle tooth decay)?
Back: Never put an infant to sleep with a bottle containing milk, juice, or sweetened liquids.

Front: When should toothpaste containing fluoride be introduced into a child’s oral care routine?
Back: At the eruption of the first tooth, using a tiny "smear" (size of a grain of rice).

Front: Amount of fluoride toothpaste recommended for children aged 3 to 6 years?
Back: A pea-sized amount.

Front: Confirmation step required if a capillary finger-stick lead screening level returns elevated ($\ge 3.5\ \mu\text{g/dL}$)?

Back: Obtain a confirmatory venous blood lead level.

Front: First-line intervention for a venous blood lead level between $3.5\ \mu\text{g/dL}$ and $44\ \mu\text{g/dL}$?

Back: Environmental investigation to identify and remove the lead source, and nutritional optimization (iron, calcium).

Front: Threshold venous lead level at which oral chelation therapy (e.g., Succimer) is indicated?

Back: $\ge 45\ \mu\text{g/dL}$.

Front: Emergency management for a child presenting with a venous lead level $\ge 70\ \mu\text{g/dL}$ or acute lead encephalopathy?

Back: Hospitalization and dual inpatient chelation therapy with Dimercaprol (BAL) and Calcium Disodium EDTA.

Front: Most common cause of a false-positive result on a routine newborn screening card?

Back: Sample collection occurred before 24 hours of life (especially true for PKU and congenital hypothyroidism metabolic markers).

Front: Diagnostic follow-up required for a newborn whose screening panel indicates elevated Immunoreactive Trypsinogen (IRT)?

Back: Sweat chloride testing to evaluate for Cystic Fibrosis.

Front: Red flag developmental timeline: Child not walking independently by what age?

Back: 18 months.

Front: Red flag developmental timeline: Child not babbling or using gestures by what age?

Back: 12 months.

Front: Red flag developmental timeline: Child without single words by what age?

Back: 16 months.

Front: Red flag developmental timeline: Child without 2-word meaningful phrases by what age?

Back: 24 months.

Front: The ultimate developmental rule regarding any loss of language or social skills at any age?

Back: Immediate referral for formal developmental, audiologic, and neurological evaluation.

Front: Expected physical growth pattern of a child with Familial Short Stature?

Back: Growth curve runs parallel to but below the 3rd percentile, with a normal growth velocity and a normal bone age equal to chronological age.

Front: Correct compression-to-ventilation ratio for two-rescuer pediatric CPR?

Back: 15:2.

Front: Correct compression-to-ventilation ratio for single-rescuer pediatric CPR?

Back: 30:2.

Front: Target depth for chest compressions during infant CPR?

Back: Approximately 1.5 inches (4 cm), or one-third the anterior-posterior diameter of the chest.

Front: Target depth for chest compressions during child CPR?

Back: Approximately 2 inches (5 cm), or one-third the anterior-posterior diameter of the chest.

Front: Preferred method for opening the airway in a pediatric trauma patient with a suspected neck injury?

Back: Jaw-thrust maneuver without head extension.

Front: Definition of hypotension in a child aged 1 to 10 years based on systolic blood pressure?

Back: $\text{Systolic BP} < 70\ \text{mmHg} + (2 \times \text{age in years})$.

Front: First line fluid resuscitation volume bolus size for a pediatric patient in hypovolemic shock?

Back: $20\ \text{mL/kg}$ of an isotonic crystalloid (e.g., Normal Saline or Lactated Ringer's).

Front: Pathognomonic oral cavity finding during the prodromal phase of Measles (Rubeola)?

Back: Koplik spots (small blue-white spots on an erythematous background on the buccal mucosa opposite the molars).

Front: Description of the classic rash of Varicella (Chickenpox)?

Back: Pruritic, maculopapular rash that rapidly evolves into vesicles on an erythematous base ("dewdrops on a rose petal"), appearing in various stages of healing.

Front: Skin manifestation of Erythema Infectiosum (Fifth Disease) caused by Parvovirus B19?

Back: Bright red rash on the cheeks ("slapped cheek" appearance) followed by a lacy, reticular rash on the trunk and extremities.

Front: Clinical presentation of Roseola Infantum (Exanthem Subitum) caused by HHV-6?

Back: High fever ($>40^\circ\text{C}$ or $104^\circ\text{F}$) for 3–5 days that abruptly drops, followed immediately by the appearance of a maculopapular, non-pruritic rash.

Front: Physical exam sign of Hand-Foot-and-Mouth Disease caused by Coxsackievirus A16?

Back: Painful oral ulcers (herpangina) accompanied by maculopapular or vesicular lesions on the palms of the hands and soles of the feet.

Section: Pediatrics (Introductory)
Topic: Congenital anomalies overview


Front: Definition of a malformation?
Back: An intrinsic morphogenetic defect in tissue development from the onset of conception.

Front: Definition of a deformation?
Back: An extrinsic mechanical force that alters the shape of normally formed tissue.

Front: Definition of a disruption?
Back: A destructive breakdown of an organ or tissue that was previously developing normally.

Front: Definition of a dysplasia?
Back: Abnormal cellular organization or architectural arrangement within a specific tissue type.

Front: Example of a classic mechanical deformation?
Back: Clubfoot (talipes equinovarus) caused by severe oligohydramnios or uterine crowding.

Front: Example of a classic tissue disruption?
Back: Amniotic band syndrome causing digital or limb amputations.

Front: Definition of a congenital "sequence"?
Back: A single primary structural defect that leads to a cascade of secondary downstream anomalies.

Front: Primary defect in Pierre Robin sequence?
Back: Micrognathia (mandibular hypoplasia).

Front: Downstream triad seen in Pierre Robin sequence?
Back: Micrognathia, glossoptosis, and a U-shaped cleft palate.

Front: Primary defect in Potter sequence?
Back: Renal agenesis or severe obstructive uropathy causing profound oligohydramnios.

Front: Classic physical features of Potter sequence?
Back: Flattened facies, low-set ears, compressed lungs (pulmonary hypoplasia), and limb deformities.

Front: Definition of a congenital "syndrome"?
Back: A recognized, recurring pattern of multiple anomalies with a single, specific underlying etiology.

Front: Definition of an "association" in genetics?
Back: A non-random statistical clustering of anomalies that occur together more often than by chance alone, without a known single cause.

Front: What does the acronym VACTERL stand for?
Back: Vertebral, Anal atresia, Cardiac, Tracheoesophageal fistula, Renal, and Limb anomalies.

Front: Most common type of single minor anomaly found in normal newborns?
Back: Preauricular tags or pits.

Front: Clinical significance of finding three or more minor congenital anomalies in a newborn?
Back: High statistical probability of an underlying, undiagnosed major structural anomaly.

Front: Major cause of death in the first year of life in developed countries?
Back: Congenital malformations.

Front: Percentage of congenital anomalies with a completely unknown etiology?
Back: Approximately 50% to 60%.

Front: Gestational period of highest susceptibility to major structural teratogens?
Back: Weeks 3 through 8 of embryonic development (organogenesis).

Front: Effect of a severe teratogenic insult during the first 2 weeks of gestation?
Back: An "all-or-none" effect, resulting in either embryonic death or completely normal recovery.

Front: Definition of a phenocopy?
Back: An environmentally induced phenotype that matches an identical phenotype caused by a genetic mutation.

Front: Developmental origin of the palatine tonsillar crypts?
Back: Second pharyngeal pouch.

Front: Developmental origin of the inferior parathyroid glands and thymus?
Back: Third pharyngeal pouch.

Front: Developmental origin of the superior parathyroid glands?
Back: Fourth pharyngeal pouch.

Front: Mechanism behind a persistent thyroglossal duct cyst?
Back: Failure of the thyroglossal duct to atrophy during thyroid migration from the foramen caecum.

Front: Most common chromosomal cause of moderate intellectual disability?
Back: Trisomy 21 (Down syndrome).

Front: Most common genetic cause of universal/global developmental delay in males?
Back: Fragile X syndrome.

Front: Recurrence risk of Down syndrome if caused by a Robertsonian translocation in a maternal carrier?
Back: Approximately 10% to 15%.

Front: Recurrence risk of Down syndrome if caused by a Robertsonian translocation in a paternal carrier?
Back: Approximately 1% to 2%.

Front: Most common cardiac defect associated with Trisomy 21?
Back: Endocardial cushion defect (Atrioventricular septal defect).

Front: Gastrointestinal anomalies classically associated with Down syndrome?
Back: Duodenal atresia and Hirschsprung disease.

Front: Leukemia type with increased risk in Down syndrome patients under 3 years old?
Back: Acute Megakaryoblastic Leukemia (AMKL).

Front: Triad of clinical features pathognomonic for Trisomy 18 (Edwards syndrome)?
Back: Micrognathia, clenched fists with overlapping fingers, and rocker-bottom feet.

Front: Most common structural cardiac defect in Trisomy 18?
Back: Ventricular septal defect (VSD).

Front: Classic midline defects associated with Trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome)?
Back: Holoprosencephaly, cleft lip/palate, and omphalocele.

Front: Pathognomonic limb/skin findings in Trisomy 13?
Back: Postaxial polydactyly and cutis aplasia of the scalp.

Front: Karyotype of Turner syndrome?
Back: 45,X.

Front: Cardinal cardiovascular anomaly in Turner syndrome?
Back: Coarctation of the aorta (bicuspid aortic valve is also common).

Front: Lymphatic anomaly responsible for webbed neck and puffy feet in Turner syndrome?
Back: Congenital lymphedema due to lymphatic dysgenesis (cystic hygroma).

Front: Karyotype of Klinefelter syndrome?
Back: 47,XXY.

Front: Clinical presentation of Klinefelter syndrome in adolescence?
Back: Tall stature, gynecomastia, small firm testes, and hypergonadotropic hypogonadism.

Front: Genetic mechanism behind Prader-Willi syndrome?
Back: Paternal deletion or maternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 15q11-q13.

Front: Phenotypic presentation of Prader-Willi syndrome in infancy?
Back: Severe hypotonia, poor feeding, and failure to thrive.

Front: Phenotypic switch in Prader-Willi syndrome during early childhood?
Back: Hyperphagia leading to early-onset morbid obesity and type 2 diabetes.

Front: Genetic mechanism behind Angelman syndrome?
Back: Maternal deletion or paternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 15q11-q13 (loss of UBE3A expression).

Front: Classic clinical triad of Angelman syndrome?
Back: Inappropriate laughter ("happy puppet"), severe ataxia, and absent speech.

Front: Chromosomal microdeletion in DiGeorge syndrome?
Back: 22q11.2 microdeletion.

Front: Developmental mechanism altered in DiGeorge syndrome?
Back: Aberrant development of the 3rd and 4th pharyngeal pouches.

Front: Clinical presentation of CATCH-22 in DiGeorge syndrome?
Back: Cardiac defects, Abnormal facies, Thymic hypoplasia, Cleft palate, and Hypocalcemia.

Front: Immediate life-threatening metabolic complication in DiGeorge syndrome newborns?
Back: Hypocalcemic tetany or seizures due to parathyroid aplasia.

Front: Classic facial triad of Fetal Alcohol Syndrome (FAS)?
Back: Short palpebral fissures, smooth philtrum, and a thin upper lip vermilion.

Front: Central nervous system manifestation of Fetal Alcohol Syndrome?
Back: Microcephaly with structural brain anomalies and neurobehavioral impairment.

Front: Cardiac anomaly specifically associated with maternal lithium use?
Back: Ebstein anomaly (atrialization of the right ventricle).

Front: Pathognomonic skeletal anomaly of Fetal Warfarin Syndrome?
Back: Chondrodysplasia punctata (stippled epiphyses) and nasal hypoplasia.

Front: Classic structural defects caused by Fetal Hydantoin (Phenytoin) Syndrome?
Back: Craniofacial clefts, hypoplastic nails, and hypoplastic distal phalanges.

Front: Phenotypic presentation of Fetal Retinoic Acid (Isotretinoin) Embryopathy?
Back: Severe microtia/otia, hydrocephalus, and conotruncal cardiac malformations.

Front: Primary congenital defect associated with maternal Valproic Acid use?
Back: Neural tube defects (specifically lumbosacral spina bifida).

Front: Mechanism by which Valproic Acid induces neural tube defects?
Back: Interference with folate metabolism and antagonism of folate transport.

Front: Structural anomalies associated with uncontrolled pregestational maternal diabetes?
Back: Caudal regression syndrome (sacral agenesis) and transposition of the great arteries.

Front: Pathognomonic transient cardiac pathology in infants of diabetic mothers?
Back: Asymmetric septal hypertrophy causing subaortic left ventricular outflow tract obstruction.

Front: Classic congenital anomalies associated with Thalidomide exposure?
Back: Phocomelia and amelia (severe limb reduction defects).

Front: Classic triad of Congenital Rubella Syndrome?
Back: Sensorineural cataracts, sensorineural deafness, and patent ductus arteriosus (PDA).

Front: Classic skin finding in neonatal Congenital Rubella Syndrome?
Back: "Blueberry muffin" spots representing extramedullary hematopoiesis.

Front: Intracranial finding pathognomonic for Congenital Cytomegalovirus (CMV)?
Back: Periventricular intracranial calcifications.

Front: Intracranial finding pathognomonic for Congenital Toxoplasmosis?
Back: Diffuse, scattered parenchymal intracranial calcifications.

Front: Classic triad of Congenital Toxoplasmosis?
Back: Chorioretinitis, hydrocephalus, and diffuse intracranial calcifications.

Front: Pathognomonic early skeletal sign of Congenital Syphilis?
Back: Wimberger sign (destructive osteopathy of the proximal medial tibia).

Front: Late-stage stigmata triad of Congenital Syphilis?
Back: Hutchinson triad: interstitial keratitis, sensorineural deafness, and notched Hutchinson teeth.

Front: Primary adverse structural outcome of prenatal Maternal Hyperthermia?
Back: Neural tube defects due to disruption of anterior neuropore closure.

Front: Neonatal withdrawal syndrome caused by maternal SSRI use in late pregnancy?
Back: Poor neonatal adaptation syndrome (hyperirritability, tachypnea, weak cry).

Front: Major neonatal vascular risk associated with maternal Cocaine use?
Back: Jejunoileal atresia or limb reduction defects due to fetal vascular disruption.

Front: Congenital defect linked to maternal smoking during pregnancy?
Back: Isolated cleft lip and palate.

Front: Drug of choice to prevent neural tube defects in standard pregnancies?
Back: Folic acid 0.4 mg daily starting pre-conception.

Front: Folic acid dosage recommended for mothers with a history of a prior child with a neural tube defect?
Back: 4.0 mg daily starting pre-conception.

Front: Definitive diagnostic test for fetal chromosomal anomalies after an abnormal maternal serum screen?
Back: Amniocentesis or Chorionic Villus Sampling (CVS) with karyotype/microarray.

Front: Most common congenital cardiac defect overall?
Back: Ventricular Septal Defect (VSD).

Front: Pathophysiology of an Omphalocele?
Back: Failure of the midgut loops to return to the abdominal cavity through the umbilical ring at week 10.

Front: Anatomical presentation of Gastroschisis?
Back: Herniation of bowel lateral to the umbilicus (usually right side) without a covering peritoneal membrane.

Front: Maternal serum marker elevated in both Gastroschisis and Omphalocele?
Back: Alpha-Fetoprotein (AFP).

Front: Syndrome strongly associated with Omphalocele, macroglossia, and hemihyperplasia?
Back: Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome.

Front: Malignancy risk associated with Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome?
Back: Wilms tumor and hepatoblastoma.

Front: Diagnostic imaging step for a newborn with excessive salivation and choking during initial feeding?
Back: Insertion of a radiopaque nasogastric tube followed by a chest/abdominal X-ray.

Front: Most common type of Tracheoesophageal Fistula (TEF)?
Back: Type C: Esophageal atresia with a distal tracheoesophageal fistula.

Front: Classic X-ray finding in Type C Tracheoesophageal Fistula?
Back: Coiled NG tube in the upper esophageal pouch with air present in the stomach.

Front: Diagnostic finding on an abdominal X-ray for Duodenal Atresia?
Back: The "double bubble" sign (air-filled stomach and proximal duodenum).

Front: Congenital anomaly presenting with a painless, midline neck mass that moves upward with tongue protrusion?
Back: Thyroglossal duct cyst.

Front: Congenital anomaly presenting with a lateral neck mass anterior to the sternocleidomastoid muscle that does not move with swallowing?
Back: Branchial cleft cyst.

Front: Embryological failure causing Hirschsprung disease?
Back: Failure of craniocaudal migration of neural crest cells to the distal colon.

Front: Definitive gold-standard diagnostic test for Hirschsprung disease?
Back: Suction rectal biopsy demonstrating an absence of ganglion cells in Meissner and Auerbach plexuses.

Front: Embryological failure causing Meckel diverticulum?
Back: Incomplete obliteration of the omphalomesenteric (vitelline) duct.

Front: Two tissue types most commonly found ectopically in Meckel diverticulum?
Back: Gastric mucosa and pancreatic tissue.

Front: Rule of 2s for Meckel diverticulum?
Back: 2% of population, 2 feet from ileocecal valve, 2 inches long, 2 types of ectopic tissue, presents by age 2.

Front: Most common congenital anomaly of the urinary tract?
Back: Ureteropelvic junction (UPJ) obstruction.

Front: Most common cause of severe bilateral hydronephrosis and bladder distension in newborn males?
Back: Posterior urethral valves (PUV).

Front: Diagnostic modality of choice to confirm Posterior Urethral Valves?
Back: Voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG).

Front: Embryological defect in an Exstrophy of the Bladder?
Back: Failure of the infraumbilical mesenchymal migration leading to a breakdown of the cloacal membrane.

Front: Embryological failure causing Anencephaly?
Back: Failure of the anterior (rostral) neuropore to close during week 4 of gestation.

Front: Embryological failure causing Spina Bifida Occluta?
Back: Failure of the embryonic vertebral arches to fuse completely midline without herniation of meninges or cord.

Front: Structural presentation of a Meningocele?
Back: Herniation of the meninges through a vertebral defect without spinal cord involvement.

Front: Structural presentation of a Myelomeningocele?
Back: Herniation of both the meninges and the spinal cord/neural elements through a vertebral defect.

Front: Central nervous system malformation universally associated with Myelomeningocele?
Back: Chiari II malformation (downward displacement of the cerebellar vermis and medulla).

Front: Classic cranial signs of hydrocephalus on physical examination of a newborn?
Back: Bulging anterior fontanelle, widened sutures, and the "setting-sun" eye sign.

Front: Definition of Craniosynostosis?
Back: Premature fusion of one or more cranial sutures.

Front: Most common type of single suture craniosynostosis?
Back: Sagittal suture synostosis (causing scaphocephaly/dolichocephaly).

Front: Diagnostic sign of Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip (DDH) that reduces a dislocated hip?
Back: Ortolani maneuver (abduction of the flexed hip with anterior pressure).

Front: First-line imaging modality to screen for structural anomalies in a fetus during routine prenatal care?
Back: Screening fetal anatomy ultrasound performed between 18 and 22 weeks of gestation.

Front: Serum markers included in the maternal quadruple screen for aneuploidy?
Back: Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), estriol (uE3), and inhibin A.

Front: Quadruple screen pattern classic for Trisomy 21?
Back: Decreased AFP, increased hCG, decreased uE3, and increased inhibin A.

Front: Quadruple screen pattern classic for Trisomy 18?
Back: Decreased AFP, decreased hCG, decreased uE3, and normal to decreased inhibin A.

Front: Next diagnostic step when a routine newborn pulse oximetry screen is positive for suspected congenital heart disease?
Back: Urgent transthoracic echocardiogram.

Front: Criteria for a positive newborn pulse oximetry screen for critical congenital heart disease?
Back: Oxygen saturation less than 90%, or less than 95% in both hand and foot on three measures, or a greater than 3% difference between hand and foot.

Front: Immediate medical management to maintain ductal patency in a newborn with a transposition of the great arteries?
Back: Intravenous Prostaglandin E1 (alprostadil) infusion.

Front: Absolute contraindication to the administration of Prostaglandin E1 in a cyanotic newborn?
Back: Total anomalous pulmonary venous return (TAPVR) with severe pulmonary venous obstruction.

Front: Primary life-threatening side effect of Prostaglandin E1 infusion requiring close monitoring?
Back: Apnea, often necessitating prophylactic endotracheal intubation.

Front: Definitive management strategy for a symptomatic congenital diaphragmatic hernia in the first 24 hours of life?
Back: Immediate endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation, avoiding bag-mask ventilation entirely.

Front: Why is bag-mask ventilation strictly contraindicated in congenital diaphragmatic hernia?
Back: It introduces air into the herniated gastrointestinal tract, causing further compression of the hypoplastic lungs.

Front: Preferred initial surgical timing for an uncomplicated isolated cleft lip repair?
Back: Around 10 to 12 weeks of life (adhering to the "rule of 10s": 10 weeks, 10 pounds, 10g hemoglobin).

Front: Preferred initial surgical timing for an uncomplicated isolated cleft palate repair?
Back: Between 9 and 18 months of age to optimize speech development while minimizing maxillary growth restriction.

Front: Immediate management step for an infant born with an exposed, ruptured omphalocele or gastroschisis?
Back: Cover the exposed bowel with sterile, warm, saline-soaked gauze and wrap it in a plastic bowel bag to prevent heat and fluid loss.

Front: First-line imaging study to evaluate a suspected developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) in an infant under 4 months of age?
Back: Hip ultrasonography, because the femoral head ossification center has not yet formed.

Front: First-line imaging study to evaluate suspected developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) in an infant over 4 to 6 months of age?
Back: Plain pelvic radiography (X-ray).

Front: Standard non-surgical treatment for a reducible developmental dysplasia of the hip diagnosed within the first 6 months of life?
Back: Dynamic splinting using a Pavlik harness.

Front: First-line diagnostic imaging test for a newborn who fails to pass meconium within the first 48 hours of life?
Back: Contrast enema to differentiate between Hirschsprung disease and meconium ileus.

Front: Classic contrast enema finding pathognomonic for Hirschsprung disease?
Back: A narrow distal rectosigmoid transition zone with a dilated proximal colon.

Front: Classic contrast enema finding pathognomonic for meconium ileus in cystic fibrosis?
Back: A microcolon containing multiple filling defects representing inspissated meconium plugs.

Front: Initial therapeutic intervention for a stable infant presenting with suspected malrotation and midgut volvulus?
Back: Emergent surgical laparotomy and a Ladd procedure.

Front: First-line imaging modality for suspected biliary atresia in a persistently jaundiced 3-week-old infant?
Back: Abdominal ultrasonography to assess for the presence of a gallbladder and the "triangular cord" sign.

Front: Definitive diagnostic test for confirming biliary atresia when ultrasound and hepatobiliary scintigraphy (HIDA) are inconclusive?
Back: Intraoperative cholangiogram.

Front: Surgical procedure that must be performed early to optimize survival in patients with biliary atresia?
Back: Kasai portoenterostomy, ideally performed before 60 days of age.

Front: Primary monitoring protocol required for an infant born with Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome due to tumor risks?
Back: Abdominal ultrasound every 3 months until age 8 and serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) every 3 months until age 4.

Front: Management step for an asymptomatic newborn found to have a single umbilical artery on umbilical cord inspection?
Back: Routine clinical monitoring, as isolated cases without other anomalies do not require further renal or cardiac imaging.

Front: Initial empirical management for an infant born with ambiguous genitalia while awaiting definitive genetic results?
Back: Strict avoidance of sex assignment, immediate checking of serum electrolytes to rule out congenital adrenal hyperplasia, and a karyotype.

Front: Life-threatening electrolyte imbalance to anticipate in a newborn with 21-hydroxylase deficiency congenital adrenal hyperplasia?
Back: Hyperkalemia and hyponatremia due to a severe salt-wasting crisis.

Front: First-line diagnostic imaging modality to confirm a suspected choanal atresia in a newborn with cyclical cyanosis relieved by crying?
Back: Computed tomography (CT) scan of the nasal cavities and skull base.

Front: Immediate stabilization method for a newborn with bilateral choanal atresia before definitive surgical repair?
Back: Placement of an oral airway or a McGovern nipple to bypass nasal airway obstruction.

Front: Screening recommendation for infants diagnosed with a cleft palate regarding hearing health?
Back: Early and frequent audiologic evaluation and tympanometry due to high risk of chronic otitis media with effusion.

Front: Standard long-term management requirement for a child post-repair of a myelomeningocele?
Back: Clean intermittent catheterization due to a neurogenic bladder.

Front: Primary orthopedic monitoring required throughout childhood for patients born with a repaired myelomeningocele?
Back: Serial spine radiographs to monitor for the development of neuromuscular scoliosis.

Front: Most common cause of late mortality in individuals with treated hydrocephalus and an ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt?
Back: Ventriculoperitoneal shunt malfunction or bacterial infection (most commonly Staphylococcus epidermidis).

Front: Classic clinical presentation of an acute ventriculoperitoneal shunt malfunction in an older child?
Back: Morning headaches, projectile vomiting, lethargy, and papilledema.

Front: Most sensitive non-invasive screening test for fetal aneuploidy available after 10 weeks of gestation?
Back: Cell-free fetal DNA (cfDNA) screening from maternal blood.

Front: Confirmatory diagnostic test of choice for a positive cell-free fetal DNA screen before 15 weeks of gestation?
Back: Chorionic villus sampling (CVS).

Front: Confirmatory diagnostic test of choice for a positive cell-free fetal DNA screen after 15 weeks of gestation?
Back: Amniocentesis.

Front: Primary structural anomaly associated with elevated maternal serum alpha-fetoprotein (MSAFP) and an intact abdominal wall?
Back: Neural tube defects such as anencephaly or open spina bifida.

Front: Classic ultrasound finding at 11 to 14 weeks of gestation that indicates an increased risk for Trisomy 21 or congenital heart disease?
Back: Increased nuchal translucency.

Front: Definitive genetic test used to identify submicroscopic deletions or duplications when a standard karyotype is normal?
Back: Chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA).

Front: Diagnostic triad pathognomonic for Tuberous Sclerosis Complex in an infant?
Back: Infantile spasms, hypopigmented macules (ash-leaf spots), and cardiac rhabdomyomas.

Front: Most common primary cardiac tumor in neonates, heavily associated with spontaneous regression?
Back: Cardiac rhabdomyoma.

Front: Standard surveillance protocol for individuals with Neurofibromatosis Type 1 (NF1) during early childhood?
Back: Annual ophthalmologic examinations to screen for optic pathway gliomas and Lisch nodules.

Front: Classic neurocutaneous syndrome presenting with a unilateral facial port-wine stain (nevus flammeus) along the ophthalmic trigeminal distribution?
Back: Sturge-Weber syndrome (encephalotrigeminal angiomatosis).

Front: Primary central nervous system vascular anomaly to monitor for in Sturge-Weber syndrome?
Back: Leptomeningeal angiomas, which can cause contralateral seizures, hemiparesis, and glaucoma.

Front: Core phenotypic features of Cornelia de Lange syndrome?
Back: Synophrys (unebrow), microcephaly, severe intellectual disability, and limb reduction defects.

Front: Classic features of Waardenburg syndrome?
Back: Lateral displacement of inner canthi, heterochromia iridis (different colored eyes), a white forelock of hair, and sensorineural deafness.

Front: Primary skeletal manifestation requiring surveillance in patients with Marfan syndrome during childhood?
Back: Progressive scoliosis and pectus excavatum or carinatum.

Front: Cardinal cardiovascular complication requiring serial echocardiography in Marfan syndrome?
Back: Aortic root dilation leading to aortic aneurysm or dissection.

Front: Standard initial surveillance protocol for an infant born with an isolated preauricular pit or tag?
Back: Routine newborn hearing screen, with no further imaging required unless accompanied by other dysmorphic features.

Front: Primary diagnostic consideration for a newborn with microcephaly, chorioretinitis, and periventricular calcifications?
Back: Congenital Cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection.

Front: Long-term neurosensory sequela that requires continuous monitoring in children with asymptomatic or symptomatic congenital CMV?
Back: Progressive, late-onset sensorineural hearing loss.

Front: Management paradigm for a newborn presenting with a large, asymptomatic congenital melanocytic nevus?
Back: Regular clinical monitoring and photographic documentation due to a small but lifelong risk of malignant melanoma conversion.

Front: Structural defect of the skull baseline that presents with a soft, compressible mass along the nasal bridge or occiput?
Back: Encephalocele (herniation of cranial contents through a bony defect).

Front: Next step in management for a newborn found to have an isolated sacral dimple within 0.5 cm of the anal verge and a visible base?
Back: Routine infant care, as this is a benign exam variant requiring no imaging.

Front: Diagnostic step required for a sacral dimple that is deep, greater than 0.5 cm in diameter, accompanied by a hair tuft, or located higher on the spine?
Back: Spinal ultrasonography to rule out spinal dysraphism or a tethered cord.

Front: Clinical feature that differentiates a simple hemangioma from a vascular malformation in infancy?
Back: Infantile hemangiomas proliferate rapidly after birth and eventually involute, whereas vascular malformations grow proportionally with the child and do not regress.

Front: First-line medical therapy for a rapidly growing infantile hemangioma causing functional impairment or visual obstruction?
Back: Oral propranolol (beta-blocker therapy).

Front: Recommended timeline for the initial comprehensive multidisciplinary team evaluation of a child born with an orofacial cleft?
Back: Within the first few days to weeks of life to address immediate nutritional and feeding mechanics.

Section: Pediatrics (Introductory)
Topic: Failure to thrive & nutritional disorders

Front: Definition of Failure to Thrive (FTT) regarding weight percentile?
Back: Weight consistently below the 3rd to 5th percentile for age and sex.

Front: Definition of FTT regarding weight percentile drops over time?
Back: Downward crossing of at least two major percentile lines on standard growth charts.

Front: Primary metric affected first in nutritional/organic FTT?
Back: Weight is affected first, followed later by height, and lastly head circumference.

Front: Meaning when head circumference is preferentially or equally reduced compared to weight?
Back: Suggests a congenital infection, genetic syndrome, or severe long-standing craniosynostosis.

Front: Definition of Non-Organic (Psychosocial) FTT?
Back: Growth failure due to environmental deprivation, poor bonding, or lack of food availability.

Front: Definition of Organic FTT?
Back: Growth failure secondary to an underlying medical condition or major organ system dysfunction.

Front: Most common general category of FTT etiology in clinical practice?
Back: Non-organic or psychosocial FTT.

Front: Core pathophysiologic mechanism behind almost all cases of non-organic FTT?
Back: Inadequate caloric intake.

Front: Initial diagnostic approach for a child presenting with FTT?
Back: Comprehensive history and physical exam, accompanied by a detailed 3-day dietary log.

Front: Role of extensive routine laboratory screening panels in asymptomatic FTT?
Back: Low utility, as history and physical exam guide targeted testing in over 90% of cases.

Front: Initial basic laboratory tests indicated if FTT etiology remains unclear after history?
Back: CBC, BMP, Urinalysis with culture, and Stool for fat/reducing substances.

Front: Indication for checking a sweat test in a child with FTT?
Back: Suspected Cystic Fibrosis presenting with respiratory symptoms or foul, fatty stools.

Front: Purpose of calculating the Mid-Parental Height in FTT evaluation?
Back: To differentiate pathological short stature/FTT from constitutional or genetic short stature.

Front: Ideal setting for initial management of severe, life-threatening FTT or suspected severe neglect?
Back: Inpatient hospital admission for multidisciplinary evaluation and supervised refeeding.

Front: Expected initial response in non-organic FTT when a child is hospitalized?
Back: Rapid weight gain when provided structured, adequate caloric intake.

Front: Primary dietary deficiency responsible for Marasmus?
Back: Severe deficiency of overall calories and all macronutrients.

Front: Primary dietary deficiency responsible for Kwashiorkor?
Back: Severe protein deficiency in the presence of adequate or near-adequate caloric intake.

Front: Classic clinical presentation of a child with Marasmus?
Back: Emaciated "skin and bones" appearance, prominent ribs, and loss of subcutaneous fat.

Front: Classic clinical presentation of a child with Kwashiorkor?
Back: Pitting edema, distended abdomen, hepatomegaly, and round "moon face."

Front: Cause of the distended abdomen and hepatomegaly in Kwashiorkor?
Back: Decreased apolipoprotein synthesis leading to fatty liver infiltration, plus fluid retention from hypoalbuminemia.

Front: Skin changes characteristic of severe Kwashiorkor?
Back: "Flaky paint" or "crazy paving" dermatosis with hyperpigmentation and desquamation.

Front: Hair changes characteristic of Kwashiorkor?
Back: "Flag sign" characterized by alternating bands of light and dark hair color.

Front: Serum albumin levels in Marasmus versus Kwashiorkor?
Back: Normal or slightly low in Marasmus; severely decreased in Kwashiorkor.

Front: Immune system consequence of severe protein-energy malnutrition?
Back: Atrophy of the thymus and T-cell dysfunction, leading to severe cellular immunodeficiency.

Front: Most common cause of death in children suffering from severe acute malnutrition?
Back: Intercurrent infections, secondary to profound immunosuppression.

Front: Primary intracellular electrolyte shifts seen during Refeeding Syndrome?
Back: Rapid movement of phosphorus, potassium, and magnesium into cells.

Front: Major hormone driver behind the metabolic shifts in Refeeding Syndrome?
Back: Insulin surge triggered by the reintroduction of carbohydrates.

Front: Most common and dangerous electrolyte abnormality in Refeeding Syndrome?
Back: Severe hypophosphatemia.

Front: Major clinical complications of severe hypophosphatemia during refeeding?
Back: Cardiac arrhythmias, muscle weakness, rhabdomyolysis, and respiratory failure.

Front: Cardiac complication of rapid fluid and sodium reintroduction in malnourished states?
Back: Congestive heart failure due to fluid overload on a compromised, atrophied myocardium.

Front: Strategy to prevent Refeeding Syndrome in severe malnutrition?
Back: Start caloric reintroduction slowly at 50% to 70% of requirement, then advance gradually.

Front: Micronutrient that must be supplemented immediately during refeeding to prevent Wernicke encephalopathy?
Back: Thiamine (Vitamin B1).

Front: Earliest clinical symptom of Vitamin A deficiency?
Back: Night blindness (nyctalopia).

Front: Conjunctival sign pathognomonic for Vitamin A deficiency?
Back: Bitot spots, which are dry, foamy plaques of keratinized epithelium.

Front: Severe corneal consequence of progressive, untreated Vitamin A deficiency?
Back: Keratomalacia, leading to corneal ulceration, perforation, and blindness.

Front: Systemic infection whose severity and mortality are significantly reduced by Vitamin A supplementation?
Back: Measles infection.

Front: Classic skeletal manifestation of Vitamin D deficiency in growing children?
Back: Rickets.

Front: Cranial bone finding in infants with early rickets?
Back: Craniotabes, characterized by thinning and softening of the cranial bones.

Front: Costochondral junction finding characteristic of Vitamin D deficiency?
Back: "Rachitic rosary," which is visible and palpable enlargement of the costochondral junctions.

Front: Long-bone deformities seen in weight-bearing children with Rickets?
Back: Genu varum (bowed legs) or genu valgum (knock-knees).

Front: Radiographic features of the long bones in active Rickets?
Back: Cupping, fraying, and widening of the metaphyses.

Front: Primary biochemical abnormalities in early nutritional Vitamin D deficiency rickets?
Back: Low or normal serum calcium, low serum phosphorus, and elevated Alkaline Phosphatase.

Front: Neurological presentation of severe Vitamin E deficiency?
Back: Hemolytic anemia in premature infants, ataxia, loss of proprioception, and deep tendon reflexes.

Front: Hematologic presentation of Vitamin K deficiency in newborns?
Back: Vitamin K Deficiency Bleeding (VKDB), presenting with umbilical, gastrointestinal, or intracranial hemorrhage.

Front: Reason why newborns are universally prone to Vitamin K deficiency?
Back: Poor placental transfer, sterile gut flora, and low Vitamin K content in breast milk.

Front: Coagulation profile alteration typical of Vitamin K deficiency?
Back: Prolonged Prothrombin Time (PT) and International Normalized Ratio (INR), with normal platelets.

Front: Standard preventive measure for Vitamin K Deficiency Bleeding administered at birth?
Back: Intramuscular injection of 1 mg of Vitamin K1 within hours of delivery.

Front: Clinical name for Vitamin B1 deficiency?
Back: Beriberi.

Front: Characteristics of "Dry" Beriberi?
Back: Symmetrical peripheral neuropathy with paresthesias, foot drop, and loss of reflexes.

Front: Characteristics of "Wet" Beriberi?
Back: High-output congestive heart failure, cardiomegaly, and peripheral edema.

Front: Classic clinical triad of Wernicke encephalopathy?
Back: Ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus, ataxia, and confusion.

Front: Clinical name for Vitamin B2 (Riboflavin) deficiency?
Back: Angular cheilitis, glossitis, stomatitis, and seborrheic dermatitis.

Front: Disease name associated with Vitamin B3 (Niacin) deficiency?
Back: Pellagra.

Front: The "Four Ds" of Pellagra presentation?
Back: Dermatitis, Diarrhea, Dementia, and Death.

Front: Classic dermatological neck finding in Pellagra?
Back: Casal necklace, a hyperpigmented, scaly rash around the lower neck.

Front: Amino acid that serves as a precursor for endogenous Niacin synthesis?
Back: Tryptophan.

Front: Medications known to interfere with Vitamin B6 (Pyridoxine) metabolism?
Back: Isoniazid (INH) and penicillamine.

Front: Neurological side effect of Isoniazid therapy prevented by Pyridoxine co-administration?
Back: Peripheral neuropathy.

Front: Hematologic manifestation of severe Vitamin B9 (Folate) or B12 deficiency?
Back: Megaloblastic anemia with hypersegmented neutrophils.

Front: Key clinical differentiator between Vitamin B12 deficiency and Folate deficiency?
Back: Vitamin B12 deficiency presents with subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord (neurological symptoms).

Front: Disease name associated with Vitamin C (Ascorbic Acid) deficiency?
Back: Scurvy.

Front: Vascular and gingival findings pathognomonic for Scurvy?
Back: Perifollicular hemorrhages, "corkscrew" hairs, and swollen, bleeding, friable gums.

Front: Bone lesion presentation of Scurvy in infants?
Back: Subperiosteal hemorrhage causing extreme bone pain and pseudoparalysis of the limbs.

Front: Cutaneous and systemic symptoms of severe Zinc deficiency?
Back: Acrodermatitis enteropathica, characterized by periorificial and acral dermatitis, alopecia, and chronic diarrhea.

Front: Primary hematologic manifestation of iron deficiency in children?
Back: Microcytic, hypochromic anemia with an elevated Red Cell Distribution Width (RDW).

Front: Dietary habit in toddlers most strongly associated with severe Iron Deficiency Anemia?
Back: Excessive consumption of cow's milk exceeding 24 ounces per day.

Front: Classic clinical manifestations of Copper deficiency?
Back: Microcytic anemia unresponsive to iron, neutropenia, and skeletal abnormalities resembling rickets.

Front: Genetic disorder characterized by impaired copper transport and brittle, "kinky" hair?
Back: Menkes disease.

Front: Neonatal/fetal consequence of severe maternal Iodine deficiency?
Back: Congenital hypothyroidism resulting in Cretinism, characterized by intellectual disability, short stature, and coarse facies.

Front: Ideal and recommended exclusive source of nutrition for the first 6 months of life?
Back: Exclusive human breast milk.

Front: Micronutrient that must be supplemented to all exclusively breastfed infants starting at birth?
Back: Vitamin D (400 IU daily).

Front: Age at which iron supplementation or iron-rich complementary foods should be introduced to term breastfed infants?
Back: 4 to 6 months of age, when prenatal iron stores begin to deplete.

Front: Major immunological factor present in high concentrations in breast milk colostrum?
Back: Secretory IgA (sIgA).

Front: Main carbohydrate component found in human breast milk and standard infant formulas?
Back: Lactose.

Front: Primary protein ratio difference between human breast milk and unmodified cow's milk?
Back: Breast milk is whey-predominant (easy to digest); cow's milk is casein-predominant.

Front: Absolute maternal contraindications to breastfeeding in developed countries?
Back: Maternal HIV infection, active untreated tuberculosis, illicit drug use, or infant classic galactosemia.

Front: Minimum age at which unmodified whole cow's milk can be safely introduced into a child's diet?
Back: 12 months of age.

Front: Reason why unmodified cow's milk is prohibited before 12 months of age?
Back: Risk of occult gastrointestinal blood loss, iron deficiency anemia, and excessive renal solute load.

Front: Typical caloric density of standard infant formula and mature breast milk?
Back: 20 kilocalories per fluid ounce (kcal/oz).

Front: Definition of "catch-up growth" in a recovering FTT child?
Back: A growth rate exceeding the normal statistical velocity for age, allowing the child to return to their original channel.

Front: Formula modification strategy used to meet the high caloric needs of recovering FTT infants?
Back: Concentrating formula recipes to 22, 24, or 27 kcal/oz under medical supervision.

Front: Definition of pediatric overweight based on BMI percentile?
Back: BMI between the 85th and 94th percentiles for age and sex.

Front: Definition of pediatric obesity based on BMI percentile?
Back: BMI equal to or greater than the 95th percentile for age and sex.

Front: Cutaneous marker of insulin resistance often seen in obese children?
Back: Acanthosis nigricans, characterized by hyperpigmented, velvety plaques in intertriginous areas.

Front: Orthopedic complication of adolescent obesity presenting with hip/knee pain and a limp?
Back: Slipped Capital Femoral Epiphysis (SCFE).

Front: Hepatic complication of pediatric obesity diagnosed via elevated ALT and ultrasound?
Back: Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD / formerly NAFLD).

Front: Neurological presentation of obesity presenting with headache, papilledema, and normal neuroimaging?
Back: Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension (Pseudotumor Cerebri).

Front: Most common gastrointestinal genetic cause of FTT presenting with malabsorption and steatorrhea?
Back: Celiac Disease.

Front: Initial serological screening test of choice for suspected Celiac Disease in children over 2 years?
Back: Tissue transglutaminase IgA (tTG-IgA) along with total serum IgA level.

Front: Why must total IgA be measured when screening for Celiac Disease?
Back: To rule out selective IgA deficiency, which can cause false-negative tTG-IgA results.

Front: Gastrointestinal condition presenting with projectile vomiting, metabolic alkalosis, and FTT?
Back: Hypertrophic Pyloric Stenosis.

Front: Metabolic derangement classic for Hypertrophic Pyloric Stenosis?
Back: Hypokalemic, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis.

Front: Cardiac lesion category that causes organic FTT due to hypermetabolism and tachypnea?
Back: Large left-to-right shunts, such as Ventricular Septal Defects (VSD).

Front: Endocrine disorder presenting with weight loss or FTT despite a voracious appetite?
Back: Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus or Hyperthyroidism.

Front: Renal pathology presenting with unexplained FTT, normal anion gap metabolic acidosis, and alkaline urine?
Back: Renal Tubular Acidosis (RTA).

Front: Screening test for a child with FTT, developmental delay, and unusual musty odor?
Back: Phenylketonuria (PKU) screening via serum phenylalanine levels.

Front: Classic radiological finding on a hand/wrist X-ray of a child with prolonged FTT?
Back: Delayed bone age compared to chronological age.

Front: Meaning of a child presenting with blue sclerae, osteopenia, and FTT?
Back: Suggests Osteogenesis Imperfecta or severe long-standing copper deficiency.

Front: Physical sign of severe marasmus visible on the face?
Back: "Monkey facies" or "aged look" due to the loss of buccal fat pads.

Front: Physical exam finding indicating severe dehydration in an infant with nutritional neglect?
Back: Sunken anterior fontanelle, dry mucous membranes, and delayed capillary refill.

Front: Meaning of "alligator skin" or severe xerosis in a malnourished toddler?
Back: Suggests chronic essential fatty acid deficiency or Vitamin A deficiency.

Front: Meaning of a child with FTT who exhibits "doll-like" facies, hepatomegaly, and fasting hypoglycemia?
Back: Suggests Von Gierke disease (Glycogen Storage Disease Type I).

Front: Clinical presentation of a child with FTT and a prominent "frontal bossing" skull configuration?
Back: Classic sign of advanced rachitic bone remodeling (Vitamin D deficiency).

Front: First line management step for a 9-month-old with mild non-organic FTT?
Back: Intensive nutritional counseling, establishing a regular feeding schedule, and close outpatient follow-up.

Front: Indication that a child with FTT is receiving adequate catch-up caloric intake?
Back: Weight gain velocity up to 2 to 3 times the normal rate for chronological age.

Front: Most common behavioral cause of inadequate caloric delivery in non-organic FTT?
Back: Improper dilution of infant formula by caregivers to save money.

Front: Presentation of "Water Intoxication" in infants fed overly diluted formula?
Back: Hyponatremia presenting with lethargy, hypotonia, and generalized seizures.

Front: Immediate management of an infant with hyponatremic seizures from diluted formula?
Back: Controlled administration of hypertonic (3%) saline to elevate serum sodium safely.

Front: Primary environmental intervention required for successful treatment of psychosocial FTT?
Back: Implementation of structured meal times without distractions like television or tablets.

Front: Major complication of untreated severe acute malnutrition on the gastrointestinal mucosa?
Back: Mucosal atrophy and loss of brush-border enzymes, causing transient secondary lactose intolerance.

Front: Dietary management of transient lactose intolerance during recovery from malnutrition?
Back: Short-term use of lactose-free formula or slow, graded reintroduction of standard feeds.

Front: Indication for introducing solid foods (complementary feeding) in standard infant development?
Back: Around 6 months of age, when developmental milestones like head control and tongue-thrust disappearance occur.

Front: Nutritional syndrome to suspect in an infant fed exclusively unpasteurized goat's milk?
Back: Pure Folate deficiency anemia, as goat's milk is notoriously low in folic acid.

Front: Growth chart pattern characteristic of GH deficiency or Hypothyroidism?
Back: Height percentile drops significantly before or simultaneously with the weight percentile drop.

Front: Growth chart pattern characteristic of nutritional or gastrointestinal FTT?
Back: Weight percentile drops significantly while height percentile is initially preserved.

Front: Serum parameter used as a sensitive, short-term indicator of nutritional status improvement?
Back: Prealbumin (transthyretin), due to its short half-life of roughly 2 days.

Front: Serum parameter used as a long-term indicator of protein status over weeks?
Back: Albumin, due to its longer half-life of approximately 20 days.

Front: Expected urinary finding in a child with prolonged starvation or Marasmus?
Back: Ketonuria, reflecting shifting metabolic pathways toward fatty acid oxidation.

Front: Mechanism of edema resolution during the early treatment phase of Kwashiorkor?
Back: Diuresis of excess interstitial fluid as intravascular oncotic pressure normalizes.

Front: Nutritional deficiency to suspect in a child with FTT, chronic diarrhea, and perianal fistulas?
Back: Crohn's disease (Inflammatory Bowel Disease).

Front: Immunization recommendation for children recovering from severe acute malnutrition?
Back: Administer all routine vaccines according to chronological age as soon as clinical stability is achieved.

Front: First sign of puberty in males that may be delayed by severe chronic malnutrition?
Back: Testicular enlargement to a volume of 4 mL or greater.

Front: First sign of puberty in females that may be delayed by severe chronic malnutrition?
Back: Thelarche (breast bud development).

Front: Expected finding on a peripheral blood smear in a child with severe Scurvy?
Back: Normocytic or macrocytic anemia, often complicated by co-existing iron deficiency.

Front: Primary reason for checking a thyroid panel (TSH, Free T4) in a child with FTT?
Back: To rule out occult hypothyroidism (causes poor linear growth) or hyperthyroidism (causes weight loss).

Front: Metabolic complication to monitor when treating a child with severe rickets with high-dose Vitamin D?
Back: Hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria, which can lead to nephrocalcinosis.

Front: Diagnostic finding on an EKG in a child with severe Hypokalemia from refeeding or malnutrition?
Back: Flattened T waves, ST depression, and prominent U waves.

Front: Cardinal sign of hypervitaminosis A due to toxic over-supplementation?
Back: Pseudotumor cerebri, signs of increased intracranial pressure, and dry, peeling skin.

Front: Next best step for an infant with FTT whose growth chart shows a sudden drop in weight but normal height, and history reveals a recent switch to home-prepared goat's milk?
Back: Switch to a standard iron-fortified infant formula and check a serum folate level.

Front: Next best step for a 4-month-old infant with FTT, persistent non-bilious emesis, and a palpable olive-shaped mass in the right upper quadrant?
Back: Obtain a right upper quadrant abdominal ultrasound to confirm hypertrophic pyloric stenosis.

Front: Next best step for a 6-week-old infant with FTT, prolonged jaundice, acholic stools, and dark urine?
Back: Obtain a serum fractionated bilirubin level and abdominal ultrasound to evaluate for biliary atresia.

Front: Next best step for a 2-year-old with FTT, chronic foul-smelling diarrhea, and a history of recurrent pneumonia?
Back: Order a sweat chloride test to evaluate for Cystic Fibrosis.

Front: Next best step for an asymptomatic 18-month-old with FTT whose screen shows positive tissue transglutaminase (tTG) IgA but normal total serum IgA?
Back: Refer to pediatric gastroenterology for an esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) with duodenal biopsies.

Front: Next best step for an exclusively breastfed 5-month-old whose weight has dropped from the 50th to the 5th percentile, with an otherwise entirely negative review of systems?
Back: Observe a live breastfeeding session to assess latch, milk transfer, and maternal technique.

Front: Next best step for a 3-year-old immigrant child with bowed legs, costochondral swelling, and low serum calcium?
Back: Measure serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D level and obtain bilateral lower extremity radiographs.

Front: Inborn error of metabolism presenting with FTT, cataracts, jaundice, and hepatomegaly soon after introducing milk?
Back: Classic Galactosemia (Galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase deficiency).

Front: Inborn error of metabolism presenting with FTT, severe vomiting, and hypoglycemia only after introducing fruits or juices?
Back: Hereditary Fructose Intolerance (Fructose-1-phosphate aldolase deficiency).

Front: Genetic syndrome presenting with FTT in infancy due to severe hypotonia and feeding difficulties, followed later by hyperphagia and obesity?
Back: Prader-Willi Syndrome.

Front: Chromosomal abnormality in a female presenting with unexplained short stature, mild FTT, and a wide-spaced nipple appearance?
Back: Turner Syndrome (45,X).

Front: Storage disease presenting with FTT, massive hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, and a cherry-red spot on the macula?
Back: Niemann-Pick Disease (Sphingomyelinase deficiency).

Front: Condition presenting with FTT, severe developmental delay, self-mutilation, and elevated uric acid?
Back: Lesch-Nyhan Syndrome (HGPRT deficiency).

Front: Differentiating growth chart pattern of Constitutional Delay of Growth vs. True Nutritional FTT?
Back: Constitutional delay shows a temporary drop followed by growth parallel to the lower curves with normal velocity; FTT shows progressive deviation away from the curves.

Front: Differentiating lab feature between Vitamin D Deficiency Rickets and Vitamin D-Dependent Rickets Type I?
Back: Nutritional deficiency has low 25(OH)D; Type I has normal 25(OH)D but low 1,25(OH)2D due to 1-alpha-hydroxylase deficiency.

Front: Differentiating presentation between Kwashiorkor and Nephrotic Syndrome in a toddler with generalized edema?
Back: Kwashiorkor presents with a normal urinalysis and distinct skin/hair changes; Nephrotic Syndrome presents with 4+ proteinuria and hyperlipidemia.

Front: Differentiating factor between Cow's Milk Protein Allergy (CMPA) and Lactose Intolerance in an infant?
Back: CMPA presents with blood or mucus in the stool and systemic allergic signs; lactose intolerance causes watery, acidic stools without blood.

Front: Differentiating baseline lab value between Type 1 and Type 2 Renal Tubular Acidosis (RTA) in FTT?
Back: Type 1 (Distal) features an inability to acidify urine (pH > 5.5); Type 2 (Proximal) features a normal ability to acidify urine during systemic acid load (pH < 5.5).

Front: Most critical risk factor in a maternal history predictive of non-organic Failure to Thrive?
Back: Maternal postpartum depression, severe social isolation, or substance abuse.

Front: Definition of "Munchausen Syndrome by Proxy" (Medical Child Abuse) in the context of FTT?
Back: A caregiver intentionally falsifies, exaggerates, or induces medical symptoms/starvation in a child to gain medical attention.

Front: Observation that strongly favors psychosocial FTT over an undiagnosed metabolic disorder during an inpatient admission?
Back: The child exhibits significant weight gain when fed exclusively by the nursing staff rather than the parent.

Front: Legal obligation of a physician when a child with FTT shows clear signs of intentional caloric deprivation or physical neglect?
Back: Immediate mandatory report to Child Protective Services (CPS) and hospitalization for child safety.

Front: Mineral deficiency presenting with impaired wound healing, altered taste (dysgeusia), and immune dysfunction, without skin lesions?
Back: Mild to moderate Zinc deficiency.

Front: Classic radiological skeletal sign seen in a child with chronic Lead Poisoning (Plumbism)?
Back: "Lead lines," which are hyperdense bands at the metaphyses of increased bone density.

Front: Chronic toxic manifestation of excessive Vitamin D consumption in a toddler?
Back: Hypercalcemia leading to polyuria, polydipsia, constipation, and nephrocalcinosis.

Front: Chronic toxic manifestation of excessive Vitamin A consumption in an adolescent treated for acne?
Back: Pseudotumor cerebri, bone pain, alopecia, and hepatosplenomegaly.

Front: Primary site of iron absorption in the human pediatric gastrointestinal tract?
Back: The duodenum and proximal jejunum.

Front: Primary site of Vitamin B12 and bile acid absorption in the gastrointestinal tract?
Back: The terminal ileum.

Front: Reason why infants with short bowel syndrome after necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) suffer from profound FTT?
Back: Massive reduction in mucosal surface area leading to generalized malabsorption of nutrients and fluids.

Front: Specific amino acid that becomes conditionally essential in premature infants recovering from nutritional stress?
Back: Arginine or Cysteine.

Front: Primary mechanism by which chronic inflammation (e.g., Crohn's disease) causes FTT besides malabsorption?
Back: Pro-inflammatory cytokines (like TNF-alpha) directly suppress appetite and alter the growth hormone-IGF-1 axis.

Front: Immediate management of severe, symptomatic hypocalcemia (tetany/seizures) found during nutritional workup?
Back: Intravenous administration of 10% Calcium Gluconate under continuous cardiac monitoring.

Front: Immediate fluid resuscitation choice for a severely malnourished, dehydrated child in hypovolemic shock?
Back: 10 to 20 mL/kg bolus of isotonic crystalloid (Normal Saline or Ringer's Lactate) infused slowly over a longer period than standard shock protocols to avoid heart failure.

Front: Serum glucose threshold defining neonatal hypoglycemia requiring immediate intervention?
Back: Less than 40 to 45 mg/dL.

Front: First line treatment for an infant with symptomatic hypoglycemia?
Back: Intravenous bolus of 2 to 5 mL/kg of 10% Dextrose (D10W).

Front: An 11-month-old infant is at the 2nd percentile for weight, 50th for height, and 50th for head circumference. What does this isolated lag in weight point toward?
Back: Acute nutritional deprivation or acute-onset non-organic failure to thrive.

Front: A 3-year-old child presents with a history of FTT, chronic diarrhea, and recurrent respiratory infections. A sweat test is borderline. What is the next diagnostic test?
Back: Genetic testing for CFTR gene mutations.

Front: A child recovering from Kwashiorkor develops carpopedal spasms 48 hours after starting aggressive nutritional therapy. What is the cause?
Back: Refeeding syndrome resulting in profound hypokalemia or hypomagnesemia leading to functional hypocalcemia.

Front: A 9-month-old infant presents with macrocytic anemia, irritability, and global developmental delay. The mother follows a strict vegan diet and exclusively breastfeeds. Deficiency of what nutrient explains this?
Back: Vitamin B12 (Cobalamin) deficiency.

Front: A 14-month-old child presents with skeletal deformities, a broad forehead, and soft skull bones. Lab results show normal calcium, low phosphorus, and very high alkaline phosphatase. What is the diagnosis?
Back: Active nutritional Vitamin D deficiency rickets.



Front: Standard caloric requirement (kcal/kg/day) for a healthy, growing infant under 6 months of age?

Back: Approximately 100 to 110 kcal/kg/day.

Front: Estimated caloric requirement (kcal/kg/day) required to achieve effective catch-up growth in an infant with severe FTT?

Back: Roughly 1.5 to 2 times the expected requirement for age, often calculated as $150 \text{ to } 200 \text{ kcal/kg/day}$.

Front: Simple formula used to calculate the exact caloric requirement for catch-up growth in a child with FTT?

Back:

$$\text{Caloric Requirement} = \frac{\text{Calories for weight age (kcal/kg/day)} \times \text{Ideal weight for length (kg)}}{\text{Actual weight (kg)}}$$

Front: Fluid requirement formula (Holliday-Segar method) for a child weighing between 10 and 20 kg?

Back: $1000 \text{ mL} + 50 \text{ mL/kg}$ for every kilogram over 10 kg.

Front: Expected average daily weight gain (grams/day) for a term infant during the first 3 months of life?

Back: Approximately 20 to 30 grams per day.

Front: Congenital disorder presenting with severe FTT, dynamic non-infectious watery diarrhea from birth, and an acidic stool pH?
Back: Congenital Glucose-Galactose Malabsorption (SGLT1 mutation).

Front: Autosomal recessive disorder presenting with severe FTT, steatorrhea, acanthocytosis on peripheral smear, and zero serum apolipoprotein B?
Back: Abetalipoproteinemia.

Front: Exocrine pancreatic insufficiency disorder presenting with FTT, chronic diarrhea, and cyclic neutropenia?
Back: Shwachman-Diamond Syndrome.

Front: Differentiating factor between Shwachman-Diamond Syndrome and Cystic Fibrosis in a child with pancreatic exocrine insufficiency?
Back: Shwachman-Diamond features neutropenia/skeletal anomalies and a normal sweat chloride test.

Front: Rare condition presenting with FTT, chronic watery diarrhea, and a striking failure to absorb dietary zinc despite normal intake?
Back: Acrodermatitis enteropathica (SLC39A4 mutation).

Front: Medication class used in pediatric epilepsy that drastically increases the hepatic clearance of Vitamin D, potentially causing rickets?
Back: Anticonvulsants (specifically Phenytoin, Phenobarbital, and Carbamazepine).

Front: Nutrient deficiency caused by chronic, therapeutic use of Sulfasalazine or Methotrexate in juvenile idiopathic arthritis?
Back: Folate (Vitamin B9) deficiency.

Front: Chronic use of which over-the-counter medication class in infants can lead to systemic metabolic alkalosis and masked FTT?
Back: Exogenous bicarbonate or antacid overuse for severe infant reflux.

Front: Diagnostic test of choice for a child with severe FTT, microcytic anemia, and dense metaphyseal bands on wrist X-rays?
Back: Serum blood lead level (BLL).

Front: Active forms of Vitamin D that must be administered to a child with FTT caused by end-stage chronic kidney disease?

Back: Calcitriol ($1,25\text{-(OH)}_2\text{D}_3$), bypassing the defective renal 1-alpha-hydroxylase enzyme.

Front: Form of Vitamin D synthesized in human skin via exposure to ultraviolet B (UVB) radiation?

Back: Cholecalciferol (Vitamin $D_3$).

Front: Form of Vitamin D typically found in plant sources and used in select commercial dietary fortifications?

Back: Ergocalciferol (Vitamin $D_2$).

Front: Biochemical transformation impaired in a child with severe Scurvy?

Back: Hydroxylation of proline and lysine residues during collagen synthesis.

Front: Enzyme cofactor role fulfilled by Thiamine ($B_1$) that is completely paralyzed during severe Beriberi?

Back: Cofactor for Pyruvate Dehydrogenase and Alpha-Ketoglutarate Dehydrogenase.

Front: Age by which a healthy term infant is expected to double their birth weight?
Back: 4 to 5 months of age.

Front: Age by which a healthy term infant is expected to triple their birth weight?
Back: 12 months (1 year) of age.

Front: Age by which a healthy term infant is expected to quadruple their birth weight?
Back: 24 months (2 years) of age.

Front: Expected average increase in head circumference during the first year of life?
Back: Approximately 12 centimeters total.

Front: Average linear height increase expected for a healthy child during their first year of life?
Back: Approximately 25 centimeters (or a 50% increase from birth length).
