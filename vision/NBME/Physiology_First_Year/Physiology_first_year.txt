Question 1: Renal Physiology (GFR Regulation)

Vignette:
A 62-year-old male construction worker presents with dizziness and reduced urine output for 2 days. He reports taking high-dose ibuprofen for chronic back pain. Past history includes hypertension and osteoarthritis.

Physical Exam:
BP: 92/58 mmHg, HR: 108/min, RR: 18/min, Temp: 36.8°C, SpO₂: 98%
Dry mucous membranes, decreased skin turgor.

Labs:
Creatinine: 2.0 mg/dL (baseline 1.0)
BUN: 40 mg/dL
Urine Na⁺: 10 mEq/L

Question:
What is the most likely mechanism of this patient’s acute kidney injury?

Choices:
A. Efferent arteriole constriction via angiotensin II
B. Afferent arteriole dilation via prostaglandins
C. Afferent arteriole constriction due to NSAIDs
D. Increased glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure
E. Decreased plasma oncotic pressure

Answer: C

Explanation:
NSAIDs inhibit prostaglandins → loss of afferent arteriole dilation → afferent constriction → ↓ GFR.

Incorrect:
A: Would maintain GFR (not decrease it)
B: Opposite effect
D: Would increase GFR
E: Seen in nephrotic syndrome

Question 1

Vignette: A 64-year-old male smoker with a 40-pack-year history presents to the clinic complaining of worsening shortness of breath over the last 6 months. He notes a chronic "smoker’s cough" but denies fever or weight loss.

Physical Examination:

Vitals: Temp: 37.0°C (98.6°F), HR: 88/min, BP: 132/84 mmHg, RR: 20/min, $SpO_2$: 91% on room air.

General: Increased anteroposterior chest diameter (barrel chest).

Lungs: Decreased breath sounds bilaterally; hyperresonance to percussion.

Extremities: No cyanosis or edema.

Laboratory Results (Arterial Blood Gas):

pH: 7.36

$PaCO_2$: 52 mmHg (Normal: 40)

$PaO_2$: 62 mmHg (Normal: >80)

$HCO_3^-$: 30 mEq/L (Normal: 24)

Which of the following changes in lung volumes or capacities is most likely present in this patient?

A. Increased FEV1/FVC ratio

B. Decreased Functional Residual Capacity (FRC)

C. Increased Lung Compliance

D. Decreased Total Lung Capacity (TLC)

E. Increased Diffusing Capacity (DLCO)

Correct Answer: C

Reasoning: This patient has emphysema (a form of COPD). The pathophysiology involves the destruction of alveolar walls and elastic fibers by elastases. This loss of elastic recoil results in an increase in lung compliance.

Incorrect Answers:

A: Obstruction (COPD) decreases the FEV1/FVC ratio (<0.7).

B: Destruction of elastic fibers causes the chest wall to expand outward, increasing the FRC.

D: TLC is typically increased in obstructive lung disease due to air trapping.

E: DLCO is decreased in emphysema due to the loss of alveolar surface area for gas exchange.

Question 2

Vignette: A 28-year-old female presents with a 3-week history of palpitations, heat intolerance, and increased frequency of bowel movements. She has no significant past medical history.

Physical Examination:

Vitals: Temp: 37.5°C (99.5°F), HR: 112/min, BP: 145/70 mmHg, RR: 16/min.

HEENT: Mild exophthalmos and lid lag.

Neck: Diffusely enlarged, non-tender thyroid gland.

Neuromuscular: 3+ deep tendon reflexes; fine tremor of outstretched hands.

Laboratory Results:

TSH: 0.1 $\mu$U/mL (Normal: 0.5–5.0)

Free T4: 3.2 ng/dL (Normal: 0.8–1.8)

Which of the following cellular mechanisms best explains this patient’s cardiovascular symptoms?

A. Downregulation of $\alpha_1$-adrenergic receptors

B. Upregulation of $\beta_1$-adrenergic receptors

C. Inhibition of the $Na^+/K^+$ ATPase pump

D. Decreased sensitivity of the SA node to catecholamines

E. Increased production of phospholamban

Correct Answer: B

Reasoning: Thyroid hormone increases the expression (upregulation) of $\beta_1$-adrenergic receptors in the heart. This leads to increased sensitivity to catecholamines, causing tachycardia, increased stroke volume, and high-output states.

Incorrect Answers:

A: $\alpha_1$ receptors are primarily involved in vasoconstriction; their downregulation wouldn't explain the tachycardia.

C: Thyroid hormone actually increases $Na^+/K^+$ ATPase activity to increase basal metabolic rate.

D: Sensitivity is increased, not decreased.

E: Thyroid hormone inhibits phospholamban (or increases its phosphorylation), which increases SERCA activity and enhances cardiac relaxation/contractility.

Question 5

Vignette: A 19-year-old male athlete collapses during a marathon. In the medical tent, he is found to be hypotensive.

Physical Examination:

Vitals: BP: 85/50 mmHg, HR: 130/min.

Which of the following reflects the immediate physiological compensatory response to the change in carotid sinus baroreceptor firing rate?

A. Increased parasympathetic outflow to the SA node

B. Decreased firing of the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX)

C. Increased firing of the Vagus nerve (CN X)

D. Decreased systemic vascular resistance (SVR)

E. Decreased renin release

Correct Answer: B

Reasoning: Hypotension leads to decreased stretch of the carotid sinus, which decreases the firing rate of the carotid sinus nerve (branch of CN IX). This reduced inhibition on the vasomotor center in the medulla leads to increased sympathetic and decreased parasympathetic outflow.

Incorrect Answers:

A/C: Parasympathetic outflow (Vagus) decreases to allow HR to rise.

D: SVR increases via $\alpha_1$ constriction to maintain BP.

E: Renin release increases due to sympathetic stimulation of $\beta_1$ receptors on JGA cells.

Question 6

Vignette: A 30-year-old woman is evaluated for profound muscle weakness that worsens throughout the day and improves with rest. She specifically notes difficulty chewing and double vision when reading in the evening.

Physical Examination:

Vitals: Normal.

Neurological: Bilateral ptosis after sustained upward gaze; normal deep tendon reflexes; no sensory deficits.

What is the primary pathophysiological mechanism of this patient’s muscle weakness?

A. Inhibition of Acetylcholinesterase in the synaptic cleft

B. Antibodies against presynaptic voltage-gated Calcium channels

C. Antibodies against postsynaptic Nicotinic Acetylcholine receptors

D. Failure of Calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum

E. Blockade of Glycine receptors in the spinal cord

Correct Answer: C

Reasoning: This is Myasthenia Gravis. Autoantibodies against the nicotinic ACh receptor at the neuromuscular junction lead to a reduced number of functional receptors, causing a decreased end-plate potential (EPP) and muscle weakness with repetitive use.

B: This is Lambert-Eaton Myasthenic Syndrome.

D: This describes Ryanodine receptor dysfunction (e.g., Malignant Hyperthermia).

E: This is the mechanism of Strychnine poisoning.

Question 10

Vignette: A 58-year-old male with a history of chronic heart failure presents with increased swelling in his legs. He is currently taking Lisinopril and Furosemide.

Physical Examination:

Vitals: BP: 118/76 mmHg.

Extremities: 2+ pitting edema to the mid-calf.

Which of the following Starling forces is primarily responsible for the formation of edema in this patient?

A. Increased Capillary Hydrostatic Pressure ($P_c$)

B. Decreased Interstitial Hydrostatic Pressure ($P_i$)

C. Increased Capillary Oncotic Pressure ($\pi_c$)

D. Decreased Interstitial Oncotic Pressure ($\pi_i$)

E. Increased Capillary Permeability ($K_f$)

Correct Answer: A

Reasoning: Heart failure leads to a "back-up" of blood into the venous system. This increases venous pressure, which is transmitted back to the capillaries as increased Capillary Hydrostatic Pressure ($P_c$), favoring the filtration of fluid into the interstitium.

Incorrect Answers:

B: Interstitial pressure usually increases as fluid enters the space.

C: Increased $\pi_c$ (as seen in dehydration) would pull fluid into the capillary, preventing edema.

D: Decreased $\pi_i$ would also protect against edema.

E: Increased permeability is typical of inflammation or sepsis, not simple heart failure.

Question 11

Vignette: A 42-year-old female presents with a 4-month history of progressive muscle weakness and a 15-lb weight loss. She also notes that her skin has become significantly "tan" despite staying indoors during the winter. She has a history of Hashimoto thyroiditis.

Physical Examination:

Vitals: BP: 92/58 mmHg (sitting), 78/44 mmHg (standing); HR: 105/min; Temp: 37.0°C.

Skin: Hyperpigmentation noted in the palmar creases and buccal mucosa.

Laboratory Results:

Serum Sodium: 129 mEq/L (Normal: 135–145)

Serum Potassium: 5.8 mEq/L (Normal: 3.5–5.0)

Serum Glucose: 65 mg/dL (Normal: 70–100)

Which of the following describes the most likely primary physiological defect?

A. Increased pituitary sensitivity to cortisol

B. Destruction of the zona glomerulosa and zona fasciculata

C. Ectopic production of ACTH by a pulmonary nodule

D. Excessive production of 11-deoxycorticosterone

E. Deficiency of 21-hydroxylase enzyme

Correct Answer: B

Reasoning: This is Primary Adrenal Insufficiency (Addison’s Disease), likely autoimmune given her history of Hashimoto's. Destruction of the adrenal cortex affects all three layers. Loss of the zona glomerulosa causes hypoaldosteronism (hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypotension), and loss of the zona fasciculata causes hypocortisolism (hypoglycemia, high ACTH leading to hyperpigmentation).

Incorrect Answers:

A: In primary insufficiency, pituitary sensitivity is irrelevant; the gland is trying to compensate with high ACTH.

C: This would cause Cushing syndrome (hypertension, hypokalemia), not Addison's.

D: This is a mineralocorticoid; excess would cause hypertension and hypokalemia.

E: This is Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia, usually presenting in infancy/childhood with virilization.

Question 12

Vignette: A 55-year-old male with a history of hypertension and chronic kidney disease (Stage 3) is brought to the physician for a follow-up. He is concerned because he feels "slower" and has frequent muscle cramps.

Laboratory Results:

Serum Calcium: 7.2 mg/dL (Normal: 8.5–10.5)

Serum Phosphate: 6.2 mg/dL (Normal: 3.0–4.5)

PTH: 250 pg/mL (Normal: 10–65)

Which of the following is the most likely cause of this patient’s hypocalcemia?

A. Defective PTH receptors in the bone

B. Decreased activity of 1$\alpha$-hydroxylase

C. Excessive renal excretion of calcium

D. Primary hyperparathyroidism

E. Inhibition of osteoclast activity

Correct Answer: B

Reasoning: In chronic kidney disease, the loss of renal parenchyma leads to decreased 1$\alpha$-hydroxylase activity. This results in decreased production of 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol), which leads to decreased intestinal calcium absorption and hypocalcemia. The high phosphate also complexes with calcium and further lowers levels.

Incorrect Answers:

A: This describes Pseudohypoparathyroidism, which is genetic, not acquired via CKD.

C: CKD patients actually have trouble excreting phosphate, not necessarily "excessive" calcium loss.

D: Primary hyperparathyroidism causes hypercalcemia.

E: High PTH in CKD (Secondary hyperparathyroidism) actually increases osteoclast activity to pull calcium from bone.

Question 13

Vignette: A 22-year-old male is participating in a study evaluating gastrointestinal motility. He consumes a meal high in fats and proteins.

Which of the following physiological changes will most likely occur in the gallbladder and sphincter of Oddi 30 minutes after the meal?

A. Gallbladder relaxation and sphincter of Oddi contraction

B. Gallbladder contraction and sphincter of Oddi relaxation

C. Gallbladder contraction and sphincter of Oddi contraction

D. Gallbladder relaxation and sphincter of Oddi relaxation

E. No change in either structure

Correct Answer: B

Reasoning: Fats and amino acids in the duodenum trigger I-cells to release Cholecystokinin (CCK). CCK has two primary motor effects on the biliary system: it causes the gallbladder to contract and simultaneously relaxes the sphincter of Oddi to allow bile to enter the duodenum.

Incorrect Answers:

A: This would prevent bile from reaching the duodenum.

C/D/E: These do not match the coordinated "pump and dump" mechanism of the biliary system.

Laboratory Results:

Aldosterone: Very High

Renin: Very Low

Which of the following sets of findings is most likely in this patient?

A. Hyponatremia, Hyperkalemia, Metabolic Acidosis.

B. Hypernatremia, Hypokalemia, Metabolic Alkalosis.

C. Hypernatremia, Hyperkalemia, Metabolic Acidosis.

D. Normonatremia, Hypokalemia, Metabolic Alkalosis.

E. Hyponatremia, Hypokalemia, Metabolic Acidosis.

Correct Answer: D

Detailed Explanation: This is Conn Syndrome (Primary Hyperaldosteronism). Aldosterone increases $Na^+$ reabsorption and $K^+/H^+$ secretion. This leads to hypokalemia and alkalosis. However, due to "Aldosterone Escape" (ANP release and pressure natriuresis), the patient does not become significantly hypernatremic; they are usually normonatremic but hypervolemic/hypertensive.

A: Describes Addison's.

B: Plausible, but "Normonatremia" is a more classic board answer for Conn due to the escape mechanism.

C: Aldosterone never causes hyperkalemia.

E: Incorrect electrolyte pattern.

Question 30

Vignette: A researcher is measuring oxygen partial pressures ($PO_2$) in various cardiac chambers.

The Question: In a healthy subject, which of the following locations would have the lowest $PO_2$?

A. Right Atrium.

B. Pulmonary Artery.

C. Coronary Sinus.

D. Internal Jugular Vein.

E. Left Ventricle.

Correct Answer: C

Detailed Explanation: The myocardium (heart muscle) has the highest oxygen extraction ratio of any tissue in the body (~75% at rest). Because the heart is so efficient at pulling oxygen out of the blood, the venous blood returning from the heart via the coronary sinus has the lowest $PO_2$ in the entire circulatory system.

A/B: These contain "mixed venous blood," which includes blood from organs with much lower extraction ratios (like the skin or kidneys).

D: The brain extracts less $O_2$ relative to flow than the heart.

E: This contains highly oxygenated blood ($PO_2$ ~100 mmHg).



Question 1

A 52-year-old male marathon runner presents to cardiology clinic with a 3-month history of exertional dyspnea and pre-syncopal episodes. A harsh mid-systolic murmur was noted incidentally 5 years ago but never evaluated. His father died of a "heart problem" in his 50s. He takes no medications.

Vital Signs: BP 110/85 mmHg | HR 72 bpm | RR 16/min | Temp 36.8°C | SpO₂ 97%

Physical Exam: Harsh 3/6 crescendo-decrescendo systolic murmur at the right upper sternal border radiating to bilateral carotids. S2 is single and paradoxically split. PMI is sustained and laterally displaced. Carotid upstroke is slow and sustained (pulsus parvus et tardus). Peripheral pulses are diminished.

Labs:



BNP: 190 pg/mL (Normal < 100) ↑

Hemoglobin: 14.8 g/dL (Normal 13.5–17.5)

Troponin I: 0.02 ng/mL (Normal < 0.04)

ECG: LVH pattern with strain ↑

Which of the following best explains the pathophysiological mechanism responsible for this patient's symptoms?



A. Decreased myocardial contractility due to beta-1 receptor downregulation

B. Fixed obstruction to LV outflow causing pressure-dependent reduction in coronary perfusion and cardiac output

C. Mitral regurgitation causing volume overload and eccentric hypertrophy

D. Aortic regurgitation causing diastolic backflow and LV dilation

E. Decreased preload due to venous pooling from autonomic neuropathy

Correct Answer: B

Reasoning: This is classic severe calcific aortic stenosis (AS). The hallmark findings — pulsus parvus et tardus, crescendo-decrescendo systolic murmur radiating to the carotids, single S2, and paradoxically split S2 — all confirm fixed LV outflow obstruction. The LV must generate markedly elevated pressures to eject across the stenotic valve, driving concentric hypertrophy. This hypertrophy increases myocardial oxygen demand while simultaneously compressing subendocardial coronary vessels (reducing supply). During exercise, demand escalates but cardiac output cannot rise proportionally due to the fixed orifice area. The result is the classic triad of AS: exertional syncope (inadequate cerebral perfusion), angina (subendocardial ischemia), and dyspnea (elevated LVEDP).

Why the other choices are wrong:

A — Beta-1 receptor downregulation is the mechanism in dilated cardiomyopathy from chronic adrenergic overstimulation. In aortic stenosis, contractility is initially preserved and often supranormal due to compensatory concentric hypertrophy. This would be correct in a patient with end-stage heart failure with reduced ejection fraction.

C — Mitral regurgitation produces a holosystolic murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla, with eccentric (volume-overload) hypertrophy and a hyperdynamic PMI. It would not produce pulsus parvus et tardus or a right upper sternal border murmur.

D — Aortic regurgitation causes a widened pulse pressure (Corrigan's/water-hammer pulse), a diastolic decrescendo murmur at the left sternal border, and LV dilation from chronic volume overload — the exact opposite of the narrow pulse pressure and delayed carotid upstroke seen here.

E — Autonomic neuropathy with venous pooling causes orthostatic hypotension, not exertional syncope tied to fixed obstruction. There is no history of diabetes, peripheral neuropathy, or positional symptoms in this vignette.

Question 2

A 28-year-old woman at 34 weeks gestation is admitted for a 12-hour history of severe headache, visual disturbances, and right upper quadrant pain. She has had no prior prenatal complications and takes only prenatal vitamins.

Vital Signs: BP 162/108 mmHg | HR 98 bpm | RR 18/min | Temp 37.1°C | SpO₂ 98%

Physical Exam: Fundoscopy shows arteriolar narrowing with AV nicking. Lungs with bibasilar crackles. RUQ tenderness on palpation. 2+ pitting edema bilateral lower extremities. Hyperreflexia on DTR testing. No focal neurological deficits.

Labs:



Urinalysis (protein): 3+ proteinuria (Normal: none) ↑

Platelets: 89,000/mm³ (Normal 150k–400k) ↓

AST: 178 U/L (Normal 10–40) ↑

ALT: 162 U/L (Normal 7–56) ↑

Creatinine: 1.4 mg/dL (Normal 0.5–1.1) ↑

LDH: 712 U/L (Normal 140–280) ↑

Peripheral smear: Schistocytes present ↑

What is the primary pathophysiological mechanism underlying this patient's hypertension and end-organ damage?



A. Increased cardiac output from hypervolemia of pregnancy causing pressure-mediated glomerular damage

B. Defective placental trophoblast invasion causing placental ischemia, release of anti-angiogenic factors, and systemic endothelial dysfunction

C. Autoimmune IgG deposition in renal glomeruli causing membranous nephropathy

D. Excessive aldosterone secretion from a placental aldosterone-producing adenoma

E. Sympathetic overdrive from anxiety-mediated catecholamine release causing labile hypertension

Correct Answer: B

Reasoning: This is HELLP syndrome (Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets) — the severe end of the preeclampsia spectrum. The core mechanism begins with defective remodeling of uterine spiral arteries by extravillous trophoblasts. Normally, trophoblasts invade and dilate these vessels, creating a low-resistance uteroplacental circuit. In preeclampsia, this invasion fails, leaving high-resistance arteries and placental ischemia. The ischemic placenta releases anti-angiogenic factors — primarily sFlt-1 (which sequesters VEGF and PlGF) and endoglin (which inhibits TGF-β signaling). This produces widespread maternal endothelial dysfunction: vasoconstriction (hypertension), increased permeability (edema, proteinuria), platelet activation (thrombocytopenia), hepatic endothelial injury (elevated AST/ALT, RUQ pain), and microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (schistocytes, elevated LDH).

Why the other choices are wrong:

A — Normal pregnancy does increase cardiac output and plasma volume, but is associated with decreased systemic vascular resistance and decreased blood pressure. Simple hypervolemia does not explain the triad of thrombocytopenia, hepatic injury, and hemolysis. This is not the mechanism of preeclampsia.

C — Membranous nephropathy causes nephrotic-range proteinuria and slowly progressive renal insufficiency, but does not cause acute thrombocytopenia, transaminase elevation, schistocytes, or third-trimester onset in an otherwise healthy pregnancy. It would be correct in a patient with chronic, insidious proteinuria and no hematologic derangements.

D — Aldosterone-producing adenomas (Conn syndrome) cause hypertension with hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis — not proteinuria, thrombocytopenia, or hepatic enzyme elevation. Placental aldosterone-producing adenomas are not a recognized clinical entity.

E — Anxiety-driven catecholamine release produces episodic labile hypertension without the persistent proteinuria, thrombocytopenia, schistocytes, or hepatic enzyme elevation seen here. Pheochromocytoma would be the structured differential for catecholamine excess, and it would not produce these obstetric-specific findings.

Question 3

A 19-year-old male college student is found unconscious in his dormitory. His roommate reports 3 weeks of polydipsia and polyuria, with the patient "drinking constantly." He has no known medical history and takes no medications. He is minimally responsive to sternal rub.

Vital Signs: BP 92/58 mmHg | HR 128 bpm | RR 28/min (deep, sighing) | Temp 37.4°C | SpO₂ 99%

Physical Exam: Kussmaul respirations. Fruity odor on breath. Poor skin turgor. Dry mucous membranes. Diffuse abdominal tenderness without guarding. GCS 9 (E2V3M4).

Labs:



Glucose: 618 mg/dL (Normal 70–100) ↑

Arterial pH: 7.08 (Normal 7.35–7.45) ↓

HCO₃⁻: 8 mEq/L (Normal 22–26) ↓

PCO₂: 20 mmHg (Normal 35–45) ↓ (respiratory compensation)

Anion Gap: 28 mEq/L (Normal 8–12) ↑

Serum Na⁺: 128 mEq/L (Normal 135–145) ↓

Serum K⁺: 5.8 mEq/L (Normal 3.5–5.0) ↑

Urine ketones: Large ↑

Which of the following best explains why this patient's serum potassium is elevated despite a total body potassium deficit?



A. Increased dietary potassium intake causing acute hyperkalemia

B. Renal failure impairing tubular potassium secretion

C. Acidosis driving transcellular potassium shift from the intracellular to the extracellular compartment

D. Aldosterone deficiency impairing renal potassium excretion

E. Insulin-mediated suppression of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity

Correct Answer: C

Reasoning: This is DKA in new-onset type 1 diabetes. Despite a serum K⁺ of 5.8 mEq/L, the patient has profound total body potassium depletion from weeks of osmotic diuresis. The apparent hyperkalemia results from the transcellular shift driven by metabolic acidosis. In acidosis, excess extracellular H⁺ ions enter cells via H⁺/K⁺ exchange on cell membranes — for each H⁺ that enters, one K⁺ exits to maintain electrochemical neutrality. This redistributes K⁺ from the intracellular compartment (where ~98% of body K⁺ normally resides) into the plasma. Additionally, insulin deficiency eliminates normal insulin-stimulated Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity, which would otherwise drive K⁺ into cells. As treatment with insulin and fluids begins, serum K⁺ will fall precipitously — making early potassium replacement critical once urine output is confirmed.

Why the other choices are wrong:

A — A patient with altered consciousness, hemodynamic instability, and Kussmaul respirations has not been eating normally. Dietary potassium excess is not a physiologically plausible mechanism here. The elevated K⁺ is entirely a transcellular shift phenomenon.

B — While DKA causes some prerenal azotemia from volume depletion, frank renal failure is not the primary mechanism. The kidneys are still functional, as evidenced by urinary ketone excretion. A uremic cause of hyperkalemia would not explain the anion gap metabolic ketoacidosis.

D — Aldosterone deficiency (as in primary adrenal insufficiency) would cause hyperkalemia along with hyponatremia and metabolic acidosis — but the clinical context here is unambiguous insulin-deficient DKA, with extreme hyperglycemia and ketonuria pointing directly away from an adrenal etiology.

E — This is the reverse of what occurs. Insulin deficiency means Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase is not stimulated, so K⁺ does not enter cells — contributing to extracellular accumulation. When insulin is given as treatment, pump activity is restored, driving K⁺ back intracellularly and causing the serum K⁺ to drop. The statement as written has the mechanism backwards.

Question 4

A 67-year-old woman with a 40-pack-year smoking history and known COPD presents with worsening dyspnea, bilateral leg edema, and progressive blue-purple discoloration of her lips over 2 months. She uses home O₂ at 2 L/min and takes tiotropium, fluticasone/salmeterol, and furosemide.

Vital Signs: BP 148/86 mmHg | HR 96 bpm | RR 24/min | Temp 36.9°C | SpO₂ 83% on room air

Physical Exam: Barrel-shaped chest. Distant breath sounds bilaterally with prolonged expiratory phase and diffuse expiratory wheezes. Loud P2 component of S2. Right parasternal heave. JVP elevated 10 cm above sternal angle. 3+ pitting edema bilateral lower extremities. Central cyanosis of lips and tongue.

Labs:



Hemoglobin: 19.4 g/dL (Normal 12–16) ↑

Hematocrit: 58% (Normal 37–47) ↑

PaO₂: 44 mmHg (Normal 80–100) ↓

PaCO₂: 64 mmHg (Normal 35–45) ↑

pH: 7.33 (Normal 7.35–7.45) ↓

HCO₃⁻: 33 mEq/L (Normal 22–26) ↑ (chronic compensation)

Which of the following mechanisms best explains the erythrocytosis observed in this patient?



A. Polycythemia vera causing autonomous EPO-independent erythroid proliferation

B. Decreased plasma volume from chronic diuretic use concentrating red blood cells

C. Chronic hypoxia stimulating HIF-1α-mediated erythropoietin release from peritubular fibroblasts

D. Iron supplementation causing increased hemoglobin synthesis in the bone marrow

E. Testosterone elevation from hypoxia-induced Leydig cell stimulation increasing erythropoiesis

Correct Answer: C

Reasoning: This is the "blue bloater" phenotype of COPD (chronic bronchitis type) with secondary erythrocytosis. Chronic hypoxia (PaO₂ = 44 mmHg) is sensed at the cellular level by prolyl hydroxylase domain (PHD) enzymes. Under normoxia, PHD enzymes hydroxylate HIF-1α (hypoxia-inducible factor-1α), targeting it for proteasomal degradation via VHL-mediated ubiquitination. Under chronic hypoxia, PHD activity falls, HIF-1α accumulates, dimerizes with HIF-1β, and translocates to the nucleus where it binds hypoxia response elements (HREs) on the EPO gene in peritubular interstitial fibroblasts of the renal cortex, massively increasing erythropoietin secretion. EPO acts on BFU-E and CFU-E erythroid precursors in bone marrow, promoting survival, proliferation, and terminal differentiation into mature RBCs — raising hemoglobin and hematocrit as a compensatory adaptation to increase oxygen-carrying capacity.

Why the other choices are wrong:

A — Polycythemia vera (PV) is an autonomous myeloproliferative neoplasm caused by the JAK2 V617F mutation. EPO levels would be suppressed (low) in PV because erythropoiesis is EPO-independent. This patient's secondary erythrocytosis is EPO-driven by hypoxia. PV would also not explain the severe hypoxia, obstructive lung disease, or cor pulmonale findings.

B — Diuretic-induced plasma volume contraction produces relative (spurious) polycythemia — but hemoglobin of 19.4 g/dL with hematocrit of 58% represents true erythrocytosis with genuine expansion of the red cell mass. Concentration effects alone would not produce values of this magnitude.

D — This patient is on no iron supplements, and iron supplementation cannot drive erythropoiesis above EPO-governed levels in someone with normal iron stores. It corrects iron-deficiency anemia; it does not cause erythrocytosis in an iron-replete individual.

E — This patient is a 67-year-old woman without functioning Leydig cells. While testosterone does have erythropoietic effects (explaining normal sex differences in hemoglobin ranges), this mechanism is entirely inapplicable here. Secondary erythrocytosis from testosterone excess is seen in male patients receiving exogenous androgen therapy.

Question 5

A 55-year-old man with hypertension and type 2 diabetes presents with a 6-hour history of severe crushing chest pain radiating to the left arm and jaw, diaphoresis, and nausea. He takes metformin and lisinopril. He is a 30-pack-year smoker.

Vital Signs: BP 88/56 mmHg | HR 118 bpm | RR 22/min | Temp 37.0°C | SpO₂ 94%

Physical Exam: S3 gallop present. Bibasilar crackles to the mid-fields. JVP elevated 9 cm. Extremities cool and clammy. Urine output via Foley catheter: 15 mL/hr.

Labs:



Troponin I: 18.4 ng/mL (Normal < 0.04) ↑

BNP: 1,240 pg/mL (Normal < 100) ↑

Creatinine: 2.2 mg/dL (Normal 0.7–1.3) ↑

Lactate: 4.8 mmol/L (Normal < 2.0) ↑

ECG: ST elevation in leads II, III, aVF ↑

What is the primary physiological mechanism causing oliguria in this patient?



A. Direct tubulotoxic injury from contrast nephropathy following recent angiography

B. Reduced renal perfusion pressure activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and ADH, causing avid sodium and water retention

C. Diabetic nephropathy causing glomerulosclerosis and reduced baseline GFR

D. Lisinopril-induced efferent arteriolar dilation reducing glomerular filtration pressure

E. Acute tubular necrosis from myoglobinuria secondary to rhabdomyolysis

Correct Answer: B

Reasoning: This patient is in cardiogenic shock complicating an inferior STEMI (RCA territory — ST elevation in II, III, aVF). Severely reduced cardiac output is sensed as effective arterial underfilling, triggering two parallel compensatory mechanisms. First, decreased renal perfusion pressure reduces afferent arteriolar stretch, and reduced NaCl delivery to the macula densa stimulates renin release from juxtaglomerular cells. Renin cleaves angiotensinogen → angiotensin I → (via ACE) → angiotensin II, which causes efferent arteriolar constriction (transiently preserving GFR), aldosterone release (promoting Na⁺ and H₂O retention in the collecting duct), and direct ADH secretion from the posterior pituitary (aquaporin-2 insertion, free water retention). Second, unloaded baroreceptors independently stimulate ADH release. Together, these mechanisms produce oliguria (< 0.5 mL/kg/hr) as the body attempts to restore circulating volume despite ongoing pump failure — classic prerenal azotemia in the setting of cardiogenic shock.

Why the other choices are wrong:

A — There is no history of recent contrast administration in this vignette. Contrast nephropathy typically presents 24–48 hours after iodinated contrast exposure with a rise in creatinine. This is acute cardiogenic shock-mediated prerenal oliguria, not contrast injury.

C — Diabetic nephropathy is a chronic, slowly progressive process unfolding over years. It produces baseline GFR reduction but cannot explain the acute, severe oliguria developing over hours in the context of hemodynamic collapse. The elevated creatinine likely reflects acute prerenal azotemia superimposed on mild background CKD.

D — Lisinopril (ACE inhibitor) dilates the efferent arteriole, reducing intraglomerular pressure. This can precipitate acute kidney injury in the setting of severely reduced renal perfusion (e.g., bilateral renal artery stenosis) — but this is a chronic, recognized side effect. In this acute shock state, forward flow failure and RAAS/ADH activation are the dominant mechanisms driving oliguria, not the ACE inhibitor in isolation.

E — Rhabdomyolysis causes myoglobinuria with cola-colored urine and markedly elevated creatine kinase. It requires a clinical trigger such as crush injury, severe statin toxicity, or prolonged seizure activity. A chest pain STEMI presentation does not provide a mechanism for significant muscle breakdown, and no CK or myoglobin elevation is reported here.

Question 6

A 34-year-old woman presents with palpitations, heat intolerance, 12-lb weight loss over 2 months despite increased appetite, and 4–5 loose stools daily. She notes her eyes appear to be "bulging." She has no prior medical history. Her mother has rheumatoid arthritis.

Vital Signs: BP 152/64 mmHg | HR 112 bpm (irregular) | RR 18/min | Temp 37.9°C | SpO₂ 99%

Physical Exam: Bilateral proptosis, lid lag, and chemosis. Thyroid: Diffusely enlarged, smooth, non-tender goiter with an audible bruit. Skin: warm and moist. Fine tremor of outstretched hands. Cardiac: Irregularly irregular rhythm.

Labs:



TSH: < 0.01 mIU/L (Normal 0.5–4.5) ↓

Free T4: 4.8 ng/dL (Normal 0.8–1.8) ↑

Free T3: 12.6 pg/mL (Normal 2.3–4.2) ↑

TSI (thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulins): Positive (Normal: negative) ↑

Anti-TPO antibodies: Positive (Normal: negative) ↑

Which of the following best explains the increased cardiac output and wide pulse pressure observed in this patient?



A. Catecholamine excess from adrenal medullary activation causing direct beta-1 receptor stimulation

B. Thyroid hormone-mediated upregulation of cardiac beta-adrenergic receptors and direct transcriptional activation of cardiac myosin heavy chain, increasing heart rate and contractility, with peripheral vasodilation reducing SVR

C. Atrial fibrillation causing loss of atrial contribution to ventricular filling and reduced stroke volume

D. Autonomous TSH-producing pituitary adenoma driving excess thyroid hormone synthesis

E. Iron deficiency anemia causing high-output cardiac failure from compensatory tachycardia

Correct Answer: B

Reasoning: This is Graves' disease, confirmed by TSI positivity (the pathognomonic autoantibody). Excess T3 exerts profound cardiac effects through two converging pathways. First, T3 directly enters cardiomyocyte nuclei and binds thyroid hormone response elements (TREs), transcriptionally upregulating: α-myosin heavy chain (faster ATPase activity → increased contractility), SERCA2a (faster sarcoplasmic Ca²⁺ reuptake → improved diastolic function and shorter systole allowing faster heart rates), and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (faster membrane repolarization). Second, T3 transcriptionally increases β₁-adrenergic receptor density on cardiomyocytes, amplifying catecholamine sensitivity — producing tachycardia and enhanced contractility without any increase in actual catecholamine levels. Peripherally, thyroid hormone directly relaxes vascular smooth muscle and increases tissue metabolic rate, requiring greater oxygen delivery, both reducing SVR. The result: markedly elevated HR + elevated SV + decreased SVR = high cardiac output with a wide pulse pressure (large systolic, low diastolic).

Why the other choices are wrong:

A — Plasma and urinary catecholamines are normal in Graves' disease. The adrenergic symptoms (palpitations, tremor, sweating) arise from receptor sensitization, not excess catecholamine production. Pheochromocytoma would cause hypertensive crises, episodic diaphoresis, and headache — and critically, would not produce exophthalmos or a positive TSI.

C — Atrial fibrillation in this patient is a consequence of hyperthyroidism, not its cause. Loss of atrial kick in AF actually reduces cardiac output by 10–15% — the hyperthyroid state more than compensates by increasing HR and contractility. AF here is a downstream complication, not the mechanism of high output.

D — A TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma (thyrotropinoma) is extremely rare and would show inappropriately normal or elevated TSH in the setting of high thyroid hormone levels. This patient's TSH is undetectable (< 0.01 mIU/L), which excludes any central pituitary-driven cause and confirms peripheral hyperthyroidism.

E — Iron deficiency anemia can cause compensatory high-output cardiac physiology, but there is no anemia documented in these labs. The specific autoimmune features — proptosis, thyroid bruit, and positive TSI — are not explained by iron deficiency and are pathognomonic for Graves' disease.

Question 7

A 72-year-old man with a 30-year history of poorly controlled type 2 diabetes (HbA1c consistently > 9%) and hypertension is referred for CKD evaluation. He takes metformin (recently held), lisinopril, and amlodipine. He denies hematuria or flank pain.

Vital Signs: BP 156/94 mmHg | HR 78 bpm | RR 16/min | Temp 36.7°C | SpO₂ 97%

Physical Exam: Fundoscopy reveals dot-blot hemorrhages, hard exudates, and neovascularization (proliferative diabetic retinopathy). 1+ bilateral pedal edema. Decreased monofilament sensation bilateral feet. Diminished ankle deep tendon reflexes bilaterally.

Labs:



Creatinine: 3.4 mg/dL (Normal 0.7–1.3) ↑

eGFR: 18 mL/min/1.73m² (Normal > 60) ↓

Urine albumin/creatinine ratio: 2,800 mg/g (Normal < 30) ↑

HbA1c: 10.1% (Normal < 5.7%) ↑

Serum albumin: 2.9 g/dL (Normal 3.5–5.0) ↓

Potassium: 5.6 mEq/L (Normal 3.5–5.0) ↑

Which of the following sequences best describes the initial pathophysiological mechanism leading to this patient's glomerular damage?



A. Hypertension → shear stress → endothelial denudation → platelet microthrombi → glomerular ischemia

B. Hyperglycemia → AGE accumulation and PKC activation → mesangial expansion and GBM thickening → intraglomerular hypertension → progressive proteinuria

C. Immune complex deposition in the mesangium → complement activation → inflammatory GBM destruction

D. Obesity-mediated RAAS activation → hyperfiltration → focal segmental glomerulosclerosis

E. Age-related nephron loss → compensatory hyperfiltration in remaining nephrons → secondary FSGS

Correct Answer: B

Reasoning: This is classic diabetic nephropathy (Kimmelstiel-Wilson disease), confirmed by the complete microvascular triad: retinopathy, neuropathy, and nephropathy after 30 years of poor glycemic control. The pathophysiological sequence begins with chronic hyperglycemia activating multiple converging pathways simultaneously. (1) Non-enzymatic glycation of proteins forms advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), which crosslink GBM collagen (causing thickening and reduced anionic charge), activate RAGE receptors on mesangial cells triggering TGF-β and VEGF release promoting matrix expansion, and increase GBM permeability. (2) Protein kinase C (PKC) activation (from diacylglycerol accumulation via the glucose → DAG pathway) drives mesangial cell proliferation and excess matrix production. (3) The glucose-driven polyol pathway generates reactive oxygen species that cause direct podocyte injury — destroying the critical size and charge barrier. (4) Intraglomerular hypertension (from afferent arteriolar vasodilation by hyperglycemia-driven prostaglandins and glucagon, while efferent arteriolar tone is relatively maintained) increases GFR (early hyperfiltration). (5) Over decades, Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules (pathognomonic nodular mesangial sclerosis), GBM thickening, and podocyte loss produce the nephrotic-range proteinuria and progressive GFR decline seen in this patient.

Why the other choices are wrong:

A — Hypertensive nephrosclerosis (arterionephrosclerosis) causes arteriolar hyalinosis and ischemic glomerular obsolescence, producing mild proteinuria (typically < 1 g/day) and slowly progressive GFR loss. The massive albuminuria of 2,800 mg/g combined with 30 years of poorly controlled diabetes makes diabetic nephropathy the overwhelmingly dominant process.

C — Immune complex-mediated glomerulonephritis (IgA nephropathy, post-infectious GN, lupus nephritis) produces mesangial or subendothelial immune deposits visible on immunofluorescence and electron microscopy. Diabetic nephropathy shows no immune deposits — the GBM thickening is entirely from non-immunological AGE accumulation and collagen crosslinking. This would be the correct answer if the patient had cola-colored urine, a sore throat 2 weeks prior, or an active lupus history.

D — Obesity-related FSGS is a distinct clinicopathologic entity characterized by perihilar FSGS lesions on biopsy and mild-to-moderate proteinuria. While obesity may contribute, the presence of all three classic microvascular complications after 30 years of poor glucose control makes diabetic nephropathy the primary diagnosis, not obesity-FSGS.

E — Age-related nephron loss does cause some compensatory hyperfiltration and can eventually produce secondary FSGS, but this process generates only modest proteinuria and does not explain nephrotic-range albuminuria of this magnitude in the context of decades of uncontrolled diabetes with microvascular complications in all three target organs.

Question 8

A 45-year-old man is brought to the ED after a witnessed tonic-clonic seizure lasting 4 minutes. His wife reports 3 days of progressive confusion and combativeness following a fever. He was treated for bacterial meningitis 6 weeks ago and reportedly "fully recovered." He has no prior seizure disorder and takes no medications.

Vital Signs: BP 134/82 mmHg | HR 102 bpm | RR 18/min | Temp 38.6°C | SpO₂ 98%

Physical Exam: Confused, oriented to name only (GCS 12). Intermittent stereotyped oro-facial automatisms observed. No nuchal rigidity. No papilledema. DTR 2+ and symmetric bilaterally.

Labs:



Serum Na⁺: 134 mEq/L (Normal 135–145) ↓

Glucose: 96 mg/dL (Normal 70–100)

CSF protein: 68 mg/dL (Normal 15–45) ↑

CSF WBC: 22/mm³, predominantly lymphocytic (Normal < 5) ↑

CSF glucose: 58 mg/dL (normal ratio to serum)

Anti-NMDA receptor antibodies (serum): Positive (Normal: negative) ↑

Which pathophysiological mechanism is most responsible for this patient's altered consciousness and seizures?



A. Bacterial re-seeding of the meninges from a parameningeal focus causing purulent meningitis relapse

B. IgG autoantibodies against NMDA receptors causing receptor internalization and disruption of GABAergic-glutamatergic synaptic balance

C. HSV-1 reactivation causing hemorrhagic temporal lobe necrosis

D. Paraneoplastic anti-Hu antibodies from an occult small-cell lung carcinoma destroying limbic neurons

E. Non-convulsive status epilepticus from electrolyte-induced cortical irritability

Correct Answer: B

Reasoning: This is anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis, the most common autoimmune encephalitis. IgG autoantibodies (directed against the GluN1 subunit of the NMDAR) bind receptors on the neuronal surface, causing internalization and degradation via crosslinking-mediated endocytosis, effectively reducing NMDAR-mediated synaptic signaling. The mechanism of symptom production is elegant: GABAergic interneurons tonically suppress excitatory circuits and are particularly dependent on NMDAR input. When NMDAR function is globally reduced, these inhibitory interneurons are preferentially silenced, producing disinhibition of excitatory glutamatergic neurons — a net pro-excitatory state. This explains: seizures (cortical hyperexcitability), psychosis and agitation (cortical/limbic disinhibition mimicking NMDAR antagonists such as PCP or ketamine), and oro-facial automatisms (temporal-limbic circuit involvement). The prior "bacterial meningitis" 6 weeks ago may have been the encephalitic prodrome misdiagnosed, or an HSV encephalitis that triggered an autoimmune cascade (a recognized association).

Why the other choices are wrong:

A — Bacterial meningitis relapse would produce a markedly different CSF profile: neutrophilic pleocytosis (hundreds of WBCs), very low glucose (< 45 mg/dL), and elevated protein. This patient's CSF shows only mild lymphocytic pleocytosis with a normal glucose ratio — consistent with viral or autoimmune etiology, not bacterial re-infection. There would also be nuchal rigidity and meningismus.

C — HSV-1 encephalitis targets the temporal and frontal lobes, causing hemorrhagic necrosis with fever, temporal lobe seizures, and olfactory hallucinations. It would show HSV PCR positivity in CSF, temporal lobe hyperintensity with hemorrhage on MRI, and no NMDAR antibodies. Importantly, anti-NMDAR encephalitis can occur as an autoimmune sequel to HSV-1 encephalitis — making this clinically plausible as a trigger, but HSV would not be the current active mechanism with a negative CSF PCR implied by this workup.

D — Anti-Hu antibodies (ANNA-1) are associated with paraneoplastic sensory neuropathy and limbic encephalitis from small-cell lung carcinoma. They cause T-cell-mediated neuronal destruction rather than receptor internalization, produce a different clinical syndrome (subacute sensory neuropathy prominent), and the specific antibody here is anti-NMDAR, not anti-Hu. These are distinct entities requiring distinct antibody testing.

E — Non-convulsive status epilepticus (NCSE) requires EEG confirmation and is a phenomenon (a consequence), not a mechanism (a cause). The mild hyponatremia at 134 mEq/L does not reach the threshold for seizure-provoking cortical irritability (typically < 120 mEq/L). The positive anti-NMDAR antibody provides the etiological diagnosis; NCSE may be occurring but is downstream of the autoimmune encephalitis.

Question 1

A 16-year-old male high school basketball player is brought to the emergency department by his coach after collapsing on the court during the final minutes of a championship game. Witnesses report he suddenly clutched his chest and fell. He was resuscitated with an AED within 2 minutes. He has no known medical history, takes no medications, and denies recreational drug use. His older brother died suddenly at age 21 during a soccer game.

Vital Signs: BP 108/72 mmHg | HR 88 bpm (post-resuscitation, sinus rhythm) | RR 18/min | Temp 37.1°C | SpO₂ 97%

Physical Exam: A harsh 3/6 systolic ejection murmur heard best at the left lower sternal border that increases in intensity with the Valsalva maneuver and decreases with squatting. S4 gallop present. No peripheral edema. PMI is sustained.

Labs:



Troponin I: 0.9 ng/mL (Normal < 0.04) ↑

ECG: LVH pattern, deep Q waves in leads I, aVL, and V4–V6, ST changes

Echocardiogram: Asymmetric septal hypertrophy (septal:posterior wall ratio 1.7:1), systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the mitral valve, LVOT gradient 60 mmHg at rest

Which of the following best explains why this patient's murmur increases with the Valsalva maneuver?



A. Valsalva increases venous return, distending the LV and increasing the LVOT gradient

B. Valsalva decreases venous return, reducing LV end-diastolic volume, allowing the hypertrophied septum to further obstruct the LVOT and worsening the dynamic gradient

C. Valsalva increases systemic vascular resistance, increasing afterload and causing greater turbulence across the outflow tract

D. Valsalva triggers reflex bradycardia via the Bezold-Jarisch reflex, prolonging diastolic filling and worsening obstruction

E. Valsalva increases intrathoracic pressure, compressing pulmonary veins and increasing left atrial pressure, worsening mitral regurgitation

Correct Answer: B

Reasoning: This is hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM), the most common cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes. The obstruction in HOCM is dynamic — it depends on LV cavity size. The hypertrophied interventricular septum and systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the mitral valve create a variable outflow obstruction that worsens when the LV cavity is small and improves when it is large. During the strain phase of the Valsalva maneuver, increased intrathoracic pressure impedes venous return to the right heart, reducing right-sided and subsequently left-sided preload. The LV end-diastolic volume falls. The smaller LV cavity brings the hypertrophied septum into closer proximity with the anterior mitral leaflet during systole, increasing SAM severity and raising the LVOT pressure gradient. This reduces stroke volume and worsens the murmur. The rule: anything that decreases LV volume (Valsalva strain, standing, dehydration, tachycardia) worsens HOCM; anything that increases LV volume (squatting, passive leg raise, increased preload) improves it. This is the opposite of fixed-obstruction lesions like valvular aortic stenosis.

Why the other choices are wrong:

A — This describes the opposite physiology. Increased venous return would distend the LV, increasing end-diastolic volume, moving the septum away from the LVOT, and reducing the dynamic gradient — thereby softening the murmur. This pattern occurs with squatting or passive leg raise, which improve HOCM.

C — Increased afterload (higher SVR) would cause LV pressure to rise, which actually tends to distend the LV and push the septum away from the LVOT, marginally reducing obstruction. Increased afterload worsens fixed outflow obstructions (like AS) but has a different effect on dynamic obstructions. This is not the dominant mechanism during Valsalva.

D — The Bezold-Jarisch reflex involves bradycardia and vasodilation triggered by inferior MI or ventricular mechanoreceptor stimulation — not by the Valsalva maneuver. Valsalva characteristically produces a phase II tachycardia from sympathetic activation as BP falls, then a phase IV overshoot bradycardia. Bradycardia would actually allow more diastolic filling, which would reduce obstruction, not worsen it.

E — This choice misidentifies the mechanism. Valsalva increases intrathoracic pressure, which actually impedes pulmonary venous return to the left atrium — reducing, not increasing, left atrial filling. The murmur change in HOCM is driven by altered LV preload and cavity size, not by changes in mitral regurgitation severity per se.

Question 2

A 62-year-old male retired shipyard worker with a 35-pack-year smoking history presents with 6 weeks of progressive dyspnea, a dry cough, and a 15-lb unintentional weight loss. He reports right-sided chest pain that worsens with deep inspiration. His wife notes his face appears "puffy" and his neck veins seem prominent, especially in the morning.

Vital Signs: BP 148/92 mmHg (right arm) vs. 142/88 mmHg (left arm) | HR 94 bpm | RR 20/min | Temp 37.2°C | SpO₂ 91% on room air

Physical Exam: Face: plethoric with bilateral periorbital edema. Neck: distended jugular veins bilaterally without collapse on inspiration. Right chest: dullness to percussion at the base with absent breath sounds. Trachea: midline. No peripheral edema of the limbs.

Labs:



Pleural fluid (thoracentesis): pH 7.18, LDH 420 U/L (serum LDH 210 U/L), protein 5.8 g/dL (serum protein 6.2 g/dL), glucose 38 mg/dL, numerous atypical cells

Hemoglobin: 10.2 g/dL (Normal 13.5–17.5) ↓

Sodium: 132 mEq/L (Normal 135–145) ↓

CT Chest: Large right-sided pleural effusion; right hilar mass with mediastinal involvement; narrowing of the superior vena cava

What is the primary pathophysiological mechanism responsible for this patient's facial plethora, periorbital edema, and distended neck veins?



A. Right heart failure from hypoxia-induced pulmonary vasoconstriction causing elevated right atrial pressure and systemic venous hypertension

B. Hypoalbuminemia from malignancy-related malnutrition reducing oncotic pressure and causing fluid to shift into the interstitial compartment

C. Extrinsic compression of the superior vena cava by the mediastinal tumor mass obstructing venous drainage from the head, neck, and upper extremities

D. Pericardial tamponade from malignant pericardial effusion compressing the right atrium and obstructing venous inflow

E. Paraneoplastic SIADH from ectopic ADH secretion causing water retention and dilutional hyponatremia with secondary venous congestion

Correct Answer: C

Reasoning: This is superior vena cava (SVC) syndrome, a classic oncological emergency. The right hilar mass with mediastinal involvement (most commonly small-cell lung carcinoma or squamous cell carcinoma in a smoker, or lymphoma) directly compresses the SVC, which has a thin, low-pressure wall and is particularly vulnerable to extrinsic compression. The SVC drains venous blood from the entire head, neck, bilateral upper extremities, and anterior chest wall into the right atrium. When it is obstructed, venous pressure rises proximal to the obstruction — manifesting as facial plethora (venous engorgement of the face), periorbital non-pitting edema (lymphatic congestion), distended neck veins that paradoxically do not collapse on inspiration (because the obstruction is fixed, not pressure-dependent), and in severe cases, cerebral edema (headache, altered consciousness). The inspiratory non-collapse of jugular veins (Kussmaul's sign) is seen here because the obstruction is structural, not caused by increased pericardial or intrathoracic pressure variation. The exudative pleural effusion with atypical cells confirms malignancy. The hyponatremia in this case is also consistent with paraneoplastic SIADH (very common in small-cell lung cancer), but the facial and venous findings are mechanically explained by SVC obstruction.

Why the other choices are wrong:

A — Right heart failure from cor pulmonale would cause elevated JVP and peripheral edema but would also cause bilateral lower extremity edema, hepatomegaly, and ascites — not the selective facial and upper body edema pattern seen here. Cor pulmonale produces systemic venous hypertension globally, not the localized head-neck distribution of SVC syndrome.

B — Hypoalbuminemia reduces oncotic pressure, causing fluid to shift into interstitial spaces — producing diffuse, bilateral, dependent edema (ankles, sacrum) or ascites. It does not cause the selective facial and upper body distribution or distended non-collapsing neck veins seen in SVC syndrome. Albumin is not reported as critically low here.

D — Pericardial tamponade classically presents with Beck's triad: hypotension, distended neck veins, and muffled heart sounds. It would cause pulsus paradoxus (> 10 mmHg BP drop during inspiration) and equalization of diastolic pressures across all chambers. The facial plethora and periorbital edema selective to the upper body distribution, combined with CT evidence of SVC narrowing, make SVC syndrome far more specific than tamponade as the primary diagnosis.

E — Paraneoplastic SIADH (ectopic ADH from small-cell lung cancer) causes dilutional hyponatremia with water retention — which is plausible given the Na⁺ of 132 mEq/L. However, SIADH causes euvolemic hyponatremia with water retention distributed throughout body water. It does not selectively cause facial plethora, periorbital edema, and non-collapsing jugular venous distension — those are specifically mechanical signs of impaired venous drainage, not hormonal water retention.

Question 3

A 58-year-old woman with a history of rheumatoid arthritis (on chronic prednisone 10 mg/day for 4 years) presents to the ED with severe left flank pain radiating to the groin, nausea, and vomiting for 6 hours. She has a history of type 2 diabetes managed with metformin. She is in significant distress.

Vital Signs: BP 156/96 mmHg | HR 108 bpm | RR 20/min | Temp 37.3°C | SpO₂ 98%

Physical Exam: Left costovertebral angle tenderness on percussion. Abdomen: mild left lower quadrant tenderness without peritoneal signs. No masses palpated.

Labs:



Serum Ca²⁺: 10.6 mg/dL (Normal 8.5–10.5) ↑

Serum phosphate: 2.1 mg/dL (Normal 2.5–4.5) ↓

PTH: 148 pg/mL (Normal 10–65) ↑

Urine Ca²⁺/creatinine ratio: Markedly elevated ↑

Urine pH: 5.0

Urinalysis: 3+ blood, no WBCs, no bacteria

Non-contrast CT abdomen/pelvis: 6mm left ureteral stone with proximal hydroureter

Which of the following best explains the pathophysiological sequence leading to nephrolithiasis in this patient?



A. Chronic prednisone use causing hypercalcemia via increased intestinal calcium absorption from activated vitamin D

B. Primary hyperparathyroidism causing elevated PTH → increased renal tubular calcium reabsorption, increased 1α-hydroxylase activity → elevated 1,25-(OH)₂D → increased intestinal calcium absorption → hypercalciuria exceeding renal reabsorptive capacity → calcium oxalate/phosphate stone formation

C. Distal renal tubular acidosis causing alkaline urine with calcium phosphate precipitation

D. Hyperuricosuria from metformin-induced purine catabolism promoting uric acid stone formation

E. Medullary sponge kidney causing urinary stasis and infection-related struvite stone formation

Correct Answer: B

Reasoning: This patient has primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT), the most common cause of hypercalcemia in outpatients, typically caused by a single parathyroid adenoma. The complete pathophysiological sequence is: Excess PTH acts on three target organs simultaneously. (1) Kidney: PTH activates adenylyl cyclase via Gs-protein coupling in the distal tubule and thick ascending limb, stimulating Ca²⁺ reabsorption (short-term protective effect). Simultaneously, PTH stimulates 1α-hydroxylase in the proximal tubule, converting 25-(OH)D → 1,25-(OH)₂D (calcitriol). PTH also inhibits phosphate reabsorption in the proximal tubule (causing hypophosphatemia — a key diagnostic clue). (2) Intestine: elevated calcitriol dramatically increases active calcium absorption via TRPV6 channels and calbindin in the duodenum. (3) Bone: PTH stimulates osteoclast activity (via RANK-L upregulation on osteoblasts), releasing Ca²⁺ from bone. The combined effect overwhelms the kidney's reabsorptive capacity → hypercalciuria. In the concentrated, acidic urine (pH 5.0) with high calcium concentration, calcium oxalate (most common) and calcium phosphate stones precipitate. Chronic prednisone use causes hypercalciuria through a different mechanism (see below) and may be a contributing factor, but the elevated PTH with hypophosphatemia diagnoses PHPT as the primary driver.

Why the other choices are wrong:

A — Glucocorticoids (prednisone) cause hypercalciuria via a completely different mechanism: they decrease intestinal calcium absorption (by reducing vitamin D receptor signaling) AND increase renal calcium wasting, stimulating PTH secondarily. This produces secondary hyperparathyroidism with normal or low-normal serum calcium — not hypercalcemia. Prednisone does increase stone risk via hypercalciuria, but it does not cause primary hypercalcemia. This choice reverses the mechanism.

C — Distal renal tubular acidosis (Type 1 RTA) causes an inability to acidify urine below pH 5.5, producing alkaline urine (pH > 5.5) with calcium phosphate stones (brushite). This patient's urine pH is 5.0 — appropriately acidic — which argues against RTA. Additionally, Type 1 RTA would show a non-anion gap metabolic acidosis with hypokalemia, not the hypercalcemia/hypophosphatemia pattern seen here.

D — Metformin does not cause hyperuricosuria or uric acid stones. Metformin inhibits hepatic gluconeogenesis and improves insulin sensitivity — it has no effect on purine catabolism. Uric acid stones occur in gout, high purine intake, or myeloproliferative disorders. Uric acid stones are radiolucent (not seen on plain film but visible on CT) and form in acidic urine — but the serum uric acid and clinical context here point away from this mechanism.

E — Medullary sponge kidney causes urinary stasis in ectatic collecting ducts, predisposing to calcium oxalate stones and urinary tract infections — but it is a structural congenital anomaly not caused by the metabolic derangements seen here. Struvite (infection) stones form in alkaline urine from urease-producing organisms (Proteus, Klebsiella). There is no pyuria or bacteriuria in this patient's urinalysis.

Question 4

A 29-year-old woman presents to her primary care physician with 8 months of episodic severe headaches, diaphoresis, and palpitations that last 15–30 minutes and resolve spontaneously. She also notes occasional pallor followed by facial flushing. Her blood pressure during these episodes is reportedly "very high." She has no family history of hypertension. She takes oral contraceptives.

Vital Signs (during clinic visit, between episodes): BP 124/78 mmHg | HR 74 bpm | RR 14/min | Temp 36.8°C | SpO₂ 99%

Physical Exam: No abnormalities detected during the inter-episode examination. No thyromegaly. No café-au-lait spots or neurofibromas on skin examination.

Labs:



24-hour urine metanephrines: 4,200 mcg/24hr (Normal < 400) ↑

24-hour urine catecholamines: Epinephrine 820 mcg/24hr (Normal < 20), Norepinephrine 1,140 mcg/24hr (Normal < 80) ↑

Plasma free metanephrines: Markedly elevated ↑

Serum glucose: 142 mg/dL (fasting) (Normal 70–100) ↑

Serum calcium: 11.2 mg/dL (Normal 8.5–10.5) ↑

PTH: 112 pg/mL (Normal 10–65) ↑

Which of the following best explains the episodic hypertension and hyperglycemia in this patient?



A. Oral contraceptive-induced RAAS activation causing aldosterone excess and episodic hypertension with insulin resistance

B. Pheochromocytoma causing episodic catecholamine release: epinephrine stimulates hepatic glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis via beta-2 receptors while norepinephrine and epinephrine cause alpha-1-mediated peripheral vasoconstriction producing severe hypertension

C. Primary hyperaldosteronism causing sodium retention, hypertension, and hypokalemia-induced insulin secretion impairment

D. Carcinoid tumor causing serotonin and bradykinin release producing flushing, hypertension, and glucose intolerance

E. Thyroid storm causing adrenergic hyperactivation with catecholamine sensitization, hypertension, and glucose dysregulation

Correct Answer: B

Reasoning: This is a classic pheochromocytoma presentation — the "5 P's": Pressure (episodic hypertension), Pain (headache), Perspiration (diaphoresis), Palpitations, and Pallor followed by flushing. The co-occurrence of hypercalcemia (from PTH-producing parathyroid adenoma) and the pheochromocytoma strongly suggests MEN 2A (multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2A: pheochromocytoma + primary hyperparathyroidism + medullary thyroid carcinoma from RET proto-oncogene mutation). The dual pathophysiology of catecholamine excess: (1) Hypertension mechanism — epinephrine and norepinephrine bind alpha-1 adrenergic receptors on vascular smooth muscle, activating Gq → phospholipase C → IP3/DAG → intracellular Ca²⁺ release → vasoconstriction and dramatically elevated SVR. The episodic nature reflects bursts of catecholamine secretion from the tumor. (2) Hyperglycemia mechanism — epinephrine acts on hepatic beta-2 receptors, activating Gs → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → PKA → phosphorylation of glycogen phosphorylase (activating glycogenolysis) and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (stimulating gluconeogenesis). Simultaneously, epinephrine inhibits insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells via alpha-2 receptors, and promotes glucagon secretion from alpha cells via beta-2 receptors. The net result is stress hyperglycemia.

Why the other choices are wrong:

A — Oral contraceptives (estrogen) do stimulate hepatic angiotensinogen production, mildly activating the RAAS and causing a modest, sustained blood pressure increase — but this effect is mild, non-episodic, and does not cause the dramatic paroxysmal hypertension, diaphoresis, headache, and markedly elevated catecholamine levels seen here. OCP-induced hypertension would not cause fasting hyperglycemia or hypercalcemia.

C — Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn syndrome) causes sustained (not episodic) hypertension with hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, and suppressed renin. Hypokalemia impairs insulin secretion, mildly raising glucose — but the glucose elevation would be modest and the defining feature is hypokalemia, not markedly elevated catecholamines. Aldosterone excess does not cause diaphoresis, headache, or palpitations.

D — Carcinoid tumors (from enterochromaffin cells) cause the carcinoid syndrome: episodic flushing, diarrhea, wheezing, and right-sided valvular heart disease from serotonin and bradykinin. The diagnosis requires elevated 24-hour urine 5-HIAA (serotonin metabolite), not elevated catecholamines or metanephrines. Carcinoid does not typically cause severe hypertension or hyperglycemia.

E — Thyroid storm would require pre-existing hyperthyroidism (suppressed TSH, elevated T3/T4) and is typically triggered by a physiological stressor. It does not cause markedly elevated catecholamines or metanephrines. Thyroid hormone sensitizes adrenergic receptors but does not produce its own catecholamines. Normal thyroid labs in this patient would exclude this diagnosis.

Question 5

A 7-year-old boy is brought by his parents for evaluation of muscle weakness, particularly when climbing stairs or rising from the floor. His parents note he "walks on his toes" and his calves appear unusually large and muscular. When asked to stand from sitting on the floor, he places his hands on his knees and walks his hands up his thighs to stand upright. He has no prior medical history. His maternal uncle was wheelchair-bound by age 12 and died of respiratory failure at age 19.

Vital Signs: BP 100/64 mmHg | HR 88 bpm | RR 18/min | Temp 36.9°C | SpO₂ 99%

Physical Exam: Bilateral calf pseudohypertrophy. Proximal lower extremity weakness (4-/5). Gowers' sign positive. Mild lumbar lordosis. Cardiac: regular rate and rhythm; no murmurs.

Labs:



Creatine kinase (CK): 18,400 U/L (Normal 22–198) ↑

Aldolase: 68 U/L (Normal < 8) ↑

ECG: Tall R waves in V1, deep Q waves in limb leads

Muscle biopsy: Absent dystrophin staining on immunohistochemistry; fiber size variation with fibrosis and fatty replacement

Genetic testing: Large deletion in the DMD gene (Xp21), frameshift mutation abolishing dystrophin production

Which of the following best explains the pathophysiological mechanism by which dystrophin deficiency leads to progressive muscle fiber destruction and elevated serum CK?



A. Dystrophin deficiency impairs mitochondrial ATP synthesis, causing energy failure and apoptotic muscle cell death

B. Absence of dystrophin disrupts the dystrophin-associated glycoprotein complex (DGC), disconnecting the intracellular actin cytoskeleton from the extracellular matrix, causing membrane instability during contraction, calcium influx, protease activation, and necrosis

C. Dystrophin deficiency upregulates myostatin, suppressing satellite cell regeneration and causing progressive fiber loss without replacement

D. Loss of dystrophin causes abnormal titin-myosin interaction, producing sarcomeric disarray and inefficient force generation with secondary inflammatory fiber destruction

E. Dystrophin deficiency causes aberrant splicing of ryanodine receptor mRNA, resulting in uncontrolled calcium release from the SR and hypercontracture necrosis

Correct Answer: B

Reasoning: This is Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), caused by X-linked frameshift mutations in the DMD gene that abolish dystrophin protein production (Becker MD, with in-frame mutations, preserves partial function). Dystrophin is a large structural protein (427 kDa) that forms a critical mechanical link: intracellularly, it binds F-actin of the subsarcolemmal cytoskeleton; extracellularly (via the DGC — dystroglycan, sarcoglycans, syntrophins, dystrobrevins), it anchors to laminin-2 in the basement membrane. This DGC complex acts as a molecular "shock absorber," distributing the lateral mechanical force generated during eccentric muscle contraction across the sarcolemma. Without dystrophin, this link is broken. During repeated muscle contractions (especially eccentric loading — stairs, rising from floor), uncompensated shear forces tear micro-defects in the fragile sarcolemma. These membrane lesions allow unregulated Ca²⁺ influx from the extracellular space (where [Ca²⁺] = 1.2 mM) into the cytoplasm (where resting [Ca²⁺] = 100 nM) — a 10,000-fold gradient. Elevated intracellular Ca²⁺ activates calpains (Ca²⁺-dependent proteases), phospholipases, and mitochondrial permeability transition pores — driving fiber necrosis. CK leaks massively from the necrotic fibers into the circulation. The pseudohypertrophy of the calves results from fatty and fibrous tissue replacing necrotic muscle fibers — not true muscle growth.

Why the other choices are wrong:

A — Mitochondrial dysfunction is not the primary mechanism in DMD. DMD results from structural membrane failure during mechanical loading, not from impaired oxidative phosphorylation. Mitochondrial myopathies (e.g., MELAS, MERRF) produce a different clinical picture — proximal weakness with lactic acidosis, ragged-red fibers on Gomori trichrome staining, and multi-system involvement.

C — Myostatin is a TGF-β family member that inhibits muscle growth. Loss-of-function mutations in myostatin cause muscle hypertrophy (the "mighty mouse" model). In DMD, myostatin is not upregulated as the primary pathological mechanism — the issue is mechanical membrane failure and calcium-mediated necrosis. Impaired satellite cell regeneration is a downstream consequence of repeated cycles of necrosis exhausting the regenerative pool, not a myostatin-driven primary event.

D — Titin is a giant sarcomeric protein acting as a molecular spring connecting myosin to the Z-disk, providing passive elasticity. Titin mutations cause dilated cardiomyopathy and limb-girdle muscular dystrophy type 2J. Dystrophin does not directly interact with titin or the myosin cross-bridge cycle. Sarcomeric disarray is not the initiating event in DMD.

E — Ryanodine receptor (RyR1) dysfunction causes malignant hyperthermia and central core disease — characterized by uncontrolled Ca²⁺ release from the SR during anesthesia (halothane/succinylcholine) or extreme exercise. In DMD, the excess Ca²⁺ enters from outside the cell through damaged sarcolemmal tears — not from SR release. Dystrophin has no direct regulatory relationship with RyR1 mRNA splicing.

Question 6

A 44-year-old male long-distance truck driver with a BMI of 38 is referred by his wife after she observed episodes of loud snoring, gasping, and witnessed apneic pauses during sleep. He reports excessive daytime sleepiness, waking with headaches, difficulty concentrating, and two near-miss driving accidents in the past month. He has a history of hypertension and takes amlodipine. He does not smoke or drink alcohol.

Vital Signs: BP 158/98 mmHg | HR 82 bpm | RR 16/min | Temp 36.8°C | SpO₂ 89% at rest on room air

Physical Exam: Neck circumference: 46 cm. Oropharynx: Mallampati class IV (soft palate not visible). Uvula: elongated and edematous. Bilateral tonsillar hypertrophy. Cardiovascular: regular rate and rhythm, no murmurs. No peripheral edema.

Labs:



Overnight polysomnography: AHI (apnea-hypopnea index) 58 events/hr (Normal < 5); minimum SpO₂ 72%; 42% of total sleep time with SpO₂ < 90%

Morning ABG: pH 7.34 | PaO₂ 54 mmHg | PaCO₂ 52 mmHg | HCO₃⁻ 27 mEq/L

Hematocrit: 54% (Normal 40–52) ↑

Fasting glucose: 118 mg/dL (Normal 70–100) ↑

ECG: Sinus rhythm; right axis deviation; right ventricular strain pattern

Which of the following best explains the mechanism by which obstructive sleep apnea causes systemic hypertension that persists throughout the day?



A. Nocturnal hypoxia causing erythrocytosis, increasing blood viscosity and peripheral vascular resistance

B. Repetitive nocturnal hypoxia and hypercapnia activating peripheral chemoreceptors → sympathetic surge → generalized vasoconstriction → chronic resetting of sympathetic baseline tone, elevated circulating norepinephrine, and structural vascular remodeling persisting during waking hours

C. Hypercapnia-induced renal HCO₃⁻ retention causing metabolic alkalosis that directly stimulates vascular smooth muscle contraction

D. Obesity-mediated RAAS activation from adipose tissue-derived angiotensinogen causing aldosterone excess and sodium retention

E. Sleep fragmentation causing cortisol hypersecretion from circadian HPA axis disruption producing glucocorticoid-mediated hypertension

Correct Answer: B

Reasoning: Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) causes sustained daytime hypertension through chronic sympathetic nervous system sensitization — the dominant, best-established mechanism. During each obstructive apnea event, progressive hypoxia and hypercapnia stimulate both the peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid bodies, sensing PaO₂ and PaCO₂) and central chemoreceptors (medullary neurons, sensing PCO₂/pH). This triggers a powerful sympathetic efferent surge: generalized vasoconstriction, tachycardia, and a blood pressure spike (often exceeding 200 mmHg during arousals). With 58 events per hour across a full sleep cycle, the sympathetic system is repeatedly activated 400–500 times per night. Over months and years, this produces: (1) chronic upregulation of sympathetic baseline tone and elevated resting plasma norepinephrine (shown by microneurography studies demonstrating elevated muscle sympathetic nerve activity in OSA patients even while awake), (2) functional vascular remodeling — endothelial dysfunction, reduced NO bioavailability from oxidative stress, increased endothelin-1 production, and smooth muscle hypertrophy — shifting the pressure-natriuresis curve. The result is hypertension that does not dip appropriately at night (non-dipping pattern on ambulatory monitoring) and persists during waking hours. CPAP therapy demonstrably lowers blood pressure in OSA, confirming the causal link.

Why the other choices are wrong:

A — Erythrocytosis increases blood viscosity (by the Hagen-Poiseuille relationship: resistance ∝ viscosity), which does raise peripheral vascular resistance and contribute to blood pressure — but this is a secondary, contributing mechanism, not the primary explanation for the sustained daytime hypertension. The dominant pathway is sympathetically mediated, and erythrocytosis alone would not explain the non-dipping nocturnal pattern or the response to CPAP.

C — Hypercapnia causes renal HCO₃⁻ retention as the metabolic compensation for respiratory acidosis (as seen in this patient's morning ABG: HCO₃⁻ 27 mEq/L). However, metabolic alkalosis does not directly stimulate vascular smooth muscle contraction — alkalosis actually causes mild vasodilation. This choice inverts the physiological effect of alkalosis on vascular tone.

D — Adipose tissue does produce angiotensinogen (contributing to obesity-related hypertension via RAAS activation) — and this is a genuine, real mechanism. However, the question asks specifically about the mechanism of OSA-induced hypertension. In controlled studies, CPAP therapy lowers blood pressure in OSA patients even without weight loss, confirming that the apnea-driven sympathetic mechanism is independently responsible beyond what obesity alone would cause.

E — Sleep fragmentation does disrupt HPA axis regulation and causes modest cortisol dysregulation — but glucocorticoid-mediated hypertension from sleep fragmentation alone is a minor contributor and not the primary established mechanism. Cushing's syndrome produces hypertension via mineralocorticoid receptor activation and Na⁺ retention — the OSA mechanism is primarily neurogenic, not hormonal.

Question 1 — Cardiac Physiology (Frank-Starling Mechanism)

Vignette:

A 68-year-old retired female schoolteacher presents to the cardiology clinic with a 3-month history of progressive dyspnea on exertion, 2-pillow orthopnea, and bilateral ankle swelling. She has a 15-year history of poorly controlled hypertension. Her current medications include amlodipine 10 mg daily.

Physical Examination:



Vital Signs: BP 158/96 mmHg | HR 88/min, irregularly irregular | RR 20/min | Temp 37.1°C | SpO₂ 93% on room air

General: Mild respiratory distress at rest

Neck: Jugular venous distension at 45°

Lungs: Bilateral basilar crackles that do not clear with coughing

Cardiovascular: Irregularly irregular rhythm; S3 gallop heard at the apex; no murmurs

Extremities: 2+ pitting edema to the level of the knees bilaterally

Laboratory Results:

TestResultReference RangeBNP620 pg/mL< 100Serum Na⁺138 mEq/L136–145Serum K⁺4.1 mEq/L3.5–5.0Creatinine1.3 mg/dL0.6–1.2Echocardiogram EF30%55–70%Chest X-rayCardiomegaly, Kerley B lines—

Question:

Which of the following best explains why this patient's stroke volume remains reduced despite an elevated preload?



A. Decreased venous return due to systemic arterial vasodilation

B. Reduced myocardial contractility shifting the Frank-Starling curve downward

C. Increased afterload from peripheral vasoconstriction as the sole mechanism

D. Excessive bradycardia reducing ventricular filling time

E. Mitral regurgitation causing retrograde flow into the left atrium

Correct Answer: B

Reasoning (B): This is heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). The fundamental defect is intrinsic myocardial contractility failure. On the Frank-Starling curve, the failing ventricle operates on a depressed and flattened curve — even at elevated end-diastolic volumes (high preload, evidenced by JVD, S3 gallop, and pulmonary edema), the ventricle cannot generate adequate stroke volume. BNP >400 pg/mL reflects neurohormonal activation (RAAS + SNS) driven by reduced effective cardiac output. The S3 gallop arises from rapid deceleration of blood entering a dilated, poorly compliant ventricle during early diastole. This is a direct application of Guyton & Hall Chapter 10 and Costanzo's discussion of the Frank-Starling law in pathological states.

Incorrect Answer Breakdown:

A: Decreased venous return would reduce preload, not elevate it. This patient has overtly elevated preload (JVD, edema, S3), which is the opposite physiology. Decreased venous return causing low SV is characteristic of distributive/hypovolemic shock or severe dehydration, not HFrEF.

C: Increased afterload alone (e.g., hypertensive crisis) does reduce stroke volume, but an EF of 30% with structural chamber dilation indicates intrinsic myopathic failure — not isolated pressure overload. Afterload excess does contribute to the pathophysiology of HFrEF but is not the primary mechanism of reduced SV once the curve is depressed.

D: Her heart rate is 88/min — a compensatory tachycardia, not bradycardia. Bradycardia reducing SV would be the mechanism in complete heart block (Stokes-Adams syndrome) or iatrogenic beta-blocker overdose, not in this clinical picture.

E: Mitral regurgitation causes reduced forward SV due to retrograde flow, but it produces a holosystolic blowing murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla. No murmur is described here. MR can develop as a secondary complication of annular dilation in HFrEF, but is not the mechanism being tested in this question.

Question:

Which of the following best describes the primary acid-base disturbance and its mechanism in this patient?



A. Primary metabolic acidosis with appropriate respiratory compensation via Kussmaul breathing

B. Primary respiratory alkalosis with appropriate renal compensation producing secondary metabolic acidosis

C. Mixed primary respiratory alkalosis and primary metabolic acidosis occurring simultaneously

D. Primary metabolic alkalosis from vomiting-induced HCl loss with compensatory hypoventilation

E. Pure respiratory acidosis from salicylate-mediated CNS depression and CO₂ retention

Correct Answer: C

Reasoning (C): Salicylate toxicity is the only classic toxidrome that produces a true simultaneous mixed primary acid-base disorder. Two distinct mechanisms operate concurrently: (1) Salicylates directly stimulate the medullary respiratory center → primary respiratory alkalosis (PaCO₂ = 18 mmHg, driving pH up); (2) Salicylates uncouple oxidative phosphorylation and accumulate organic acids (lactic acid, keto acids) → primary metabolic acidosis (HCO₃⁻ = 12 mEq/L, driving pH down). The near-normal but slightly alkalemic pH (7.46) reflects two opposing primary processes nearly canceling each other out. Hypokalemia results from alkalemia-driven intracellular K⁺ shift. This is explicitly covered in Costanzo BRS Physiology Chapter 7 and Guyton & Hall Chapter 31.

Incorrect Answer Breakdown:

A: Pure primary metabolic acidosis with respiratory compensation would show a low pH (e.g., 7.20–7.30) with a compensatory reduction in PaCO₂. Using Winter's formula: expected PaCO₂ = (1.5 × 12) + 8 ± 2 = 26 mmHg. The measured PaCO₂ of 18 mmHg is far below this expected compensation, confirming that a co-existing independent primary respiratory alkalosis is present — not just compensation.

B: If respiratory alkalosis were the sole primary disorder with appropriate chronic renal compensation, the expected HCO₃⁻ would fall by approximately 5 mEq/L per 10 mmHg drop in PaCO₂ (chronic) → HCO₃⁻ ≈ 22 − 13.5 = ~18–19 mEq/L. An HCO₃⁻ of 12 mEq/L is far below expected renal compensation, proving a co-existing primary metabolic acidosis — not a simple compensatory response.

D: Primary metabolic alkalosis requires elevated HCO₃⁻ and alkalemic pH with elevated PaCO₂ as compensation. This patient has low HCO₃⁻ (12 mEq/L) and low PaCO₂, making metabolic alkalosis physiologically impossible here. Metabolic alkalosis from vomiting (HCl loss) is the correct context for that answer choice.
