# Top 10 Medical Claim Denial Codes in 2026 (CARC/RARC) & How to Fix Them Fast

Insurance denied your claim? You’re not alone. Claim denial rates continue to rise, with many patients and providers losing money on preventable issues. Understanding **CARC** (Claim Adjustment Reason Codes) and **RARC** (Remittance Advice Remark Codes) is the first step to fighting back.

Here are the top 10 most common denial codes patients and billers see in 2026, along with clear explanations and actionable fixes.

![Common medical claim denial codes and paperwork](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1450101499163-c8848c66ca85?w=1200&q=80&auto=format&fit=crop)

## Top 10 Common Denial Codes Explained

### 1. CO-11 – Diagnosis inconsistent with procedure
**Why it happens:** The insurance company’s automated system doesn’t see a "medical link" between the diagnosis (ICD-10) and the treatment (CPT). For example, if a doctor bills for a foot X-ray but the diagnosis code is for a headache.
**How to fix/appeal:** Review the claim for typos. Ensure the primary ICD-10 diagnosis code supports the medical necessity of the CPT code. If correct, submit clinical notes that prove the treatment was relevant.

### 2. CO-16 – Claim lacks information or has billing errors
**Why it happens:** This is the "incomplete homework" denial. It could be a missing subscriber ID, an incorrect birth date, or a missing modifier.
**How to fix/appeal:** Most of these are simple clerical errors. Reach out to your provider's billing office and ask them to verify patient demographics and resubmit a "Corrected Claim."

### 3. CO-97 – Service already adjudicated / bundled
**Why it happens:** The insurer believes the procedure is already covered by another payment. This often happens with surgical "global periods" or when minor services (like a blood draw) are considered part of a larger office visit.
**How to fix/appeal:** Check if the service was truly secondary. Use our [Medical Bill Audit tool](https://www.medihelp.live/audit) to see if the unbundling was inappropriate. If the services were distinct, appeal with a modifier (like -59) to show they were separate.

### 4. CO-4 – Modifier issues
**Why it happens:** Modifiers (the two-digit codes like -25 or -51) provide extra context. If they are missing or used incorrectly, the claim is auto-denied.
**How to fix/appeal:** Common fixes involve adding Modifier -25 for significant, separately identifiable evaluation and management services during a procedure, or Modifier -59 for distinct procedural services.

### 5. PR-22 / CO-18 – Duplicate claim or timely filing issues
**Why it happens:** Either the claim was sent twice by mistake, or it was sent after the "timely filing" deadline (often 90–180 days).
**How to fix/appeal:** For duplicates, simply ignore the second entry. For timely filing, provide proof of original submission (if available) or appeal based on "extenuating circumstances" like changed insurance information.

### 6. CO-29 – Prior authorization missing
**Why it happens:** Many surgeries and advanced imaging (MRIs) require a "hall pass" from your insurance before the procedure happens. If your doctor didn't get one, the claim is denied.
**How to fix/appeal:** Ask your provider to request a "Retroactive Authorization." If that fails, appeal by showing that the procedure was an emergency or that the necessity was undeniable.

### 7. CO-15 – Authorization number invalid/missing
**Why it happens:** An authorization was obtained, but the number on the claim is wrong or missing.
**How to fix/appeal:** Contact your doctor to get the correct authorization number and have them resubmit the claim with the number in Box 23 of the CMS-1500 form.

### 8. CO-45 – Contractual adjustment
**Why it happens:** This isn't strictly a "denial," but it shows the difference between the doctor's sticker price and the insurance's "allowed amount."
**How to fix/appeal:** Ensure you aren't being "balance billed" for this amount. If you are in-network, the provider must legally write off this CO-45 amount.

### 9. CO-50 – Medical necessity not established
**Why it happens:** The insurer doesn't think the treatment was the standard of care for your condition.
**How to fix/appeal:** This requires the most effort. You must gather a letter of medical necessity from your doctor, clinical peer-reviewed studies (if applicable), and your complete medical records to prove why the treatment was vital.

### 10. CO-59 – Processed based on multiple or concurrent procedures
**Why it happens:** When multiple procedures are done in one day, insurance often reduces the payment for the second and third items.
**How to fix/appeal:** Verify if the "Multiple Procedure Payment Reduction" (MPPR) was applied correctly. If the procedures were in different anatomical areas, appeal for full payment using appropriate modifiers.

## Step-by-Step Appeal Process That Works

Many first-level appeals succeed when you provide the right evidence. Follow these steps:

1.  **Gather supporting documents:** Request copies of your medical records, doctor’s clinical notes, and any prior approvals.
2.  **Use the Decoding Tools:** Don't guess. Use our [Claim Denial Code Explainer](https://www.medihelp.live/claim-denial-codes) to see exactly what the CARC/RARC codes mean.
3.  **Write a clear appeal letter:** Reference the specific denial code, your policy number, and the date of service. Clearly state why the denial is an error.
4.  **Submit within the deadline:** Most payers require appeals within 60–180 days. Don't wait.
5.  **Track everything and follow up:** Note who you talk to at the insurance company and the date/time of every call.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the difference between CARC and RARC codes?**
CARC (Claim Adjustment Reason Codes) provide the primary reason why a claim was adjusted or denied. RARC (Remittance Advice Remark Codes) provide additional, more specific details that complement the CARC.

**How long does an insurance appeal take?**
Standard appeals usually take 30 to 60 days. Urgent or "expedited" appeals for life-threatening conditions are typically resolved within 72 hours.

**Can patients appeal denials themselves?**
Yes. While providers often handle the initial "corrected claims," patients have a legal right to file member appeals directly with their insurance carrier.
