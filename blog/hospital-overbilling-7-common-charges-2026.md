# Hospital Overbilling: The 7 Most Common Charges You're Probably Paying For (But Shouldn't Be)

You received a hospital bill. You paid it. But what if you just handed a hospital hundreds — or thousands — of extra dollars for charges that were completely wrong?

Studies show that **80% of hospital bills contain at least one error**. And these aren't always innocent typos. Some are the result of systematic billing practices that inflate costs at the patient's expense. In 2024 alone, hospital spending in the U.S. reached $1.63 trillion — growing 8.9% in a single year — and overbilling is one of the core drivers behind that number.

The good news: you have the right to review, question, and dispute every single line of your bill. You just need to know what to look for.

Here are the 7 most common hospital charges patients are overpaying — and exactly what to do about each one.

## 1. Duplicate Charges — Being Billed Twice for the Same Thing

This is the most common billing error and one of the easiest to spot if you know what to look for. A duplicate charge means you were billed two or more times for the same procedure, test, or supply.

It can happen because:
- A service was entered into the billing system more than once
- Two departments both submitted a charge for the same item
- A procedure performed on one day was mistakenly billed on two separate dates

**What to do:** Request a fully itemized bill — not the summary version. Go through every line item and look for the same CPT code appearing more than once without a clinical reason. ## 2. Upcoding — Being Charged for a More Expensive Service Than You Received

Upcoding is when a hospital or provider bills for a higher-level, more expensive service than the one you actually received. It's one of the most financially damaging forms of overbilling — and it's not always obvious.

For example:
- You had a brief, routine office visit but were billed for a comprehensive evaluation
- A standard ER visit was coded as a high-complexity case
- A simple wound was billed under a code for a complex surgical repair

Research has linked upcoding to **$14.6 billion in additional hospital payments** across Medicare, Medicaid, and private insurance. One analysis found that hospitals using AI-assisted billing software saw sharp spikes in patients coded with conditions far more complex than the care they actually received.

**What to do:** Look up the CPT codes on your bill using MediHelp's [CPT Code Lookup](https://www.medihelp.live/cpt-code-search). Compare what the code describes with what you remember receiving. If there's a mismatch, that's grounds for a formal dispute.

## 3. Unbundling — Splitting a Package Into Expensive Pieces

Some procedures and tests are meant to be billed together as a single bundled charge. Unbundling is when a hospital separates those components and bills each one individually — which is almost always more expensive for you.

For instance, a series of lab tests that should be billed as a panel at one price might be split into 8 individual test charges, each carrying its own fee.

The official name for these bundling rules is **NCCI (National Correct Coding Initiative) edits**, maintained by CMS. Violating them — either through error or intent — directly overcharges patients.

## 4. Phantom Services — Charges for Things That Never Happened

A phantom service charge is exactly what it sounds like: you're billed for a procedure, test, medication, or supply that was never actually provided. This can range from a single aspirin tablet to an entire diagnostic test.

Common phantom charges include:
- Operating room fees for procedures that happened in a clinic
- Physical therapy sessions that were scheduled but cancelled
- Lab tests that were ordered but not performed
- Medications listed on the bill that you never received

**What to do:** Compare your itemized bill against your own medical records. You have a legal right to request your complete records. If a charge doesn't match your records or memory of treatment, flag it immediately and request written clarification from the billing department.

## 5. Out-of-Network Surprise Charges — Same Hospital, Hidden Cost

You carefully chose an in-network hospital. So why is there an out-of-network charge on your bill?

This happens more often than most patients realize. A Yale study of over 8.9 million ER bills found that **22% of privately insured patients were treated by out-of-network doctors** — even when they went to an in-network hospital. More than 60% of hospitals outsource their ER physicians to staffing firms that often keep doctors deliberately out-of-network to charge higher rates.

The result: your hospital is in-network, but the anesthesiologist, radiologist, or ER physician who treated you is not — and their bills can be substantially higher.

**What to do:** Review your Explanation of Benefits (EOB) carefully for any out-of-network designations. The No Surprises Act (passed in 2020) protects you from most unexpected out-of-network charges for emergency services. If you were charged out-of-network rates for emergency care, you can file a dispute with your insurer citing this law. ## 6. Facility Fees — The Hidden Tax on Hospital-Owned Clinics

Here's a charge many patients have never heard of — and yet it can add hundreds of dollars to a routine visit.

When a doctor's office or outpatient clinic is owned by a hospital, the hospital can add a "facility fee" on top of the physician's fee — simply because the visit happened on hospital-affiliated property. You might see your regular doctor at what looks like a normal medical office, but because that office was acquired by a hospital system, you get hit with an additional facility charge.

These fees are rarely disclosed upfront and often appear as a separate line item on your bill that looks like a hospital charge even though you never set foot in a hospital.

**What to do:** Before any appointment, ask whether the facility is hospital-owned and whether you'll be subject to facility fees. If you see a facility fee on a bill for an outpatient visit, ask the billing department to justify it and verify whether your insurer covers it.

## 7. Incorrect Patient or Insurance Information — Errors That Cost You

Not all overbilling is intentional. Sometimes, simple data entry errors cause real financial damage. A wrong date of birth, a transposed insurance ID number, or an incorrect diagnosis code can result in:
- Claims being denied that should have been covered
- Patients being billed for the full amount when insurance should have paid
- Procedures being assigned to the wrong insurance plan

With nearly 70,000 diagnosis codes and over 71,000 procedure codes in use, even a single digit error can change everything about how a claim is processed.

**What to do:** Every time you receive a bill or EOB, verify your name, date of birth, insurance ID, and the date of service. Even small discrepancies are worth flagging before you pay a single dollar.
