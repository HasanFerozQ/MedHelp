# FDA Approves Rybrevant Faspro for Head and Neck Cancer

On November 5, 2026, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) granted accelerated approval to **Rybrevant Faspro (amivantamab and hyaluronidase-lpuj)** for adults with recurrent or metastatic head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) whose cancer has progressed after platinum-based chemotherapy and immunotherapy. It is the first new drug approved for this group of patients in ten years — and the first therapy to attack two cancer-driving proteins at the same time.

<div class="key-takeaway">
  <strong>🔑 Key Takeaway</strong>
  Rybrevant Faspro is the first dual-targeted therapy — blocking both EGFR and MET — approved for recurrent or metastatic head and neck cancer. In the OrigAMI-4 trial, 42% of patients who had already failed chemotherapy and immunotherapy saw their tumors shrink or disappear, more than double the response rate of currently approved therapies.

## Why Head and Neck Cancer Has Been So Hard to Treat

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma covers cancers of the mouth, throat, voice box, and sinuses. When the disease comes back or spreads after initial treatment, options narrow fast. The standard path — platinum-based chemotherapy, then immunotherapy that blocks the PD-(L)1 pathway — helps some patients, but once both have stopped working, there has been almost nothing left to offer. According to the FDA, currently approved therapies in this setting are associated with an overall response rate of around 20% or less, with poor outcomes overall.

That is why this approval matters beyond the headline: a full decade passed without a single new drug for patients with recurrent or metastatic HNSCC who had already received platinum chemotherapy — a long wait for a disease the FDA describes as aggressive and life-threatening, with a high unmet need for effective therapies.

## What Is Rybrevant Faspro? How Does It Work?

Rybrevant Faspro is a **bispecific antibody** — a single drug engineered to hit two targets at once. Its active ingredient, amivantamab, blocks two proteins that head and neck cancers use to grow and survive: **EGFR (epidermal growth factor receptor)**, a growth-signal receptor overexpressed in most head and neck squamous cell carcinomas, and **MET (mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor)**, a second receptor tumors use as an escape route when EGFR alone is blocked. Hitting both at once closes off that backup path.

The "Faspro" part of the name refers to the formulation. The drug combines amivantamab with **hyaluronidase-lpuj**, an enzyme that temporarily loosens the tissue under the skin so the drug can be given as a **subcutaneous injection** — a shot under the skin — rather than a lengthy intravenous infusion. For patients, that can mean a shorter clinic visit and no infusion chair. The approved strength is 160 mg/2,000 units per mL, given as a single agent. The drug was developed by Janssen Biotech, Inc. (Johnson & Johnson), which received Breakthrough Therapy designation in February 2026 and Priority Review in July 2026.

## The OrigAMI-4 Trial — Breaking Down the Data

The approval rests on Cohort 1 of the **OrigAMI-4** trial, an open-label, multicenter Phase 1b/2 study of **102 adults** with recurrent or metastatic HNSCC whose disease had progressed after platinum-based chemotherapy and anti–PD-(L)1 immunotherapy. Patients with HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancer were excluded. The data were presented at the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) Annual Meeting and published in the *Journal of Clinical Oncology* (Burtness et al., 2026;44(24):2271–2277).

**Key results at a glance:**

- **Overall response rate:** 42% (95% confidence interval, 32–52%) — tumors shrank or disappeared, per blinded independent central review
- **Complete responses:** 15% of patients — tumors disappeared entirely
- **Partial responses:** 28% of patients — their tumors shrank measurably
- **Durability:** 56% of patients who responded were still responding six months or longer
- **The comparison:** currently approved therapies in this setting are associated with a response rate of around 20% or less — so Rybrevant Faspro more than doubled the share of patients who responded

> *"Today's approval represents a meaningful step forward for patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, a disease where treatment options have historically been limited and outcomes remain poor. This therapy offers patients and their physicians a new option with the potential to make a real difference in people's lives."* — Angelo De Claro, M.D., director of the FDA's Oncology Center of Excellence

## What This Means for Patients

**Who can receive it?** Adults with recurrent or metastatic HNSCC whose cancer has progressed on or after platinum-based chemotherapy and immunotherapy. It is **not** indicated for HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancer — a distinct subgroup with different biology and a better outlook.

**What should patients ask their oncologist?** Whether they fall in the approved group, what side effects to expect, and how the subcutaneous injection fits into their visit schedule. The prescribing information carries warnings for hypersensitivity and administration-related reactions, interstitial lung disease (ILD)/pneumonitis, skin reactions, eye toxicity, and harm to an unborn baby — so report rashes, breathing changes, or eye symptoms promptly.

**What about cost?** New cancer therapies are among the most expensive drugs in American medicine, and a new approval is often followed by a large bill — sometimes before insurance authorization is sorted out. **What comes next?** This is an **accelerated approval**, granted on response rate and duration of response — not yet on proof of longer survival. The Phase 3 OrigAMI-5 trial is already underway, testing Rybrevant Faspro with carboplatin and pembrolizumab as a first-line treatment; if it confirms a real clinical benefit, the approval stands, and if not, it can be withdrawn.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is Rybrevant Faspro?**
A subcutaneous cancer drug combining amivantamab, a bispecific antibody targeting EGFR and MET, with hyaluronidase to allow injection under the skin. It received FDA accelerated approval on November 5, 2026.

**Who is it for?**
Adults with recurrent or metastatic head and neck squamous cell carcinoma whose disease progressed on or after platinum-based chemotherapy and immunotherapy. It is not indicated for HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancer.

**How well does it work?**
In the OrigAMI-4 trial of 102 patients, 42% saw their tumors shrink or disappear, including complete responses in 15%. More than half of responders were still responding after six months — versus around 20% or fewer for currently approved therapies.

**Is it a cure?**
No. It is a targeted therapy that can shrink tumors and extend the time before the disease progresses. Whether it improves overall survival is still being tested in the confirmatory Phase 3 OrigAMI-5 trial.

**What are the main side effects?**
Per the FDA prescribing information: hypersensitivity and administration-related reactions, interstitial lung disease/pneumonitis, skin reactions, eye toxicity, and potential harm to an unborn baby. Patients should discuss the full risk profile with their oncologist.

**Is it available now?**
The FDA granted approval on November 5, 2026, so U.S. oncologists can now prescribe it for the approved indication. Availability at a specific clinic depends on that clinic's formulary and the patient's insurance authorization.
