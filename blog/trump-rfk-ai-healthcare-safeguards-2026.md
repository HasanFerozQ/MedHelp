# Trump & RFK Jr. Want to Strip AI Healthcare Safeguards — Here's What's at Stake for Patients

**The Trump administration and HHS Secretary RFK Jr. are proposing to gut regulations that protect patients from faulty, opaque AI healthcare tools — and medical safety experts are sounding the alarm.**

If you've visited a doctor recently, there's a good chance an AI was in the room with you — silently transcribing your conversation, summarizing your symptoms, or flagging your lab results. AI healthcare tools are now deeply embedded in American medicine. And for years, federal regulations have existed to make sure those tools actually work, are transparent, and don't put you in danger.

That may be about to change.

  <strong>⚠️ Patient Safety Alert</strong>
  Proposed HHS deregulation would eliminate user-centered design testing and AI transparency requirements, potentially exposing patients to untested, error-prone clinical algorithms.

## What the Trump Administration Is Proposing

The Office of the National Coordinator for Health IT (ONC) — the federal body that regulates electronic health records (EHRs) — has put forward proposed rules that would dramatically roll back oversight of AI-powered healthcare software.

Specifically, the proposals would:

- **Eliminate "user-centered design" testing requirements** — meaning AI tools no longer have to be tested on real doctors and nurses before deployment in clinical settings
- **Remove transparency obligations** — developers would no longer be required to disclose how AI systems make decisions, leaving clinicians in the dark about what's driving a recommendation
- **Scrap regulations keeping medical records secure and understandable** — weakening standards governing how data is formatted and transmitted between systems
- **Strip privacy protections** on sensitive medical data that current rules safeguard

The administration's stated rationale: giving clinicians "more health IT choices to meet their needs through increased competition." Public comment on the proposal closed in February 2026. HHS has declined to comment further, noting the rule is still moving through the regulatory process.

---

## Why Doctors and Safety Experts Are Worried

Dr. Raj Ratwani, a leading health IT safety researcher, warns that weakening these rules could have direct consequences for patients. An EHR that uses opaque AI — without any obligation to explain its reasoning — could produce records that are confusing or outright wrong, and a confused or incorrect record can lead to medication errors, missed diagnoses, and delayed treatment.

The concern isn't theoretical. Consider what's already happening on the ground even *with* the current safeguards in place.

Paul Boyer, a psychotherapist at Kaiser Permanente in Oakland, California, is one of thousands of clinicians now using AI scribe tools. His health system rolled out software from Abridge — one of the leading AI documentation companies — intended to summarize patient visits automatically. For many clinicians, these tools are a genuine relief: less paperwork, more time with patients.

But Boyer's experience tells a more complicated story. The AI scribe, he says, is "not super useful." His team ends up correcting the AI's notes regularly. More critically, the software is "not good at picking up on clinical nuance, at picking up on the emotional tone" — factors that are *especially* critical in mental health care, where what a patient *doesn't* say can matter just as much as what they do.

Now imagine removing the requirements that would force companies like Abridge to test their products on real clinicians like Boyer *before* deploying them at scale — or that would require the AI to show its work so doctors can catch when it's wrong.

## What's Actually Being Eliminated — In Plain English

Here's a simple breakdown of the safeguards on the chopping block:

| What's Being Removed | What It Currently Does | The Risk |
|---|---|---|
| User-centered design testing | Ensures AI tools are tested with real clinical users before launch | Untested tools reach patients first |
| AI transparency requirements | Forces vendors to disclose how AI reaches decisions | Doctors can't catch AI errors they can't see |
| Medical record security standards | Keeps records in standardized, protected formats | Data breaches and interoperability failures |
| Sensitive data privacy rules | Protects mental health, reproductive, and other sensitive records | Exposure of your most private health information |

## The Broader Context: Deregulation + Defunding

This proposal doesn't exist in a vacuum. It's part of a broader push by the Trump administration and DOGE-driven cost-cutting to reduce oversight across federal agencies — including health agencies.

At the same time, the administration has been:
- Withholding $1.3 billion in Medicaid reimbursements from California
- Threatening to pull Medicaid funding from all 50 states
- Demanding confidential transgender patient records from hospitals

The pattern critics see: systematically weakening the infrastructure of regulated, accountable healthcare — while AI adoption in hospitals accelerates at record speed, driven partly by financial pressure from those same Medicaid cuts.

## What Supporters of the Change Say

The administration and some healthcare technology advocates argue that existing regulations are too rigid and slow innovation. Their case:

- Current rules create unnecessary compliance burdens for small AI developers trying to enter the market
- Increased competition could drive down the cost of AI tools, making them more accessible to smaller clinics and rural hospitals
- Physicians should have more freedom to choose the tools that work best for their practice without being constrained by federal mandates

There's a legitimate debate here. Over-regulation *can* stifle beneficial innovation, and healthcare AI genuinely has the potential to reduce physician burnout, improve diagnostic accuracy, and cut administrative waste.

The question safety experts are asking: is the answer to strip *all* the safeguards, or to reform them intelligently?

## What This Means for Patients

If these rules go through in their current form, the average patient should be aware that:

1. **AI tools used in your care may not have been tested** on anyone like your doctor before being deployed
2. **Your doctor may not know when the AI is wrong** — because transparency requirements would be gone
3. **Your sensitive health data** — mental health records, reproductive health history — could be less protected than it is today
4. **Medical errors linked to AI confusion** could increase, with less regulatory recourse

## The Bottom Line

AI in healthcare is not going away — and it shouldn't. Used correctly, with proper guardrails, it has genuine potential to make medicine faster, smarter, and more accessible. But removing the very safeguards designed to catch AI failures before they reach patients is a different proposition entirely.

The rules being proposed aren't red tape for the sake of red tape. They were built in response to real documented cases of health IT failures causing harm. Rolling them back — in an environment where AI adoption is accelerating faster than clinical evidence can keep up — is a bet that the industry will self-regulate effectively.

History suggests that's a bet patients can't afford to lose.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the ONC and why does it matter?**  
The Office of the National Coordinator for Health IT (ONC) is the federal body under HHS that regulates electronic health records and health information technology. It sets the standards that govern how your medical records are stored, shared, and protected.

**Are AI scribes and EHR tools currently FDA-regulated?**  
Many AI tools used in clinical settings fall under FDA oversight as medical devices, but software that is purely administrative (like note-taking scribes) often falls under ONC jurisdiction instead. The proposed rules specifically target ONC oversight requirements.

**When would these rules take effect?**  
The proposal is still working through the regulatory process as of May 2026. Public comment closed in February. Final rulemaking timeline is not yet confirmed.

**Can my doctor opt out of AI tools?**  
Yes. Individual physicians and practices can choose not to use specific AI tools. However, when health systems make system-wide AI deployments, individual clinicians may have limited ability to opt out.

**Where can I learn more or submit a public comment?**  
Visit regulations.gov and search for ONC proposed rulemaking to track the proposal and any future comment periods.
