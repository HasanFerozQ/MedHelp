# How to Spot a Fake Medical Bill: 12 Warning Signs and What to Do

Receiving a medical bill can be stressful enough without wondering whether the bill itself is legitimate.

Medical billing problems can range from simple clerical mistakes to duplicate charges, incorrect billing codes, services you never received, and, in some cases, actual medical identity theft or fraud. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) warns that medical identity theft can involve someone using your personal or insurance information to obtain medical care or submit fraudulent claims.

The good news is that you don't have to simply assume a medical bill is correct.

By comparing your bill with your medical records and your health insurance Explanation of Benefits (EOB), you can identify many common warning signs. ## Fake vs. Incorrect vs. Unexpected Medical Bills

Before examining a bill, it is important to understand the difference between three situations.

### 1. A fraudulent or fake medical bill

This may involve charges for healthcare services you never received, use of your insurance information without authorization, or a billing document designed to obtain money or personal information fraudulently.

### 2. An incorrect medical bill

A legitimate healthcare provider can still send you a bill containing errors. Examples include duplicate charges, incorrect billing codes, incorrect dates, or charges for services that aren't documented in your medical records.

CMS specifically recommends checking medical bills for duplicate charges, services that weren't received, and discrepancies between the bill and medical records.

### 3. A legitimate but unexpectedly expensive bill

A bill can also be completely legitimate while being much higher than you expected. For example, you may receive an out-of-network charge or a bill that involves deductible or coinsurance amounts.

The CFPB notes that unexpected medical bills can occur when patients receive care from out-of-network providers, while the federal No Surprises Act provides protections for certain types of unexpected out-of-network bills.

So, **an expensive bill isn't automatically a fake bill.**

The key is verification.

## 12 Warning Signs of a Fake or Incorrect Medical Bill

## 1. You don't recognize the provider

Look at the provider or facility name on the bill.

If you don't recognize it, don't immediately assume it's fraudulent. Hospitals and healthcare systems may use separate physicians, laboratories, radiologists, anesthesiologists, ambulance companies, or other medical groups that bill independently.

CMS recommends checking the provider or facility name and contacting the billing office if the name doesn't match the provider you expected.

**What to do:** Contact the provider using a phone number obtained from a trusted source — not necessarily a number provided in a suspicious email or text message — and ask why the provider appears on your bill.

---

## 2. You were billed for a service you never received

This is one of the most important warning signs.

Check each major service against what actually happened during your appointment, procedure, hospital stay, or emergency visit.

For example, a bill might list:

- A laboratory test you don't remember having
- A procedure you never received
- Medical equipment that was never delivered
- A consultation you never had
- A medication you never received

CMS advises patients to compare their bills with their medical records and says you shouldn't be billed for services that aren't documented in your records.

The HHS Office of Inspector General (OIG) also identifies billing for services that were not actually provided as an example of improper healthcare billing.

**What to do:** Request an itemized statement and compare every line with your records. MediHelp's [Bill Audit Tool](https://www.medihelp.live/medical-bill-audit) can help you catch charges that don't belong on the bill.

## 3. The same service appears more than once

Duplicate billing doesn't necessarily mean someone intentionally committed fraud. Billing systems can contain errors, particularly when multiple providers or departments are involved.

However, duplicate charges should be investigated.

For example, your bill might contain two charges for:

**CBC Blood Test — $125**

**CBC Blood Test — $125**

If you only received the test once, ask the billing department to explain the duplicate entry.

CMS specifically recommends checking medical bills for duplicate charges. MediHelp's audit tool automatically flags duplicate CPT codes in real time.

## 4. The date of service is wrong

Check the dates carefully.

A bill may contain:

- A date when you weren't at the facility
- A duplicate date
- A procedure date that doesn't match your records
- Charges from a period before or after your actual treatment

A date discrepancy doesn't automatically prove fraud. Administrative errors happen.

But if you don't recognize the date or the service associated with it, ask the provider for documentation.

Your insurance EOB can also help because it normally identifies the date of service and description of the service billed.

## 5. The medical billing code doesn't match the service

Medical bills often contain CPT, HCPCS, or other billing codes.

These codes communicate what healthcare service or item was billed.

A suspicious discrepancy might occur when the code appears to represent a substantially different service from what you actually received.

One example is **upcoding**.

HHS-OIG describes upcoding as using a billing code that represents a more severe condition or more expensive service than the one actually provided.

CMS recommends looking up medical billing codes and comparing their descriptions with the care you actually received.

**Important:** Don't assume that an unfamiliar code is fraudulent.

Medical coding is complicated, and some services may have technical descriptions that don't sound like the procedure a patient remembers.

A code should be investigated rather than automatically labeled fraudulent.

## 6. Your bill doesn't match your Explanation of Benefits

If you have health insurance, your **Explanation of Benefits (EOB)** is one of the most useful documents for checking a medical bill.

An EOB generally shows:

- Provider
- Date of service
- Service description
- Provider charges
- Allowed charges
- Amount paid by insurance
- Your patient responsibility
- Claim information

CMS explains that an EOB is **not itself a bill**. It explains how the health plan processed your claim and what amount the patient may owe.

After receiving your provider bill, compare it with the EOB.

For example:

**EOB: Patient responsibility — $250**

**Provider bill: Balance due — $850**

That discrepancy needs to be investigated.

CMS specifically advises patients to compare the amount on the medical bill with the amount shown as their responsibility on the EOB.

## 7. You receive a bill for someone else's treatment

This can be a particularly serious warning sign.

If you receive a bill containing medical services, medications, or procedures that clearly belong to someone else, contact the provider and your insurance company.

It may be an administrative mistake.

However, it can also be associated with **medical identity theft**.

According to the FTC, medical identity theft occurs when someone uses another person's personal or insurance information to obtain medical services, prescriptions, medical devices, or other healthcare benefits.

Warning signs can include:

- Bills for healthcare you didn't receive
- EOBs showing unfamiliar treatment
- Debt collection notices for medical debt you don't recognize
- Insurance notifications about benefits you didn't use

---

## 8. You are being pressured to pay immediately

Be cautious if someone contacts you unexpectedly and demands immediate payment using unusual methods.

For example, a suspicious message might say:

> "Your medical account will be sent to collections today unless you pay immediately."

Scammers may also attempt to obtain your insurance information, Social Security number, credit-card details, or other personal information.

The FTC recommends not giving medical information to someone who unexpectedly contacts you by phone, email, or text. Instead, access your healthcare or insurance account through a website you know is legitimate or contact the organization using a trusted phone number.

**Never let urgency prevent verification.**

---

## 9. The payment instructions look suspicious

Examine how the bill asks you to pay.

Be cautious if an unexpected medical bill asks you to send money through an unusual payment method or to an individual rather than the healthcare provider or billing organization.

Instead of clicking a payment link in an unexpected email or text:

1. Find the healthcare provider's official website.
2. Locate its billing department.
3. Contact the provider using independently verified contact information.
4. Ask whether the bill and payment instructions are legitimate.

This is especially important if the message requests sensitive information.

## 10. The bill contains charges that don't make sense

You don't need to be a medical billing expert to identify charges that deserve an explanation.

For example, suppose you had a short office visit but the bill includes:

- Multiple procedures
- Several laboratory tests
- Expensive medical equipment
- Multiple consultation charges

That doesn't automatically mean the bill is fraudulent.

However, you have every right to ask the billing department for an itemized explanation.

CMS recommends requesting a detailed bill listing the costs for individual medical services and items.

---

## 11. You already paid, but the bill still shows the full balance

Check your receipts, credit-card statements, bank records, and previous payments.

A billing system may not have properly applied a payment.

For example:

**Original balance:** $1,000

**Payment already made:** $400

**Expected remaining balance:** $600

But your new statement might still show:

**Balance due: $1,000**

Don't pay the entire amount again without investigating.

Contact the provider's billing department and provide evidence of your previous payment.

---

## 12. Your insurance company says something completely different

Your insurer can be an important second source of information.

If your provider says:

> "Your insurance didn't pay anything."

but your EOB says the insurer paid $1,500, you should investigate the discrepancy before paying the amount requested.

Similarly, if the provider says you owe $2,000 but your EOB says your patient responsibility is $500, ask the provider to explain the difference.

CMS recommends contacting the health insurance company when you believe there is a billing error and comparing the bill with the EOB.

## A Simple 5-Step Medical Bill Verification Process

You don't have to investigate a medical bill blindly.

Use this process.

### Step 1: Request an itemized bill

If you received only a summary statement, ask the provider for a detailed, itemized bill.

You want to see exactly what you are being charged for.

### Step 2: Compare it with your medical records

Check:

- Date
- Provider
- Procedure
- Tests
- Medications
- Equipment
- Number of services

CMS recommends comparing the bill with your medical records.

### Step 3: Compare it with your EOB

Look for:

**Provider charge → Allowed amount → Insurance payment → Patient responsibility**

Your provider bill should generally correspond to the amount your insurance says you owe, subject to the circumstances of your particular plan and bill. CMS specifically advises comparing these amounts.

### Step 4: Investigate questionable billing codes

If a code doesn't appear to match the service you received, ask the provider to explain it.

Don't assume that an unfamiliar code means fraud.

👉 Use the [CPT Code Lookup](https://www.medihelp.live/cpt-code-search) first, then [audit the full bill](https://www.medihelp.live/medical-bill-audit) for duplicates and overcharges.

### Step 5: Contact the provider and insurer

Give them the exact discrepancy.

Instead of saying:

> "This bill is fake."

say:

> "I received a bill containing a charge for a service that I don't believe I received. Can you provide documentation showing when and how this service was provided?"

That creates a much clearer paper trail.

## What If the Bill Really Is Fraudulent?

If you discover evidence that someone used your personal or insurance information without authorization, take the issue seriously.

The FTC recommends obtaining and reviewing medical records, reporting errors to healthcare providers, checking credit reports, and using IdentityTheft.gov for an identity-theft recovery plan.

For suspected fraud involving Medicare or Medicaid, HHS-OIG provides resources for reporting suspected healthcare fraud. HHS-OIG also advises beneficiaries to review their Medicare Summary Notices or EOBs for unauthorized charges.

Keep copies of:

- The original bill
- EOB
- Medical records
- Receipts
- Emails
- Letters
- Payment confirmations
- Notes from phone calls
- Names of representatives you spoke with

Documentation can make it much easier to dispute an incorrect charge.

## Don't Confuse a High Bill With a Fake Bill

This is one of the most important points.

A medical bill can be **shockingly expensive and still be legitimate**.

For example, an emergency room visit can involve facility charges, physician charges, laboratory services, imaging, medications, and other providers.

Likewise, an out-of-network charge may be unexpected without necessarily being fraudulent.

The No Surprises Act provides federal protections for certain emergency services and certain out-of-network services received at in-network facilities, although the protections have specific eligibility rules and exceptions.

Therefore, the question shouldn't simply be:

**"Is this bill expensive?"**

Instead ask:

**"Does this bill accurately represent the healthcare I received, and does the amount I owe match how my insurance processed the claim?"**

## Medical Bill Red Flags Checklist

Before paying a medical bill, check:

- ☐ Do I recognize the provider?
- ☐ Was I actually treated on the listed date?
- ☐ Did I receive every service listed?
- ☐ Are any services listed more than once?
- ☐ Do the billing codes appear consistent with the services?
- ☐ Do my medical records support the charges?
- ☐ Did my insurance process this claim?
- ☐ Does the bill match my EOB?
- ☐ Does the amount listed as my responsibility match the amount I'm being asked to pay?
- ☐ Have I already paid some or all of this amount?
- ☐ Are the payment instructions legitimate?
- ☐ Did anyone contact me unexpectedly requesting insurance or personal information?

If you answer "no" or "I'm not sure" to any of these questions, **pause before paying and investigate the discrepancy.**

## Final Takeaway

A suspicious medical bill isn't necessarily a scam, and a legitimate medical bill can contain billing errors.

The safest approach is to **verify rather than guess**.

Start with an itemized bill. Compare it with your medical records and your insurance EOB. Look for duplicate charges, services you didn't receive, incorrect dates, questionable codes, unexplained balances, and unauthorized use of your insurance information.

If something doesn't match, contact the provider and your insurance company and request an explanation before making payment.
