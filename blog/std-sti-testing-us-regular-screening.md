# STD & STI Testing in the United States: Why Regular Screening Matters

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are common in the United States and can affect people of every age, background, relationship status, and sexual orientation. Many infections cause no noticeable symptoms. Someone can feel completely well and still have an infection that can be passed to a sexual partner.

That is one reason **STD/STI testing matters**. Testing is not only for people with symptoms. For many people, screening is part of preventive care: early diagnosis can help people get appropriate treatment, reduce transmission, and prevent complications.

The latest provisional CDC surveillance data for 2024 recorded more than **2.2 million reported cases of chlamydia, gonorrhea, and syphilis** in the United States. That was lower than the 2023 count, but the national burden remains substantial. Reported cases are only part of the picture: many infections are never diagnosed or reported.

## STI and STD: what do the terms mean?

The terms **STI** and **STD** are often used interchangeably, but they describe different things. An STI is an infection transmitted through sexual contact. An STD generally refers to an infection that has progressed to cause disease or symptoms. A person can have an infection, such as chlamydia, without symptoms and still transmit it. For that reason, many health organizations prefer the term STI.

STIs may be caused by bacteria, viruses, or parasites. Common examples include chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis, HIV, herpes simplex virus (HSV), human papillomavirus (HPV), trichomoniasis, and hepatitis B. Some bacterial STIs can be cured with treatment. Viral infections may not always be curable, but diagnosis and medical care can reduce complications and transmission risk.

These are reported cases of nationally notifiable infections, not estimates of every infection in the country. Some infections cause no symptoms, are never tested for, or are not reported to surveillance systems. The 2024 figures are provisional and may change when finalized.

Compared with 2023, reported chlamydia cases declined about 8%, gonorrhea about 10%, primary and secondary syphilis about 22%, and the combined count for chlamydia, gonorrhea, and syphilis about 9%. Congenital syphilis remains a serious concern. During pregnancy, untreated syphilis can harm the fetus, so prenatal screening and follow-up are important.

## Why STIs can go unnoticed

Many STIs can be present without pain, discharge, visible lesions, or urinary symptoms. **Feeling healthy does not reliably tell you whether you have an STI.** Screening can identify some infections before symptoms or complications develop.

### Chlamydia

Chlamydia is one of the most frequently reported bacterial STIs in the United States. It often causes no symptoms. Untreated infection can lead to pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), which may contribute to ectopic pregnancy, infertility, or chronic pelvic pain.

### Gonorrhea

Gonorrhea is caused by the bacterium *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*. It can infect the genitals, rectum, or throat, and may cause symptoms such as discharge or painful urination. Some infections cause few or no symptoms. Testing may need to cover the sites exposed: a urine or genital test alone may not detect an infection in the throat or rectum.

### Syphilis

Syphilis, caused by *Treponema pallidum*, progresses through stages. Symptoms may be subtle, may change over time, or may go unnoticed. Syphilis during pregnancy can pass to the fetus and lead to severe outcomes, including stillbirth or infant illness. CDC recommends screening during pregnancy, beginning at the first prenatal visit; additional screening depends on individual risk and local guidance.

## Who should consider testing?

There is no single testing schedule that fits everyone. The right tests and timing depend on factors such as age, sexual history, types of sexual contact, pregnancy, symptoms, previous STI diagnoses, partner diagnoses, and local recommendations.

CDC recommends that everyone ages 13 to 64 be tested for HIV at least once. More frequent testing may be appropriate depending on individual circumstances.

Examples of CDC screening recommendations include:

- Sexually active women younger than 25 should receive annual chlamydia and gonorrhea screening.
- Women 25 and older may need annual chlamydia and gonorrhea screening when risk factors are present, such as a new partner, multiple partners, or a partner with an STI.
- Sexually active men who have sex with men may need at least annual screening for chlamydia, gonorrhea, and syphilis. Testing every 3 to 6 months may be appropriate for some people at increased risk. Tests should cover sites based on sexual exposure.
- Pregnant patients should receive recommended screening because some infections can affect both the pregnant person and the baby.

These are general examples, not a personalized testing plan. A healthcare professional can help determine which recommendations apply to you.

## What does an STI test include?

There is no single test that checks for every STI. The test depends on the infection being considered and the site of possible exposure. Testing may include:

- **Urine samples** for infections such as chlamydia and gonorrhea.
- **Blood tests** for HIV, syphilis, and certain hepatitis infections. Herpes blood testing is used only in selected circumstances.
- **Vaginal or cervical swabs** for chlamydia, gonorrhea, trichomoniasis, or other infections as clinically appropriate.
- **Throat swabs** when oral exposure makes testing at that site appropriate, particularly for gonorrhea.
- **Rectal swabs** when receptive anal exposure makes testing at that site appropriate, including for chlamydia and gonorrhea.

Tell the healthcare professional what kinds of contact and body sites may have been exposed. That information helps them select appropriate tests; it is not a judgment about you.

## When should you get tested?

Consider contacting a healthcare professional about testing if you have symptoms, a partner has told you they have an STI, you have a new or multiple partners, you may have had an exposure, you are pregnant, or routine screening is recommended for you. You do not need to wait for symptoms to appear.

After a possible recent exposure, test timing matters. Different infections have different window periods, and an early negative result may not rule out infection. Ask a clinician or testing service whether and when repeat testing is appropriate. Seek medical advice promptly if you have symptoms or believe you may have been exposed.

## Testing after a new partner or within a relationship

A new relationship can be a good time for both partners to discuss sexual-health testing. You might talk about previous tests and their dates, any diagnoses, condom use, other partners, HIV testing, and vaccination against preventable infections such as HPV and hepatitis B.

Being in a monogamous relationship does not by itself establish either partner's STI status. Testing may still be relevant if neither partner was tested before the relationship, a previous diagnosis or exposure is involved, symptoms develop, pregnancy occurs, or a clinician recommends screening. A result describes infection status at the time of testing and should be interpreted in light of exposure and test window periods.

## Where can you get tested?

Testing may be available through primary-care offices, sexual-health clinics, community health centers, public-health departments, specialized STI clinics, and some testing programs. Confidential and free or low-cost services may be available locally. Check with the provider about confidentiality, cost, which infections are included, and how results and follow-up are handled.

Some at-home services offer self-testing or self-collected samples for particular infections. Not every advertised “STD panel” checks for every STI, and a test may not cover every exposed site or be appropriate for every situation. Before ordering, confirm what is tested, how specimens are processed, whether the test is authorized for its intended use, how results are delivered, and what clinical support is available if a result is positive or unclear.

## What can happen after a test?

A negative result may need to be interpreted alongside the date of possible exposure, symptoms, and the test's window period. A healthcare professional can advise whether repeat or additional testing is appropriate.

If a result is positive, follow up with a qualified healthcare professional about treatment or ongoing management. Depending on the infection, they may recommend partner notification, partner testing or treatment, temporary precautions, retesting, or additional tests. Do not self-treat based only on online information. Retesting after treatment is recommended for some infections because reinfection is common; CDC recommends retesting about three months after treatment for chlamydia and gonorrhea.

## Potential effects of untreated STIs

Complications vary by infection and person. Untreated chlamydia or gonorrhea can lead to PID and may damage reproductive organs, increasing the risk of infertility or ectopic pregnancy. Some infections can cause serious pregnancy or newborn complications. Certain bacterial STIs can increase the risk of acquiring or transmitting HIV, and some infections can affect multiple organs over time.

Early diagnosis gives a healthcare professional the opportunity to discuss available treatment, reduce the chance of passing an infection to others, and plan any needed follow-up.

## Testing is preventive care, not a judgment

STIs are infections, not a measure of someone's character, relationship, or worth. They can affect people across ages, communities, relationship statuses, and sexual orientations. Seeking testing is a practical way to get information about your health and make informed decisions with partners and healthcare professionals.

Prevention remains important. Depending on your circumstances, strategies may include using condoms correctly and consistently, discussing testing with partners, getting recommended HPV and hepatitis B vaccines, following screening guidance, seeking care for symptoms, and completing prescribed treatment.

## The bottom line

More than 2.2 million reported cases of chlamydia, gonorrhea, and syphilis were recorded in the United States in 2024. Although those reported counts declined from 2023, they do not include every infection, and many STIs can cause no noticeable symptoms.

**You cannot reliably determine your STI status by how you feel.** Talk with a healthcare professional about which tests are appropriate for you, when to take them, and how often to screen. Testing is about information, prevention, treatment, and protecting your health and the health of your partners.
