# FDA Approves Two New Myasthenia Gravis Treatments

For people living with myasthenia gravis, a rare autoimmune disease that causes muscles to weaken with use, November 2026 brought two new myasthenia gravis treatment options in a single week. On November 16, Alexion and AstraZeneca announced the FDA approval of **Klygefa (gefurulimab)**, a once-weekly injection patients give themselves at home. Two days later, Regeneron announced the FDA approval of **Livsayis (cemdisiran)**, the first siRNA therapy ever approved for the disease — given just four times a year. Both drugs are for adults with generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG) who test positive for acetylcholine receptor (AChR) antibodies, the most common form of the disease.

## What Is Myasthenia Gravis?

Myasthenia gravis is a chronic autoimmune disease of the neuromuscular junction — the place where a nerve tells a muscle to contract. In most patients, the immune system makes antibodies against the acetylcholine receptor (AChR) on the muscle side of that junction. Those antibodies activate complement, a branch of the immune system, which damages the muscle membrane and destroys receptors. The result is weakness that gets worse with use and improves with rest: drooping eyelids, double vision, difficulty speaking, chewing or swallowing, and weakness in the arms and legs. In severe cases, breathing muscles fail — a myasthenic crisis that needs emergency care.

## Klygefa: A Weekly Injection You Give Yourself

Klygefa (gefurulimab-gaks), from Alexion, AstraZeneca Rare Disease, is the first and only dual-binding nanobody C5 inhibitor approved for gMG. A nanobody is a small antibody fragment: this one binds C5 in the terminal complement cascade, and also binds serum albumin, which extends its half-life and makes once-weekly dosing possible. After a weight-based loading dose on day one, patients inject it themselves once a week with an autoinjector — no clinic visit, no infusion chair.

The approval was supported by the phase 3 PREVAIL trial (NCT05556096), published in *JAMA Neurology* in 2026. The trial enrolled 260 adults with AChR antibody–positive gMG across 113 sites in 20 countries, randomly assigned to gefurulimab or placebo for 26 weeks.

**Key results at a glance:**

- **Daily functioning:** on the Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily Living (MG-ADL) scale — which measures how the disease affects talking, chewing, swallowing, breathing and other daily tasks — scores improved by **4.2 points with Klygefa versus 2.6 with placebo** (treatment difference −1.6; 95% CI −2.4 to −0.8; P<.001).
- **Muscle strength:** on the Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis (QMG) test, a clinician-scored measure of muscle strength, scores improved by **4.5 points versus 2.4 with placebo** (difference −2.1; P<.001). Nearly half of Klygefa patients (46.3%) improved by 5 or more QMG points, compared with 25.2% on placebo.
- **Speed:** improvements in daily functioning were seen **as early as week one**, and benefits were sustained through week 26.

## Livsayis: Four Shots a Year — the First siRNA Therapy for MG

Livsayis (cemdisiran), from Regeneron, works on the same target from a different angle. It is a small interfering RNA (siRNA) therapy: instead of blocking the C5 protein after it is made, it degrades the messenger RNA that tells liver cells to produce C5 in the first place, lowering circulating C5 levels and shutting down terminal complement activation. It is the first siRNA therapy approved for gMG, Regeneron's first approved siRNA therapy, and its first neurology medicine. The molecule was discovered by Alnylam Pharmaceuticals.

The approval was based on the phase 3 NIMBLE trial (NCT05070858), published in *The Lancet* in April 2026 and described by Regeneron as the largest interventional trial in the disease to date. The trial enrolled 284 adults with AChR antibody–positive gMG at 86 sites in 13 countries.

**Key results at a glance:**

- **Daily functioning:** at week 24, MG-ADL scores improved by **4.5 points with cemdisiran 600 mg every 12 weeks, versus 2.2 with placebo** — a placebo-adjusted difference of −2.3 points (P=.0005).
- **Muscle strength:** QMG scores improved by **2.8 points more than placebo**.
- **Dosing:** one subcutaneous injection **every three months** — four times a year — the longest dosing interval of any approved MG treatment.

## Two Myasthenia Gravis Treatments, One Target

What makes this week remarkable is not just that two drugs were approved — it is that both attack the same protein, complement C5, by opposite routes. Klygefa grabs C5 in the bloodstream and blocks it. Livsayis stops the liver from making C5 at all. Both approaches end at the same place: less terminal complement activation, less damage at the neuromuscular junction, and stronger muscles.

For patients, the real difference is the routine. Klygefa is weekly, at home, by autoinjector. Livsayis is four injections a year, also under the skin. Compare that with older complement therapies that require regular clinic infusions, and the direction of travel is clear: less time in infusion chairs, more treatment at home. Which one suits a given patient depends on their disease, their other medicines, and what their neurologist recommends — the trials compared each drug with placebo, not with each other, so there is no head-to-head evidence saying one works better.

## What This Means for Patients

**Who can receive them?** Both drugs are approved only for **adults with AChR antibody–positive generalized myasthenia gravis**. That antibody test matters: if you have MG but test negative for AChR antibodies, these two drugs are not approved for you, and your neurologist will look at other options.

**The safety warning you must know about:** both drugs carry a **boxed warning for serious, life-threatening meningococcal infections**. Blocking complement C5 weakens the immune system's defence against *Neisseria meningitidis*, the bacteria that cause meningitis. Both drugs are available only through restricted REMS safety programmes, and patients must complete **meningococcal vaccination at least two weeks before the first dose**. If treatment cannot wait for vaccination, antibiotics are used as cover. This is not a minor formality — it is the single most important safety step with this whole class of drugs.

**What about cost?** Both are specialty biologics, the kind of drug that typically comes with prior authorization, high list prices and complex billing. Before starting, ask your insurer what your share will be, whether the drug is covered under the medical or pharmacy benefit, and what patient-assistance programmes the manufacturer offers. And when the bills arrive, check every line: infusion and administration charges, coding errors and duplicate charges are common with specialty drugs.     <h4>📚 MG Pathophysiology (Step 1)</h4>
    <p>AChR antibodies activate complement at the neuromuscular junction, destroying postsynaptic folds and reducing functional AChR density. The result is fatigable weakness — worse with repeated use, better with rest — classically ptosis and diplopia first, then bulbar and limb weakness. Think postsynaptic: the nerve releases acetylcholine normally, but the muscle cannot respond.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="lifestyle-item">
    <h4>📚 Complement and Neisseria (Step 1 / Step 2 CK)</h4>
    <p>Terminal complement (C5–C9, the membrane attack complex) is the key defence against encapsulated <em>Neisseria</em> species. Block C5 and you invite meningococcal infection — which is exactly why both drugs carry a boxed warning and require meningococcal vaccination before the first dose. The same logic explains why inherited terminal complement deficiency presents with recurrent meningococcal disease.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="lifestyle-item">
    <h4>📚 siRNA Mechanism (Step 1)</h4>
    <p>siRNA drugs like cemdisiran are short double-stranded RNAs that load into the RISC complex and slice a specific messenger RNA — here, the mRNA encoding C5 — so the protein is never made. Contrast with monoclonal antibodies, which bind a protein that already exists. Exams love this distinction: siRNA reduces production; antibodies block function.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="lifestyle-item">
    <h4>📚 Myasthenic vs. Cholinergic Crisis (Step 2 CK / Step 3)</h4>
    <p>Both cause worsening weakness, but the cause is opposite: myasthenic crisis is under-treatment (too little acetylcholine signalling), cholinergic crisis is over-treatment with a cholinesterase inhibitor such as pyridostigmine. Look for SLUDGE symptoms, miosis and bradycardia in cholinergic crisis — and remember that edrophonium, which improves myasthenic weakness, worsens cholinergic crisis.</p>

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is Klygefa?**
Gefurulimab (Klygefa), from Alexion/AstraZeneca, is a once-weekly, self-injected C5 inhibitor for adults with AChR antibody–positive generalized myasthenia gravis. The FDA approval was announced November 16, 2026, supported by the phase 3 PREVAIL trial.

**What is Livsayis?**
Cemdisiran (Livsayis), from Regeneron, is a C5-directed siRNA therapy given subcutaneously four times a year — the first siRNA therapy approved for myasthenia gravis. The FDA approval was announced November 18, 2026, supported by the phase 3 NIMBLE trial.

**How do the two drugs differ?**
Both target complement C5, but Klygefa binds the C5 protein in the blood, while Livsayis silences the mRNA that produces C5. Klygefa is injected weekly at home; Livsayis is injected once every three months.

**Why do both drugs need a meningitis vaccine first?**
Blocking C5 weakens the immune defence against meningococcal bacteria. Both drugs carry a boxed warning for serious meningococcal infections and require vaccination at least two weeks before the first dose, under a restricted REMS programme.

**Who can take these drugs?**
Adults with generalized myasthenia gravis who test positive for AChR antibodies. They are not approved for children, or for patients whose MG is driven by other antibodies.

**Are these drugs a cure?**
No. They control the disease by blocking complement attack on the neuromuscular junction, but they do not remove the underlying antibodies. Patients need ongoing treatment, and long-term data beyond the six-month trials are still being collected.
