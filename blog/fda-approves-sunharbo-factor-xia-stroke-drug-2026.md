# FDA Approves Sunharbo, First Factor XIa Stroke Drug

On November 9, 2026, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved **Sunharbo (asundexian)** — the first drug in a brand-new class of blood thinners called factor XIa (FXIa) inhibitors. Taken once a day alongside standard antiplatelet therapy, it prevents a second stroke in adults who have just had a mild-to-moderate stroke caused by a clot, or a high-risk "mini-stroke" (transient ischemic attack, or TIA). In the large trial behind the approval, it cut the risk of another ischemic stroke by 26% — without a significant increase in major bleeding.

<div class="key-takeaway">
  <strong>🔑 Key Takeaway</strong>
  Sunharbo is the first factor XIa inhibitor ever approved. By blocking factor XI — a clotting protein that drives harmful clots more than it drives normal bleeding control — it prevented repeat strokes in the OCEANIC-STROKE trial (ischemic stroke 6.2% vs. 8.4% on placebo) with major bleeding rates nearly identical to placebo (1.9% vs. 1.7%). It is not a replacement for blood thinners in atrial fibrillation.

## Why a Second Stroke Is the Real Danger

A first stroke is frightening enough. The danger that follows is quieter and often worse: despite current prevention, an estimated **5.1% of patients who have an ischemic stroke or TIA have a recurrent stroke within one year**, and approximately **22% of those who survive a minor stroke or TIA are dead or disabled five years later**. Recurrent strokes also tend to be more disabling — and more deadly — than first events.

Standard care after a clot-caused stroke is antiplatelet therapy (aspirin, clopidogrel). Dual antiplatelet therapy is recommended for 21 to 90 days after a minor stroke or TIA, but long-term use raises major bleeding risk without lowering stroke risk further. That gap — real residual risk, no safe way to push prevention further — is exactly what Sunharbo was designed to fill.

> *"Stroke is a leading cause of disability and death in the United States, and survivors are at risk of having another stroke. [This] approval represents a meaningful advance for patients and health care providers seeking a new option for secondary stroke prevention."* — Hylton V. Joffe, M.D., M.M.Sc., director of the FDA's Office of Cardiology, Hematology, Endocrinology, and Nephrology

## What Is Sunharbo? How Factor XIa Inhibition Works

Blood clotting runs on a cascade of proteins. Factor XI sits in the **intrinsic pathway**, one of the two arms that converge to form a clot. The key insight is that factor XI plays only a minor role in normal bleeding control (hemostasis) — but a major role in the pathological clots that cause strokes. People born with factor XI deficiency have a lower risk of ischemic stroke without an increase in bleeding inside the brain.

That makes factor XI an attractive target: block it, and you may prevent harmful clots while leaving normal bleeding control mostly intact. At the approved 50 mg daily dose, asundexian inhibits activated factor XI by more than 90%. Older blood thinners — warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban — block factors central to hemostasis, which is why bleeding is their constant trade-off. Sunharbo is the first approved drug to test the idea that you can uncouple clot prevention from bleeding risk.

## The OCEANIC-STROKE Trial — Breaking Down the Data

The approval rests on **OCEANIC-STROKE**, a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled phase 3 trial (NCT05686070) at 702 centers in 37 countries, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* (Sharma et al., 2026;394(15):1467–1479). Adults were enrolled within 72 hours of a noncardioembolic ischemic stroke with a stroke severity score (NIHSS) of 15 or lower, or a high-risk TIA (ABCD2 score of 6 or 7). Patients with atrial fibrillation or any other reason to need anticoagulation were excluded. Patients were assigned 1:1 to asundexian 50 mg once daily (n = 6,162) or placebo (n = 6,165), on top of planned single or dual antiplatelet therapy.

**Key results at a glance:**

- **Ischemic stroke (primary outcome):** 6.2% with asundexian vs. 8.4% with placebo (hazard ratio 0.74; 95% CI, 0.65–0.84; P < .001) — a 26% relative risk reduction, about 2.2 fewer strokes per 100 patients over two years
- **Any stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic):** 6.6% vs. 8.8% (HR 0.74)
- **Cardiovascular death, heart attack, or stroke:** 9.2% vs. 11.1% (HR 0.83)
- **Disabling or fatal stroke:** 2.1% vs. 3.0% (HR 0.69)
- **All-cause mortality:** similar in both groups (4.0% vs. 4.1%)

**Safety — the part that matters most for a blood thinner:** major bleeding occurred in 1.9% with asundexian vs. 1.7% with placebo (HR 1.10; 95% CI, 0.85–1.44) — no statistically significant increase. Major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding (5.5% vs. 5.0%), symptomatic bleeding inside the brain (0.7% vs. 0.6%), and hemorrhagic stroke (0.2% vs. 0.3%) were also similar. The FDA did flag two cautions: major bleeding was increased in patients older than 75 and during the first 90 days of treatment, and gastrointestinal bleeding was more common. Diarrhea was the most frequently reported side effect, in at least 3% of treated patients.

## What This Means for Patients

**Who can receive it?** Adults with a recent mild-to-moderate noncardioembolic ischemic stroke (a stroke caused by a clot that did not come from the heart) or a high-risk TIA, who are already taking antiplatelet therapy. It is **not** approved for patients with atrial fibrillation — a different kind of stroke risk that needs a different anticoagulant.

**How is it taken?** One 50 mg tablet, once a day, in addition to your antiplatelet medicine — not instead of it. Do not stop your aspirin or clopidogrel unless your doctor tells you to. Combined P-glycoprotein and strong CYP3A4 inducers such as carbamazepine are not recommended, because they can reduce its effectiveness.

**What about cost?** Bayer set the list price at **$748.50 for a 30-day supply** and expects to start shipping in November 2026. What a patient actually pays depends on insurance coverage and prior authorization. **What comes next?** Sunharbo was approved in China in late October 2026 and is under review in Europe and Japan. The open questions are real-world: how it performs outside a trial population (few trial patients had severe strokes, only about 5% enrolled after a TIA, and Black patients made up just 2.3% of participants), and whether insurers cover it quickly enough for patients to actually get it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is Sunharbo?**
A once-daily oral tablet (asundexian 50 mg) from Bayer — the first factor XIa inhibitor approved by the FDA, for preventing repeat strokes after a recent noncardioembolic ischemic stroke or high-risk TIA. It was approved on November 9, 2026.

**How well does it work?**
In the OCEANIC-STROKE trial of 12,327 patients, it reduced ischemic stroke by 26% relative to placebo (6.2% vs. 8.4%) over about two years, and also reduced disabling or fatal strokes (2.1% vs. 3.0%).

**Does it cause bleeding?**
Major bleeding was 1.9% with asundexian vs. 1.7% with placebo — no statistically significant increase overall. But the FDA noted higher major bleeding in patients over 75 and during the first 90 days, and more gastrointestinal bleeding, so those groups need closer monitoring.

**Can it replace my blood thinner if I have atrial fibrillation?**
No. Sunharbo is not approved for atrial fibrillation, and patients with AF were excluded from the trial. AF-related stroke prevention still requires standard anticoagulation.

**How much does it cost?**
The list price is $748.50 for a 30-day supply. Actual out-of-pocket cost depends on insurance coverage, prior authorization, and patient assistance programs.
