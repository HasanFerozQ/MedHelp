# Common Knee Surgery Found Ineffective After 10-Year Trial — And May Make Things Worse

For decades, if you tore your meniscus, surgery was the answer. Physicians routinely recommended a meniscectomy — a procedure to trim or remove the damaged portion of the crescent-shaped cartilage cushioning your knee joint. 

In the US alone, more than **700,000 meniscectomies** are performed every year, making it one of the most common surgical procedures in the world. Now, a landmark 10-year clinical trial has delivered a stunning verdict: not only does the surgery fail to outperform non-operative treatments, it may actually accelerate joint deterioration in many patients.

## What the Study Found

The trial, one of the longest and most rigorous of its kind, followed patients who underwent arthroscopic partial meniscectomy (APM) and compared their outcomes over a full decade with patients who received conservative management — physical therapy, exercise, and pain management.

The results were striking. Patients who had surgery showed no statistically significant advantage in pain relief or functional improvement over those who didn't. More troublingly, the surgical group showed signs of accelerated cartilage loss in the affected knee, a precursor to osteoarthritis.

## Who Is Most Affected?

The findings are particularly relevant for **middle-aged and older adults** with degenerative meniscus tears — the kind that develop gradually through wear and tear, not sudden trauma. This population makes up the majority of meniscectomy patients.

For younger patients with acute, traumatic meniscus tears — like athletes who sustain a sudden injury during sport — the picture may be different, and surgery may still be appropriate. Experts caution against over-applying the study's conclusions to all meniscus cases.

## The Problem With Cutting Cartilage

The meniscus performs critical jobs: it distributes load across the knee, absorbs shock, and stabilizes the joint. When even a portion is removed, the knee loses some of that protective architecture. Over years, the increased mechanical stress on the cartilage surfaces below can promote the very breakdown surgeons were trying to prevent.

This is why the trial's long 10-year follow-up is so important — short-term studies sometimes showed modest surgical benefits that simply evaporated over time, while harms compounded.

## What Works Instead? Evidence-Based Alternatives

1. **Physical Therapy:** Targeted strengthening of quadriceps and hamstrings reduces knee load and is shown to match surgical outcomes in most degenerative cases.
2. **Exercise Therapy:** Supervised exercise programs — including cycling, swimming, and resistance training — reduce pain and restore function without surgical risk.
3. **Weight Management:** Reducing excess body weight directly reduces mechanical load on the knee joint, slowing degeneration significantly.
4. **Anti-inflammatory Medication:** NSAIDs and corticosteroid injections can manage flare-ups while conservative rehab proceeds.
5. **Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) Therapy:** An emerging option with growing evidence for slowing cartilage degeneration, though more research is ongoing.

## What Should Patients Do Now?

If you've already had a meniscectomy, don't panic. The findings don't mean the surgery caused irreversible harm — many patients do experience relief. However, it does underscore the importance of long-term joint monitoring and proactive conservative care post-surgery.

If you've been *recommended* for a meniscectomy, the guidance is clear: have an in-depth conversation with your physician about whether you fall into the degenerative category, ask specifically about non-surgical options, and consider seeking a second opinion from a sports medicine physician.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is all meniscus surgery bad?**  
No. The findings apply most strongly to degenerative meniscus tears in middle-aged and older adults. Acute traumatic tears, particularly in younger, active patients, may still benefit from surgical repair. 

**How long does conservative treatment take?**  
Most structured physical therapy programs run 6–12 weeks. Many patients see significant pain improvement within 4–8 weeks of consistent exercise therapy.

**Can I re-tear a meniscus without surgery?**  
A degenerative tear does not necessarily progress to a worse tear with conservative management. Exercise therapy actually strengthens the supportive structures around the knee, potentially reducing injury risk.

**What if physical therapy doesn't work?**  
Surgery remains an option if conservative care genuinely fails after an adequate trial. The study doesn't eliminate surgery — it repositions it as a later resort for many patients rather than a first response.
