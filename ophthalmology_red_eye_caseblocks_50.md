## Case 1: The Contact Lens Wearer with a White Infiltrate

A 24-year-old female medical student presents to the ophthalmology clinic with severe pain, redness, photophobia, and purulent discharge in her right eye for the past 24 hours. She admits to sleeping in her extended-wear soft contact lenses for the past week due to exam preparation.

**Physical Examination:**
*   **Vitals:** Temp: 98.6°F (37.0°C) | HR: 72 bpm | BP: 120/80 mmHg
*   **Visual Acuity:** OD: 20/200 (pinholes to 20/100); OS: 20/20
*   **Ocular Examination (OD):** Significant lid edema and conjunctival injection with ciliary flush. Slit-lamp examination reveals a 2.5 mm round, dense, white-gray stromal infiltrate/ulcer with epithelial loss in the central cornea. A 1.0 mm sterile hypopyon is visible in the anterior chamber. OS exam is unremarkable.

---

### Question 1: Diagnosis & Initial Management
Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in the management of this patient's condition?

A. Immediate corneal scraping for culture followed by hourly fortified topical tobramycin and cefazolin.
B. Application of a tight pressure patch and scheduling a follow-up in 48 hours.
C. Initiation of topical prednisolone acetate 1% drops every 2 hours.
D. Application of topical acyclovir 3% ointment 5 times daily.
E. Prescribing oral ciprofloxacin 750 mg twice daily.

**Q1 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** This patient presents with a classic bacterial corneal ulcer (keratitis), highly suspicious for *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* given her history of contact lens overwear. A central corneal ulcer with a hypopyon is an ocular emergency. The standard of care is immediate corneal scraping for Gram stain and culture/sensitivities, followed immediately by empiric, hourly broad-spectrum topical antibiotics (either fortified tobramycin/cefazolin or monotherapy with a fourth-generation fluoroquinolone like moxifloxacin). 
*   **B (Pressure patch)** is absolutely contraindicated in contact lens-related keratitis because it traps pathogens (especially *Pseudomonas*) against the cornea, leading to rapid corneal melting and perforation.
*   **C (Topical steroids)** are contraindicated in the acute phase of active bacterial keratitis as they inhibit host immunity and delay epithelialization; they are only considered later to reduce scarring once the infection is sterilized.
*   **D (Topical acyclovir)** is used for herpes simplex virus (HSV) epithelial keratitis, which presents with dendritic ulcers, not a dense stromal ulcer with a hypopyon.
*   **E (Oral ciprofloxacin)** does not reach therapeutic concentrations in the avascular cornea; topical administration is required.

---

### Question 2: Pathophysiology (Follow-up)
Which characteristic of the most likely pathogen responsible for this patient's condition allows it to cause rapid corneal destruction?

A. Production of collagenase and proteases that cause stromal necrosis.
B. Ability to replicate intracellularly within corneal epithelial cells.
C. Formation of multinucleated giant cells that disrupt Descemet's membrane.
D. Secretion of IgA proteases that cleave mucosal antibodies.
E. Release of dermatonecrotic exotoxins that damage the nasociliary nerve.

**Q2 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* is the most common pathogen in contact lens-associated keratitis. It produces proteolytic enzymes, including collagenase and elastase, which can liquefy the corneal stroma (corneal melting) within 24 to 48 hours, leading to corneal perforation.
*   **B (Intracellular replication)** is characteristic of viral pathogens like HSV or bacteria like *Chlamydia*, not *Pseudomonas*.
*   **C (Multinucleated giant cells)** are associated with viral infections (e.g., HSV/VZV) on Tzanck smear, not bacterial keratitis.
*   **D (IgA proteases)** are virulence factors for *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and *Neisseria* species, but do not drive the rapid stromal necrosis characteristic of *Pseudomonas*.
*   **E (Nasociliary nerve damage)** is a feature of Herpes Zoster Ophthalmicus (VZV), not acute bacterial keratitis.

## Case 2: The Chronic Red Eye with Dendritic Branching

A 34-year-old male presents with a 3-day history of irritation, redness, photophobia, and a "gritty" foreign body sensation in his left eye. He reports having similar, milder episodes in the same eye over the last few years, usually resolving spontaneously. He denies contact lens wear.

**Physical Examination:**
*   **Vitals:** Temp: 98.9°F (37.2°C) | HR: 68 bpm | BP: 118/76 mmHg
*   **Visual Acuity:** OS: 20/40 (uncorrected); OD: 20/20
*   **Ocular Examination (OS):** Conjunctival injection concentrated around the limbus (ciliary flush). Corneal sensation is significantly decreased in the left eye compared to the right. Fluorescein staining reveals a branching, dendritic epithelial lesion with terminal bulbs in the central cornea. No stromal edema or anterior chamber cell/flare is noted.

---

### Question 1: Diagnosis & Initial Management
Which of the following is the most appropriate treatment for this patient's current condition?

A. Topical ganciclovir 0.15% gel 5 times daily.
B. Topical prednisolone acetate 1% drops every 2 hours.
C. Topical ketorolac 0.5% drops 4 times daily.
D. Oral acyclovir 800 mg 5 times daily for 14 days.
E. Topical tobramycin 0.3% drops 4 times daily.

**Q1 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** The clinical presentation of a branching dendritic ulcer with terminal bulbs and decreased corneal sensation is diagnostic of Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) epithelial keratitis. Initial treatment requires topical antivirals (e.g., ganciclovir 0.15% gel or trifluridine 1% drops) or oral acyclovir (400 mg 5 times daily). Topical ganciclovir is highly effective and has less corneal toxicity than trifluridine.
*   **B (Topical steroids)** are strictly contraindicated in active HSV epithelial keratitis. Steroids promote viral replication, which can turn a simple dendritic ulcer into a massive, geographic ulcer, risking corneal perforation.
*   **C (Topical ketorolac)** is a topical NSAID; it does not treat the underlying viral infection and can cause corneal melting with prolonged use.
*   **D (Oral acyclovir 800 mg 5 times daily)** is the dosage for Herpes Zoster Ophthalmicus (HZO); for HSV epithelial keratitis, the standard oral dose is 400 mg 5 times daily (or valacyclovir 500 mg 3 times daily) if oral therapy is preferred over topical.
*   **E (Topical tobramycin)** is an antibacterial agent and has no activity against HSV.

---

### Question 2: Pathophysiology (Follow-up)
The recurrence of this patient's ocular symptoms is mediated by viral latency in which of the following structures?

A. Trigeminal ganglion.
B. Ciliary ganglion.
C. Superior cervical ganglion.
D. Edinger-Westphal nucleus.
E. Geniculate ganglion.

**Q2 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** HSV-1 remains latent in the sensory neurons of the trigeminal (CN V) ganglion. Upon reactivation (triggered by stress, UV light, fever, or immunosuppression), the virus travels anterogradely along the ophthalmic division (V1) of the trigeminal nerve to the cornea, causing recurrent epithelial keratitis.
*   **B (Ciliary ganglion)** contains parasympathetic postganglionic cell bodies supplying the sphincter pupillae and ciliary muscle; it is not a site of HSV latency.
*   **C (Superior cervical ganglion)** contains sympathetic postganglionic cell bodies; it is not involved in HSV latency.
*   **D (Edinger-Westphal nucleus)** is the parasympathetic preganglionic nucleus of CN III in the midbrain, not a peripheral sensory ganglion.
*   **E (Geniculate ganglion)** is the sensory ganglion of the facial nerve (CN VII), associated with HSV/VZV reactivation in Ramsay Hunt syndrome (herpes zoster oticus), not corneal keratitis.

## Case 3: The Photophobic Patient with Disc-Shaped Corneal Edema

A 45-year-old female presents with pain, blurred vision, and extreme photophobia in her left eye for 5 days. She has a history of cold sores and remembers having a "scratched cornea" in the same eye 2 years ago that was treated with drops.

**Physical Examination:**
*   **Visual Acuity:** OS: 20/80; OD: 20/20
*   **Ocular Examination (OS):** Mild conjunctival injection with ciliary flush. Slit-lamp exam reveals a round, disc-shaped zone of central corneal stromal edema with overlying intact epithelium (no fluorescein uptake). Fine, keratic precipitates (KPs) are present on the corneal endothelium directly behind the area of edema. The anterior chamber shows 1+ cell and flare. Corneal sensation is reduced.

---

### Question 1: Diagnosis & Initial Management
Which of the following is the most appropriate management strategy for this patient?

A. Combined topical prednisolone acetate 1% and topical ganciclovir 0.15% gel.
B. Topical moxifloxacin 0.5% drops hourly.
C. Topical ganciclovir 0.15% gel alone.
D. Debridement of the corneal epithelium followed by a pressure patch.
E. Systemic intravenous acyclovir administration.

**Q1 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** This patient has HSV stromal keratitis (specifically disciform keratitis), which is an immune-mediated (hypersensitivity) reaction to viral antigen in the corneal stroma, rather than active viral replication. Unlike HSV epithelial keratitis, the treatment of stromal keratitis requires topical corticosteroids (prednisolone acetate 1%) to suppress the inflammatory response and prevent permanent scarring. However, to prevent a reactivation of epithelial disease by the steroid, concurrent antiviral cover (topical ganciclovir or oral acyclovir) is mandatory.
*   **B (Topical moxifloxacin)** is for bacterial keratitis, which presents with stromal infiltrates and epithelial defects, not disciform edema with an intact epithelium.
*   **C (Topical ganciclovir alone)** is insufficient because the stromal edema and inflammation are immune-mediated and will not resolve without corticosteroids; untreated stromal keratitis leads to vascularization and scarring.
*   **D (Epithelial debridement)** is sometimes used for active HSV epithelial dendritic ulcers to reduce viral load, but is contraindicated here where the epithelium is intact and the disease is deep in the stroma.
*   **E (IV acyclovir)** is reserved for severe systemic HSV infections (e.g., encephalitis or neonatal HSV), not isolated stromal keratitis.

---

### Question 2: Complications & Monitoring (Follow-up)
What is the most common long-term vision-threatening complication of chronic recurrences of this patient's disease?

A. Corneal scarring and neovascularization.
B. Rhegmatogenous retinal detachment.
C. Tractional retinal detachment.
D. Closed-angle glaucoma.
E. Sympathetic ophthalmia.

**Q2 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** Recurrent episodes of HSV stromal keratitis lead to progressive corneal scarring, lipid deposition, and corneal neovascularization. This destroys the transparency of the cornea, leading to permanent visual loss that may eventually require a penetrating keratoplasty (corneal transplant).
*   **B & C (Retinal detachments)** are complications of posterior segment disease (e.g., CMV retinitis, acute retinal necrosis), not anterior corneal disease.
*   **D (Closed-angle glaucoma)** can occur if posterior synechiae cause pupillary block, but it is not the most common complication of recurrent HSV stromal keratitis.
*   **E (Sympathetic ophthalmia)** is a bilateral granulomatous panuveitis following penetrating ocular trauma or intraocular surgery, not recurrent viral keratitis.

## Case 4: Forehead Rash and a Painful Red Eye

A 68-year-old male presents with severe pain, redness, and tearing in his right eye for 2 days. For the past week, he has experienced a tingling sensation and a painful vesicular rash on his right forehead and upper eyelid.

**Physical Examination:**
*   **Vitals:** Temp: 99.1°F (37.3°C) | HR: 80 bpm | BP: 134/86 mmHg
*   **Physical:** A vesicular dermatomal rash in various stages of crusting is noted on the right side of the forehead, extending down to the tip of the nose.
*   **Visual Acuity:** OD: 20/50; OS: 20/20
*   **Ocular Examination (OD):** Moderate conjunctival injection. Slit-lamp exam reveals multiple elevated, mucous-like pseudodendrites on the corneal epithelium that stain poorly with fluorescein and lack terminal bulbs. There is 2+ cell and flare in the anterior chamber.

---

### Question 1: Diagnosis & Initial Management
Which of the following is the most appropriate initial systemic therapy for this patient?

A. Oral valacyclovir 1000 mg three times daily for 7 days.
B. Oral acyclovir 400 mg twice daily for 1 year.
C. Oral prednisone 60 mg daily tapered over 2 weeks.
D. Topical trifluridine 1% drops 9 times daily.
E. Intravenous acyclovir 10 mg/kg every 8 hours.

**Q1 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** This patient has Herpes Zoster Ophthalmicus (HZO), which is the reactivation of Varicella Zoster Virus (VZV) in the ophthalmic branch (V1) of the trigeminal nerve. The presence of the rash at the tip of the nose (Hutchinson’s sign) indicates involvement of the nasociliary nerve, which directly innervates the ocular structures and correlates with a high risk of ocular disease (keratitis, uveitis). Treatment consists of oral antivirals initiated within 72 hours of rash onset to reduce the severity of HZO and post-herpetic neuralgia. The standard dosage for VZV is oral valacyclovir 1000 mg three times daily (or oral acyclovir 800 mg 5 times daily) for 7 days.
*   **B (Oral acyclovir 400 mg twice daily)** is a suppressive dose used to prevent recurrences of HSV, not treatment for acute HZO.
*   **C (Oral prednisone alone)** does not treat the active viral infection and can promote viral replication; systemic steroids are sometimes used as an adjunct for severe post-herpetic neuralgia in the elderly, but never as monotherapy.
*   **D (Topical trifluridine)** is used for HSV epithelial keratitis, but VZV corneal lesions are pseudodendrites (which are plaques of cells rather than true ulcers) and do not respond well to topical antivirals; treatment is systemic.
*   **E (IV acyclovir)** is reserved for immunocompromised patients or those with disseminated shingles.

---

### Question 2: Pathophysiology (Follow-up)
The vesicular rash on the tip of this patient's nose indicates involvement of which specific nerve?

A. Nasociliary nerve.
B. Frontal nerve.
C. Lacrimal nerve.
D. Infraorbital nerve.
E. Facial nerve.

**Q2 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** The tip of the nose is innervated by the external nasal branch of the nasociliary nerve, which is a branch of the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve (V1). Because the nasociliary nerve also provides sensory innervation to the cornea, ciliary body, and iris, the presence of vesicles on the tip of the nose (Hutchinson's sign) strongly predicts intraocular inflammation (such as keratitis and iridocyclitis).
*   **B (Frontal nerve)** supplies the forehead and upper eyelid; its involvement causes rash there but does not predict ocular involvement as strongly as the nasociliary nerve.
*   **C (Lacrimal nerve)** innervates the lacrimal gland and lateral conjunctiva.
*   **D (Infraorbital nerve)** is a branch of V2 (maxillary division); it supplies the lower eyelid and cheek, not the forehead or nose tip.
*   **E (Facial nerve)** is CN VII, which provides motor innervation to the muscles of facial expression, not sensory innervation to the skin of the forehead or nose.

## Case 5: The Contact Lens Wearer with Excruciating Pain

A 22-year-old male college student presents with severe, boring pain, redness, and photophobia in his left eye for 2 weeks. He states the pain is so intense it keeps him awake at night and is completely out of proportion to what he sees in the mirror. He uses monthly disposable contact lenses and admits to rinsing them in tap water when he ran out of sterile solution. He has been using topical moxifloxacin drops prescribed at an urgent care clinic for 10 days without relief.

**Physical Examination:**
*   **Visual Acuity:** OS: 20/100; OD: 20/20
*   **Ocular Examination (OS):** Conjunctival and episcleral injection. Slit-lamp exam reveals a ring-shaped corneal stromal infiltrate in the mid-periphery. There is moderate corneal perineural infiltration (radial keratoneuritis). Overlying epithelial staining is diffuse but patchy.

---

### Question 1: Diagnosis & Initial Management
Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in the diagnosis and management of this patient's condition?

A. Perform corneal scraping for culture on non-nutrient agar with an *Escherichia coli* overlay, and initiate topical chlorhexidine 0.02% and propamidine isethionate 0.1%.
B. Obtain a viral culture for HSV and initiate topical ganciclovir 0.15% gel.
C. Increase the frequency of topical moxifloxacin to every 15 minutes.
D. Initiate topical prednisolone acetate 1% every hour to reduce the corneal ring inflammation.
E. Apply a bandage contact lens and start topical tobramycin drops.

**Q1 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** This clinical picture is classic for *Acanthamoeba* keratitis: a contact lens wearer with poor hygiene (tap water exposure), severe pain out of proportion to clinical signs, radial keratoneuritis (corneal perineural infiltrates), a ring-shaped infiltrate (often a later sign), and lack of response to standard antibacterial therapy. Diagnosis is confirmed by corneal scraping and culture on non-nutrient agar seeded with *E. coli* (which the amoebae feed on) or by confocal microscopy. First-line medical treatment involves topical cysticidal agents, typically a combination of a biguanide (chlorhexidine 0.02% or polyhexamethylene biguanide [PHMB] 0.02%) and a diamidine (propamidine isethionate 0.1% [Brolene]).
*   **B (HSV)** can cause dendritic keratitis and decreased sensation, but does not present with this level of pain, radial keratoneuritis, or a history of tap-water contact lens rinsing.
*   **C (Fluoroquinolones)** are antibacterial and are ineffective against *Acanthamoeba*.
*   **D (Topical steroids)** are controversial and generally avoided in the early phase of *Acanthamoeba* keratitis because they inhibit the host immune response and cause trophozoites to encyst, making the infection harder to eradicate.
*   **E (Bandage lens + tobramycin)** will worsen the infection by trapping the amoebae and providing no effective treatment.

---

### Question 2: Pathophysiology (Follow-up)
Which of the following forms of the causative organism is highly resistant to standard medical therapy and responsible for chronic recurrence?

A. Cyst.
B. Trophozoite.
C. Endospore.
D. Tachyzoite.
E. Bradyzoite.

**Q2 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** *Acanthamoeba* exists in two life stages: the active, replicating trophozoite and the dormant, double-walled cyst. The cyst form is highly resistant to desiccation, temperature changes, and most antimicrobials. When exposed to drugs, the organism encysts and can remain dormant in the corneal stroma for months, leading to treatment failure and late recurrences when it excysts back into trophozoites.
*   **B (Trophozoite)** is the active, pathogenic form that is susceptible to anti-amoebic agents.
*   **C (Endospore)** is a bacterial survival structure (e.g., in *Bacillus* or *Clostridium*), not protozoan.
*   **D & E (Tachyzoite & Bradyzoite)** are life stages of *Toxoplasma gondii*, which causes retinochoroiditis, not corneal keratitis.

## Case 6: Corneal Injury in a Landscaper

A 42-year-old male landscaper presents to the Emergency Department with a painful, red left eye for 4 days. He recalls being struck in the left eye by a small tree branch while cutting brush last week. He has been using over-the-counter lubricating eye drops without improvement.

**Physical Examination:**
*   **Visual Acuity:** OS: 20/70; OD: 20/20
*   **Ocular Examination (OS):** Conjunctival injection with ciliary flush. Slit-lamp exam of the cornea reveals a central infiltrate with feathery, indistinct borders and several small, independent "satellite" lesions in the surrounding stroma. An endothelial plaque is visible behind the main lesion. No hypopyon is present.

---

### Question 1: Diagnosis & Initial Management
Which of the following is the most appropriate management for this patient?

A. Perform corneal scraping for Giemsa and Gomori methenamine silver (GMS) stains, and initiate topical natamycin 5% suspension.
B. Initiate topical moxifloxacin 0.5% drops hourly.
C. Initiate topical ganciclovir 0.15% gel 5 times daily.
D. Apply topical dexamethasone 0.1% drops 4 times daily.
E. Prescribe oral acyclovir 400 mg 5 times daily.

**Q1 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** The clinical history of trauma involving vegetable matter (a tree branch) and slit-lamp findings of a corneal infiltrate with feathery borders and satellite lesions are highly characteristic of fungal keratitis (usually filamentous fungi like *Aspergillus* or *Fusarium*). Diagnosis is confirmed with corneal scraping for microscopy (Giemsa, KOH prep, Calcofluor white, or GMS stain) and culture on Sabouraud dextrose agar. Topical natamycin 5% suspension is the only FDA-approved topical formulation for filamentous fungal keratitis; amphotericin B 0.15% is preferred for yeast infections (like *Candida*).
*   **B (Moxifloxacin)** is an antibacterial; it will not treat a fungal infection and can lead to worsening of the disease.
*   **C & E (Ganciclovir & Acyclovir)** are antivirals and have no efficacy against fungi.
*   **D (Topical dexamethasone)** is contraindicated in active fungal keratitis as steroids promote fungal growth and increase the risk of corneal perforation.

---

### Question 2: Pathophysiology (Follow-up)
Why are filamentous fungal corneal infections particularly dangerous to the intraocular structures?

A. They can penetrate an intact Descemet’s membrane into the anterior chamber.
B. They release neurotoxins that paralyze the pupillary sphincter.
C. They lead to systemic hematogenous dissemination to the brain.
D. They cause occlusion of the central retinal artery.
E. They selectively destroy the lacrimal gland, causing permanent dry eye.

**Q2 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** Filamentous fungi (*Fusarium* and *Aspergillus*) have the ability to penetrate deep into the corneal stroma and pass through an intact Descemet’s membrane into the anterior chamber. This can lead to endophthalmitis, which is extremely difficult to treat and often requires surgical intervention (therapeutic penetrating keratoplasty or anterior chamber washout with intravitreal antifungals).
*   **B (Neurotoxins)** are not produced by these fungi.
*   **C (Hematogenous dissemination)** is not a typical feature of localized fungal keratitis in immunocompetent hosts.
*   **D (Central retinal artery occlusion)** is a posterior segment vascular event and not a complication of corneal fungal infections.
*   **E (Lacrimal gland destruction)** is not caused by fungal keratitis.

## Case 7: The Newborn with Swollen, Purulent Eyelids

A 3-day-old male infant is brought to the pediatric emergency room by his mother due to severe swelling and discharge from both eyes. The infant was born via vaginal delivery at home with no prenatal care. The mother reports that her own pregnancy was uneventful, but she has not had any regular health checkups.

**Physical Examination:**
*   **Vitals:** Temp: 98.8°F (37.1°C) | HR: 130 bpm | RR: 34/min
*   **Physical:** Bilateral, marked eyelid edema with thick, copious, purulent discharge streaming from both palpebral fissures.
*   **Ocular Examination:** Diffuse, severe conjunctival chemosis and injection. The corneas are clear, but the child is difficult to examine due to severe blepharospasm.

---

### Question 1: Diagnosis & Initial Management
Which of the following is the most urgent intervention for this infant?

A. Admission for intravenous ceftriaxone and frequent saline irrigation of both eyes.
B. Administration of oral erythromycin suspension for 14 days.
C. Prescription of topical erythromycin 0.5% ointment to be applied 4 times daily.
D. Outpatient follow-up with topical tobramycin drops.
E. Performance of a nasolacrimal duct probing.

**Q1 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** This is a case of hyperacute ophthalmia neonatorum (neonatal conjunctivitis) occurring within the first 2-5 days of life, which is highly characteristic of *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*. Gonococcal conjunctivitis is a medical emergency because the bacterium can rapidly penetrate intact corneal epithelium, leading to corneal ulceration, perforation, and permanent blindness. The patient requires admission for systemic therapy (IV ceftriaxone 25-50 mg/kg, up to 125 mg) and frequent, hourly saline irrigation of the eyes to wash away the toxic purulent debris.
*   **B (Oral erythromycin)** is the treatment of choice for *Chlamydia trachomatis* conjunctivitis (which typically presents at 5-14 days of life), not *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*.
*   **C (Topical erythromycin ointment)** is used as prophylaxis at birth, but it is completely inadequate as monotherapy for active, established gonococcal conjunctivitis, which requires systemic antibiotics.
*   **D (Topical tobramycin)** does not provide adequate coverage for *Neisseria* and does not address the systemic nature of the infection.
*   **E (Nasolacrimal duct probing)** is a treatment for congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction (dacryostenosis), which presents with tearing and mild matting, not hyperacute purulent conjunctivitis.

---

### Question 2: Pathophysiology (Follow-up)
What is the mechanism by which the standard prophylaxis given at birth prevents this condition?

A. Topical erythromycin ointment has bactericidal activity against *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* on the ocular surface.
B. Intramuscular phytonadione inhibits the replication of Gram-negative diplococci.
C. Silver nitrate drops promote the production of secretory IgA in the neonate's tears.
D. Oral azithromycin clears maternal vaginal colonization prior to delivery.
E. Topical povidone-iodine induces corneal epithelial shedding to clear viral particles.

**Q2 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** Standard neonatal prophylaxis involves the application of topical erythromycin 0.5% ointment (or tetracycline/silver nitrate historically) within 1 hour of birth. Erythromycin is active against *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* and prevents the acquisition of gonococcal ophthalmia neonatorum during passage through the birth canal. Note that erythromycin prophylaxis does *not* reliably prevent neonatal chlamydial conjunctivitis.
*   **B (IM Vitamin K / phytonadione)** is given to prevent hemorrhagic disease of the newborn, not infections.
*   **C (Silver nitrate)** acts by direct chemical toxicity to bacteria, not by stimulating neonatal IgA production.
*   **D (Oral azithromycin)** is not a neonatal ocular prophylaxis.
*   **E (Povidone-iodine)** is an antiseptic occasionally used, but it does not act by inducing epithelial shedding.

## Case 8: The Infant with Mild Redness and Congestion

A 10-day-old female infant is brought to the clinic with redness, swelling, and a watery, mucopurulent discharge from her left eye for 48 hours. The mother notes the baby has also had mild nasal congestion. The delivery was vaginal at a local hospital, and erythromycin ointment was applied to the eyes immediately after birth.

**Physical Examination:**
*   **Ocular Examination (OS):** Moderate eyelid swelling and palpebral conjunctival injection. The discharge is mucopurulent. Slit-lamp/loupe examination shows conjunctival follicular response is absent (which is normal in neonates, as they lack lymphoid tissue in the conjunctiva for the first month). The cornea is clear. OD is normal.

---

### Question 1: Diagnosis & Initial Management
Which of the following is the most appropriate treatment for this infant's condition?

A. Oral erythromycin base 50 mg/kg/day divided into 4 doses for 14 days.
B. Topical erythromycin 0.5% ointment applied 4 times daily for 10 days.
C. Topical ganciclovir 0.15% gel 5 times daily.
D. Intravenous acyclovir therapy for 14 days.
E. Topical moxifloxacin 0.5% drops 4 times daily.

**Q1 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** The onset of neonatal conjunctivitis at 10 days of life (range: 5-14 days) with mucopurulent discharge is highly suspicious for *Chlamydia trachomatis* (inclusion blennorrhea). Topical prophylaxis with erythromycin ointment at birth prevents gonococcal conjunctivitis but does not prevent chlamydial conjunctivitis. Active chlamydial conjunctivitis in neonates must be treated with systemic oral erythromycin (or azithromycin) to eradicate the nasopharyngeal colonization and prevent the development of chlamydial pneumonitis.
*   **B (Topical erythromycin alone)** has a high failure rate because it does not treat the systemic reservoir (nasopharynx), leaving the infant at risk for chlamydial pneumonia.
*   **C & D (Antivirals)** are used for neonatal HSV (which typically presents with vesicular skin lesions, microphthalmia, or keratitis around 1-2 weeks of life), not chlamydial conjunctivitis.
*   **E (Topical moxifloxacin)** does not address the risk of systemic chlamydial pneumonitis.

---

### Question 2: Complications & Monitoring (Follow-up)
What potential systemic complication of the treatment must the parents be educated about?

A. Infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis.
B. Grey baby syndrome.
C. Acute kidney injury.
D. Permanent tooth discoloration.
E. Tendon rupture.

**Q2 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** Oral erythromycin use in infants under 6 weeks of age is associated with an increased risk of infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis (IHPS). Parents must be instructed to monitor the baby for projectile vomiting and poor feeding. Despite this risk, oral erythromycin (or azithromycin) remains the standard of care for neonatal chlamydial conjunctivitis because the risk of untreated chlamydial pneumonitis is high.
*   **B (Grey baby syndrome)** is a complication of chloramphenicol toxicity in neonates due to immature glucuronidation.
*   **C (AKI)** is associated with aminoglycosides (e.g., gentamicin).
*   **D (Tooth discoloration)** is a complication of tetracyclines in children under 8 years of age.
*   **E (Tendon rupture)** is a risk associated with fluoroquinolones (e.g., ciprofloxacin), not macrolides.

## Case 9: The College Student with a Chronic Follicular Red Eye

A 19-year-old female college student presents with a 3-week history of redness, foreign body sensation, and watery discharge in her right eye. She has been treated with two courses of topical antibacterial drops (neomycin-polymyxin-gramicidin and tobramycin) by her university student health center without any improvement. She reports being sexually active with a new partner.

**Physical Examination:**
*   **Visual Acuity:** 20/20 in both eyes.
*   **Ocular Examination (OD):** Moderate bulbar conjunctival injection. Slit-lamp exam of the palpebral conjunctiva (especially the inferior tarsus) reveals large, fleshy, lymphoid follicles. A small, non-tender right preauricular lymph node is palpable. The cornea shows mild superior epithelial keratitis but no ulceration. OS exam is completely normal.

---

### Question 1: Diagnosis & Initial Management
Which of the following is the most appropriate management for this patient?

---

### Question 1: Diagnosis & Pathophysiology
Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Neovascular Glaucoma (NVG).
B. Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma (AACG).
C. Fuchs Uveitis Syndrome.
D. Bacterial Endophthalmitis.
E. Phacolytic Glaucoma.

**Q1 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** This is a case of Neovascular Glaucoma (NVG) in a patient with proliferative diabetic retinopathy. Initially, this causes an open-angle glaucoma as the neovascular membrane covers the trabecular meshwork (as seen in this patient with an open angle). *   **E (Phacolytic)** is caused by a leaking hypermature cataract, not retinal ischemia.

D. Emergency trabeculectomy.
E. Surgical removal of the lens (cataract extraction).

**Q1 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** The definitive treatment for neovascular glaucoma is to address the underlying retinal ischemia that is producing VEGF. This is achieved through Panretinal Photocoagulation (PRP) to ablate the ischemic peripheral retina, combined with intravitreal anti-VEGF injections (e.g., bevacizumab or ranibizumab) to rapidly shrink the new vessels on the iris and angle. Medical IOP-lowering drops are used as adjunctive therapy.
*   **B (Laser peripheral iridotomy)** is the treatment for pupillary-block angle-closure glaucoma, not neovascular glaucoma.
*   **C (Steroids alone)** will not address the ischemia or reduce the IOP.
*   **D (Trabeculectomy)** may be needed later if medical and laser therapy fail, but performing filtration surgery on a highly vascularized, active rubeotic eye carries a very high risk of intraocular hemorrhage (hyphema) and failure.
*   **E (Cataract extraction)** is not the treatment for NVG.

### Question 1: Diagnosis & Tear Film Dynamics
Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis, and which layer of the tear film is primarily affected?

A. Posterior Blepharitis (Meibomian Gland Dysfunction); Lipid layer.
B. Anterior Blepharitis (Staphylococcal); Aqueous layer.
C. Aqueous Tear Deficiency (Sjogren's); Mucin layer.
D. Superior Limbic Keratoconjunctivitis; Aqueous layer.
E. Contact Dermatitis; Lipid layer.

**Q1 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** This is a classic case of posterior blepharitis, also known as Meibomian Gland Dysfunction (MGD). *   **D (SLK)** is an inflammatory friction-related disease of the superior limbus.
*   **E (Contact dermatitis)** is an acute allergic reaction of the eyelid skin.

Which of the following represents the most appropriate initial therapy for this patient?

A. Warm compresses and eyelid hygiene (lid scrubs), combined with oral omega-3 fatty acids, and topical erythromycin ointment to the lid margins.
B. High-frequency topical prednisolone acetate 1% drops daily.
C. Hourly preservative-free artificial tears only.
D. Urgent surgical excision of the meibomian glands.
E. Oral acyclovir 400 mg twice daily.

**Q1 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** First-line management of posterior blepharitis is conservative: warm compresses (to liquefy the clogged lipid) followed by eyelid massage/hygiene, oral omega-3 supplements to improve lipid quality, and topical antibiotic ointment (erythromycin or bacitracin) applied to the lid margin at bedtime to reduce the lipase-producing bacterial population. For refractory cases, oral doxycycline is used for its anti-inflammatory and lipid-modifying properties.
*   **B (Topical steroids)** are avoided for long-term management of blepharitis due to side effects.
*   **C (Tears alone)** do not address the underlying meibomian gland obstruction.
*   **D (Surgical excision)** is incorrect.
*   **E (Oral acyclovir)** is not indicated.

## Case 47: The Patient with a Red Eye and an Irregular Pupil

A 35-year-old female presents with pain, redness, and photophobia in her right eye for 4 days. She has a history of Ulcerative Colitis and is currently experiencing a flare of abdominal pain and bloody diarrhea.

**Physical Examination:**
*   **Visual Acuity:** OD: 20/40; OS: 20/20
*   **Ocular Examination (OD):** Circumcorneal injection (ciliary flush). The pupil is irregular (clover-leaf shaped) and miotic. Slit-lamp exam shows 2+ cells in the anterior chamber. There are multiple adhesions between the posterior iris and the anterior lens capsule (posterior synechiae). OS is normal.

---

### Question 1: Diagnosis & Pathophysiology
Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis, and what is the mechanism of the pupil irregularity?

A. Acute anterior uveitis; Posterior synechiae binding the iris to the lens.
B. Acute angle-closure glaucoma; Iris ischemia.
C. Fuchs Uveitis Syndrome; Iris stroma atrophy.
D. Adie's tonic pupil; Ciliary ganglion dysfunction.
E. Horner's Syndrome; Sympathetic pathway interruption.

**Q1 Answer: A**
**Reasoning:** This patient has acute anterior uveitis associated with her Inflammatory Bowel Disease (Ulcerative Colitis). The irregular, clover-leaf shape of the pupil is caused by posterior synechiae, which are inflammatory adhesions between the posterior surface of the iris and the anterior capsule of the lens. Which of the following drops is most appropriate to break the posterior synechiae and restore a round pupil in this patient?

D. Topical ketorolac 0.5% drops.

*   **C (Timolol)** and **E (Latanoprost)** are IOP-lowering drops and have no effect on pupillary dilation or breaking synechiae.

## Case 49: The Welder with a Deep Eye Injury

Slit-lamp exam reveals a tiny, self-sealing wound in the cornea at the 2 o'clock position. The anterior chamber is shallow compared to the right eye. ---

Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in the management of this patient?

A. Place a rigid eye shield over the left eye, schedule an urgent CT scan of the orbit (no MRI), and consult ophthalmology immediately.
B. Perform an immediate MRI of the brain and orbit to locate the metal fragment.

## Case 50: Painless Bilateral Blindness in an Elderly Patient

Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis, and what is the appropriate treatment?

A. Age-related cataracts; Elective cataract extraction with intraocular lens (IOL) implantation.

### Question 2: Pathophysiology (Follow-up)
The development of age-related cataracts is primarily driven by which of the following molecular mechanisms within the lens?

A. Oxidative damage leading to the aggregation of insoluble crystallin proteins.
